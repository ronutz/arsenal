## Who is frightened, and of what

Not of the outage. Of the consequence to themselves.

- **The person who made the change**, who has worked out before anybody else that the timing implicates them.
- **The junior who noticed something odd an hour earlier** and did not raise it, and now has two problems.
- **The vendor's engineer**, whose product may be at fault and who has a commercial relationship to protect.
- **The manager**, who has to report upward and does not yet have anything to report.

None of them is frightened of the fault. Every one of them is frightened of a conversation.

## What fear does to the information

It does not usually produce lies. It produces four quieter things, all of which cost you time:

**Delay.** The relevant fact arrives at minute fifty rather than minute five, once the person has judged the room.

**Omission around the edges.** Not the change itself, but *"I also restarted the agent while I was in there"* — the small, innocuous-seeming action, left out because mentioning it invites scrutiny.

**Hedging that erases precision.** *"I think it was around two"* from somebody who knows exactly, because exactness feels like exposure.

**The flat denial that is nearly true.** *"I didn't do anything"* — meaning *I did nothing that should have caused this*, which is a judgement rather than a report, and is usually correct and occasionally the whole answer.

> **The innocuous thing is very often the key**, and it is precisely the thing fear removes first.

## Ask about the system, not the person

The single most effective change, and it costs nothing:

| gets an answer | gets a defence |
|---|---|
| *"What was the state at 02:00?"* | *"What did you do at 02:00?"* |
| *"What did the screen show?"* | *"Why did you set it to that?"* |
| *"Walk me through what you saw."* | *"Walk me through what you did."* |
| *"What else was touched in that window, by anyone?"* | *"Did you change anything else?"* |

Same information, different grammar. **Questions with a person as the subject invite an account of intentions; questions with the system as the subject invite an account of observations** — and observations are what an investigation runs on.

This is [RCA without a scapegoat](/practice/rca-without-a-scapegoat) applied live rather than in the review, and it matters more during than after, because during is when the information is still recoverable.

## Say the sentence, and mean it

*"Nobody is in trouble. I need the timeline."*

Said early, in front of everybody, by whoever is most senior in the room. It works, and it works **only if it is true** — people are extremely good at detecting the version that is a technique. If somebody will in fact be in trouble, do not say it; say something narrower that you can keep, such as *"right now I only care about the sequence."*

The credit for that sentence is spent once. Used honestly it buys the information for years; used as a device it is disbelieved permanently, and after that the room's silences get longer.

## Frightened people over-act

The two failure modes are opposite and both are worse than asking.

**Doing things to look useful.** Restarting something, clearing something, adjusting a setting — each action taken partly to demonstrate contribution, each one adding a variable to a system somebody is trying to reason about, and each one now also hard to admit to.

**Freezing.** Not touching anything, including the thing they are best placed to check, because the risk of being the one who made it worse outweighs the credit for helping.

Both are addressed the same way: **say out loud what you want done and what you want left alone.** Ambiguity is what drives both behaviours, and an explicit instruction removes the need for anybody to guess where the safe ground is.

## Your visible state is an operational input

The room reads the face of whoever is most technical. That is not vanity; it is how people calibrate how bad this is when they cannot assess it themselves.

Which does not mean performing confidence. **Manufactured certainty is worse than visible uncertainty**, because it collapses badly and takes your credibility with it. What works is uncertainty stated calmly: *"I do not know yet what this is. I know what I am checking next, and I will know more in twenty minutes."*

That sentence is honest and steadying at the same time, and it is available even at the worst moment of an incident.

## Afterwards, close the loop yourself

The person whose change was implicated will find out how it ended. **They should hear it from you, in person, before they read it in a document** — including, and especially, when their change turned out not to be the cause.

Where it was the cause, the same applies with the framing the review will use: the mechanism that let it through, not the hand that was on the keyboard. Somebody who learns from a circulated report that they were the proximate cause of a major outage learns, accurately, what to expect next time — and behaves accordingly, for years.

**That cost is invisible and cumulative**, and it is paid in the length of every subsequent incident where somebody waits fifty minutes before mentioning the innocuous thing.
