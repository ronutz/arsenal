## Two different situations with the same volume

**Furious and wrong** is a communication problem. They have misread a status page, misunderstood a maintenance window, or been told something inaccurate by somebody else. It is uncomfortable and it is tractable: establish the facts, and the temperature falls with them.

**Furious and correct** is not a communication problem at all. The service did fail, it failed for the reason they think, they did warn you about it in March, and the workaround they were promised never arrived. **It is an accountability problem wearing a communication costume**, and treating it as the first kind is what turns a bad call into a lost account.

## Why the standard techniques make it worse

*"I understand your frustration."* *"Let me make sure I have captured your concern."* *"I hear that this has been difficult."*

These are designed to demonstrate that somebody who feels unheard has been heard. Applied to a person who is factually right, **they read as substitution — empathy offered in place of the thing actually owed** — and the effect is to confirm that the organisation intends to manage the conversation rather than fix the problem.

The tell is unmistakable from the other side: the more precisely somebody describes a real failure, the more the acknowledgement language sounds like a script being deployed at them.

## What is owed, in order

The order matters more than the words, and getting it wrong is the common failure.

**1. That it is real, and that it is ours.** Plainly and without hedging. *"You are right. Payments failed for forty minutes and it was our load balancer, not your integration."* No *"appears to have"*, no *"may have contributed"*. Anybody angry and correct can hear a hedge from across a room.

**2. What is happening now.** Present tense, concrete, with a name attached where possible.

**3. What stops it recurring** — or an honest statement that you do not yet know, with when you will. *"I do not know yet, and I will tell you on Thursday"* holds; a manufactured answer does not survive Thursday.

**4. The apology.** Last, and brief.

An apology offered first, before the other three, is the single most reliable way to make things worse. **It reads as the transaction being closed** — as though the sorry were the deliverable — and it is precisely what somebody who has been fobbed off before is braced for.

## Do not defend the organisation to somebody who is right about it

The instinct is loyalty, and it is a trap. If they say *"this is the third time this year"* and it is, agreeing costs nothing you still have — **and disagreeing spends the credibility you will need in the parts of the conversation where you are right.**

There is nearly always such a part. They are usually correct about impact and history, and often wrong about cause. **Sequence that carefully**: correcting their theory of the cause while they are still angry about the impact reads as deflection, because that is exactly what deflection looks like. Deal with the impact first; the cause becomes discussable once the impact has been conceded.

## The thing you can offer that nobody else has

Not authority, and not compensation — those belong to somebody else.

**The truth about the technical situation, delivered straight**, including the parts that are unflattering. *"The monitoring gap is why it ran forty minutes instead of five. That is ours and it is being fixed."*

It is disarming precisely because it is rare. A person braced for a managed conversation, receiving instead an accurate technical account with the awkward parts left in, generally stops fighting — not because they have been placated, but because they have found somebody who is dealing with the same reality they are.

The corollary is that **it only works while it stays true.** One over-promised restoration time and the mechanism is spent, exactly as in [communicating upward](/practice/communicating-upward-while-live).

## When you cannot fix the systemic thing

Often the correct answer is a change you have no authority to make.

Say that, precisely, without pretending otherwise and without disowning it: *"The redundancy you are describing is a funding decision, not mine. I am putting the case in writing, and here is who decides it."* Then do it, and — the part that determines whether any of this was worth saying — **tell them what happened, including if the answer was no.**

Inventing authority to end an uncomfortable call produces a second, worse call later, with the added problem that you personally are now the unreliable party rather than the organisation.

## And name the cost

Absorbing anger that is entirely justified is genuinely draining, in a way that fielding an unreasonable complaint is not — because there is nothing to push back on, and the professional part of you agrees with them.

**That is work**, and organisations that describe it as *"handling difficult customers"* have mislabelled it. The person who spends an hour being told, accurately, that the estate they support is failing somebody has done something costly, and should not also carry the implication that they mishandled it.

## The four things owed

| | |
|---|---|
| **1. It is real, and it is ours** | plainly, no hedge |
| **2. What is happening now** | present tense, with a name |
| **3. What stops it recurring** | or when you will know |
| **4. The apology** | last, and short |

And the two rules that decide whether the order survives contact: **concede impact before discussing cause**, and **never buy quiet with a promise you cannot keep** — the quiet lasts a day and the promise is remembered for a year.
