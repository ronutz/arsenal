## The week is the cost, not the call

*"You were only called twice."* True, and it measures the wrong thing.

For the seven days you held the phone you could not have two glasses of wine, could not be ninety minutes from a laptop, could not go to the coast, could not be entirely present at dinner. **The availability was continuous. The interruptions were occasional.** What you sold was the week.

Every compensation model built around per-call-out payment prices the interruption and gives the constraint away free, which is why on-call feels underpaid even where the per-incident rate is generous. **The honest structure has two components**: a retainer for holding the phone at all, and a payment for actually being used. Organisations that pay only the second are buying the first without naming it.

## Sleep does not work the way the rota assumes

The rota assumes an eight-hour night minus the twenty minutes you were awake. That is not how it works.

**Fragmentation costs more than duration.** A night broken once in the middle is worth far less than an unbroken shorter night, and the recovery is not a simple subtraction.

**Anticipatory waking is real and uncounted.** People on call wake at three in the morning to check a phone that did not ring. That is not anxiety in a clinical sense; it is a completely rational response to holding a device that might demand a decision. **It happens on the nights nobody was called**, which are the nights the organisation counts as free.

So a week of on-call with two call-outs is not five undisturbed nights plus two disturbed ones. It is seven nights of lighter sleep, two of which were also interrupted.

## Alert quality is a welfare question

The usual framing is operational: noisy alerts train people to ignore the console. True, and it understates it.

> **Every page that fires at three in the morning for something nobody would act on until nine is a tax levied on a person's sleep for no return.** It is not a monitoring defect with a human side effect. It is a human cost with a monitoring cause.

The test is one sentence, and it is worth applying to every rule that can wake somebody:

> **"Would we get out of bed for this?"**

If the answer is no — and for a large fraction of overnight alerts it is — then it is a morning notification, and moving it there costs nothing but the discomfort of admitting that somebody configured it without asking the question.

A rota where half the pages are not actionable is not a staffing problem. It is an alerting problem being paid for in other people's nights.

## Responsibility without authority is the worst combination

Being woken to fix something you are not permitted to fix is the most corrosive version of this job.

If the on-call engineer cannot restart the service, fail over, or take the site out of rotation without waking somebody senior for permission, then **the rota has distributed the accountability and kept the authority**, and the person holding the phone absorbs the stress of both the fault and the wait.

Authority at that hour should be explicit, written, and larger than it is comfortable to grant — because the alternative is a chain of woken people, each adding delay while the incident runs. Where a decision genuinely cannot be delegated, say which ones, so everything else is unambiguously delegated.

## Recovery is not a perk

Somebody who worked from 02:00 to 06:00 should not be expected at 09:00, and the reason is not kindness.

**An impaired engineer handling the next incident is an operational risk**, and the arithmetic is unforgiving: the hours you recover by having them at their desk are worth less than the one bad decision they make at eleven the following morning. That is the subject of [judgment at hour eleven](/practice/judgment-at-hour-eleven), and it applies to the day after quite as much as to the night itself.

Where recovery time is discretionary, nobody takes it — so it has to be the default, stated, and taken by senior people visibly, or it does not exist.

## What to ask before you join a rota

Reasonable questions, and the answers tell you more about an organisation than anything in the interview:

- **How often does the phone actually ring at night?** Per week, in numbers, over the last quarter.
- **What fraction of those were actionable then, rather than at nine?**
- **What can I do without waking anybody?** The list should be long and written.
- **What is the retainer, separately from the call-out rate?** If there is no retainer, the constraint is not being paid for.
- **What happens the morning after a bad night?** *"Take the day"* as a stated default, or discretion nobody uses.
- **Who is my escalation, and have they ever been called?** A path nobody has used is a name, not a path.
- **How many people are in the rota?** One in four is a life. One in two is a temporary arrangement being described as permanent.

The last one deserves saying plainly: **a rota that only works while nobody leaves is already broken**, and everyone in it knows the date it will fail.
