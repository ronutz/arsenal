## Two nights, one mechanism

On the night of 11 to 12 October 2026, the refrigerated aisles of hundreds of French supermarkets went into defrost and stayed there. By morning, freezers that should have been holding food at well below zero were warm, some far warmer than room temperature, and whole aisles of fresh and frozen products had to be thrown away. On 13 October, France's Finance Minister, Roland Lescure, told Parliament that about 700 of the country's 10,000 supermarkets had been affected, as the Associated Press (AP) reported him.

Three weeks later it happened again. On the night of 1 to 2 November, as Euronews and the BBC reported this morning, fridges and freezers warmed overnight in supermarkets in Belgium, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Sweden, Finland, Estonia and Ireland. A Belgian refrigeration installer told the Belga news agency that the evaporators had been switched to defrost mode, which he said was the same mechanism as in France.

The obvious question is how anyone can switch a supermarket freezer from the internet. The short answer is that the freezer was never the target. The target is the small computer that runs the whole refrigeration plant of a store, a supervisory controller with a web interface, and in a great many stores that interface was reachable from the internet so that a contractor could look after it remotely. That is an Internet of Things (IoT) story and an operational technology (OT) story at the same time, and it is the same story that sits under almost every notorious IoT hack of the past fifteen years.

Everything below comes from the documents of the parties themselves, the French government's computer emergency response team, the manufacturers, the researchers, regulators and courts, and, where only the press has it, from the press, labelled as such. Every source was read on 2 November 2026 and is listed at the end.

## What was attacked: the store's refrigeration supervisor

A supermarket's display cases, cold rooms and freezers each have a small controller of their own, which keeps a setpoint and runs the defrost cycles that stop ice building up on the evaporator coils. Above them sits a system manager that coordinates all of them. Claroty's Team82, which published research on one of these platforms, the Danfoss AK-SM 800A, on 9 August, describes it as "a centralized system manager responsible for coordinating refrigeration controllers throughout an installation", with a web interface from which an operator monitors and configures the whole refrigeration environment. Team82 also says why the interface is there: technicians administer these systems remotely, which saves a trip to every installation. The exposed consoles it found suggested, in its words, "that administrators had exposed the web console for remote management".

That is the detail a network person should hold on to. In France the remote link is the refrigeration contractor's, the *prestataire frigoriste*, not the store IT department's: the alert from CERT-FR, the computer emergency response team of the national cybersecurity agency ANSSI (Agence nationale de la sécurité des systèmes d'information), speaks of the data sent over the internet to that contractor and of filtering on the contractors' source addresses, and even tells owners to check their insurance contract before cutting that feed. Dominique Schelcher, the chief executive of Coopérative U, put the same point in commercial terms on RTL radio, as the Guardian reported: "Our supermarkets are not customers of Danfoss." The controllers are bought, installed and maintained through a contractor, and many of the people who run the store never see them.

Defrost is the clever part of the attack, and it is clever only in the sense that it is ordinary. A refrigeration controller is supposed to defrost. CERT-FR's alert asks owners to check their whole configuration, "notamment les plages de dégivrage", the defrost ranges in particular. Press accounts add the rest: BFM TV, as AP relayed it, reported a continuous defrost cycle with temperatures set as high as 50°C, and the Guardian reported that initial investigations suggested the alarms had been disabled, or the defrost programme extended, so that nothing went off until the first employee opened a freezer in the morning.

## What the authorities and the manufacturers said

**CERT-FR** published alert CERTFR-2026-ALE-012 on 12 October and revised it twice: on 13 October to add other targeted equipment, and on 16 October to update its recommendations. As read this morning, it lists the Danfoss AK-SM and AK-SC series and Copeland and Dixell xweb, and says CERT-FR is aware of "nombreuses compromissions", numerous compromises, of those remote controllers, that exposing a management interface on the internet "est contraire aux bonnes pratiques", is contrary to good practice, and that attack campaigns against these devices are "probablement toujours en cours", probably still under way. The alert names no vulnerability and no actor, and its status on CERT-FR's list is still *Alerte en cours*.

Its instructions are the most useful text published about this event, and they are network instructions. Remove the internet exposure of the affected units and of every unit reachable on a public IP address. Restore access only through a secure virtual private network (VPN) or a protected network. In an emergency, filter on the contractors' source addresses at a firewall, but do not treat that as lasting. Update the firmware where there is one, set Danfoss equipment's security level to Strict, and make sure default passwords have been changed. And one warning I would print and pin on the wall of every OT team: for an end-of-life device with no security fix, a factory reset removes what the attacker left behind, but "l'équipement restera vulnérable et sera certainement de nouveau compromis", the device stays vulnerable and will certainly be compromised again.

**Danfoss** gave its account to the trade publication Cooling Post on 12 October. Most of the affected units were legacy products, or current products running outdated firmware: the AK-SM800, declared end of life in 2021, and the AK-SC355, phased out in 2016, neither of which receives software or security updates any more; the current AK-SM800A units that were hit were running outdated firmware. Danfoss said it was working with affected customers on "firmware updates where available, stronger network protections and removing direct internet exposure where applicable". AP quoted the company the same week: it "has long advised customers to keep supported systems updated with the latest firmware, as well as to operate behind a firewall". Danfoss's own security advisories page, read this morning, carries no advisory about the attack; its latest entry, of 18 September, concerns another product line.

**Copeland**, which owns the Dixell brand, published a product bulletin whose French version, dated 14 October and completed by Dixell France, is the document CERT-FR links from its alert. It acknowledges a vulnerability in a generation of XWEB EVO controllers that is no longer sold and has reached end of life, tells customers to remove every XWEB EVO from the public internet at once, with no port forwarding, no DMZ (demilitarised zone) and no direct public IP address, to look for suspicious activity from the local network and to restore the units, and to plan a migration to XWEB PRO, which "ne doit pas être exposé à Internet" either. Its further advice reads like a segmentation lesson, and I come back to it below.

**The retailers**, as reported: Groupement Les Mousquetaires said 300 of its Intermarché and Netto stores were affected, and Coopérative U's chief executive said 80 of its 2,000, according to AP; the Guardian names Carrefour, Leclerc and Monoprix among the others, and quotes Intermarché's chief executive, Thierry Cotillard, putting the losses at possibly "tens of millions of euros". **The Paris prosecutor's office** said on 13 October that its cybercrime unit was investigating the attack, including the extent of the damage from spoiled food and reported fires, according to AP. Nobody has claimed it, and no authority has named anyone.

## Which flaw? No document names one

It is tempting to write that a known vulnerability went unpatched. The record is more interesting than that, and less tidy.

Danfoss's system managers have a public vulnerability history. In January 2023, researchers from HackDefense reported flaws in the AK-SM 800A through the Dutch Institute for Vulnerability Disclosure (DIVD), which, after Danfoss published its advisory DSA-2023-08-01 in August 2023 (three CVEs, fixed in release 3.3), began notifying the owners of vulnerable internet-facing units in September 2023. Those three CVEs, entries in the Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures list, were the first of several. In March 2025 Danfoss published DSA-2025-03-01, CVE-2025-41450, an authentication bypass in the AK-SM 8xxA series scored 8.2 under the Common Vulnerability Scoring System (CVSS), fixed in release 4.2; in August 2025, DSA-2025-08-01, two post-authentication flaws, CVE-2025-41451 (command injection leading to remote code execution) and CVE-2025-41452, fixed in 4.3.1. Both 2025 advisories credit Tomer Goldschmidt of Claroty Team82.

Team82's write-up of 9 August 2026 sums up what it found: a hidden "code-of-the-day" authentication mechanism, which it believes was "likely intended for vendor support or maintenance", that could be abused to bypass normal authentication, a command injection that led to remote code execution, and a way for an authenticated user to inject web server configuration. Before looking at the firmware, Team82 counted exposed management interfaces with internet-wide scanning services and reports 2,765 AK-SM 800A devices exposed according to Censys. The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) published a separate advisory in February 2026, ICSA-26-057-10, for 23 vulnerabilities in Copeland XWEB and XWEB Pro, also reported by Team82, with fixes for XWEB Pro.

Team82's account of the code of the day, CVE-2025-41450, is short and worth knowing in outline. Instead of checking the configured administrator password, the web application accepted a specially crafted authentication request carrying a generated "code of the day", which it checked against a password the device generated internally from its current date and time. Team82 says it fully reconstructed the routine that produces the daily code, so that "anyone capable of reproducing the algorithm could generate valid authentication credentials without knowing the administrator's password", and that a login through this path gave "a fully authenticated administrative session". For an owner, the point is that a credential nobody at the site had chosen opened the administrative interface. Team82's advice is firmware R4.3.1 or later and management interfaces kept off the internet; Danfoss had fixed the bypass itself in release 4.2, in March 2025.

Now put those next to what the parties said in October. CERT-FR's alert lists no CVE. Danfoss says the majority of the units hit were either legacy AK-SM800 and AK-SC355 systems, which are not the AK-SM 800A of the advisories above and get no updates, or current AK-SM800A units on outdated firmware, without saying which release. Copeland says its affected generation is end of life, and does not say which vulnerability. So no first-party document I read says which flaw was used, or whether any flaw beyond reachable, unprotected logins was needed. The one movement in the records since the attack is CISA's: entries dated 19 October in each of the three 2025 Danfoss CVE records, under its Stakeholder-Specific Vulnerability Categorization (SSVC), give the exploitation status as "poc", a public proof of concept. None of them is in CISA's catalogue of known exploited vulnerabilities (version 2026.10.30).

My assessment, labelled as such: the detail of which bug matters less here than the shape. The fleets that were hit combined three things, a management interface reachable from the internet, a device that its maker no longer fixes or a fixed device that nobody had updated, and an access model built for a contractor's convenience. Any one of those can be survived. All three together is what CERT-FR's warning about end-of-life devices describes.

## The second wave, as reported this morning

This part is still moving, and everything in it comes from the press of today, 2 November, relaying what the companies and agencies said. Euronews counts about 75 affected stores in Belgium, from figures in Le Soir: around 40 Delhaize Proxy supermarkets, 15 Carrefour Shop & Go, 15 Intermarché and five Spar Q8 service-station shops, plus a butchery group and the IKEA store at Mons. Carrefour Belgium said 15 of its 700 stores were affected, none of them hypermarkets, and its spokesman said "It's a cyberattack, there is no other reason". The Belgian federal prosecutor's office has opened an investigation, and the Centre for Cybersecurity Belgium (CCB) said it was aware of an incident but had no further information.

Outside Belgium, the BBC reports that ICA said a "small number of ICA stores operating outside ICA's central network" were affected in Sweden, and that Coop counted seven of its 800 Swedish stores; that Musgrave, which owns SuperValu, confirmed to the Irish Times a "cyber incident" at 13 Irish stores, limited to a "specific model" of refrigerator; that Danfoss told the Dutch broadcaster NOS that the cooling systems of at least 17 companies in the Netherlands were attacked overnight, affecting butchers' shops and small neighbourhood stores; that a Selver store in Estonia was affected; and that refrigerator malfunctions were detected at some of Kesko's K-stores in Finland. Danfoss told the Belgian broadcaster VTM, as Euronews relayed, that the vulnerability lies primarily in outdated software and that it had urged customers to move to its newer software. The UK's National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) said it was working with partners to "fully understand details of an incident"; no UK incident has been reported.

Two sentences from that coverage are worth a network engineer's attention. ICA's says the stores that were hit sat outside its central network; my reading is that they sat outside whatever controls that network applies. And Stephan Moelans, chief executive of Sabcobel, the installer that runs Delhaize's refrigeration, told Belga that his company had been working since the French attack to protect customers against this vulnerability, that a software update alone is not enough and physical modifications are needed, which takes investment, and that "the simplest solution is to manage the fridges over a secure internet connection. Most of our customers switched to that after the attack in France." Three weeks after a national alert, the change most of his customers had made is, as I read it, the one CERT-FR's alert recommends: take the controller off the open internet and reach it through a protected path.

## A transatlantic footnote: the commissaries

In late August, before France, several US military commissaries, the grocery stores on bases, reported refrigeration failures within days of each other. Fort Huachuca's own post, as Military Times quoted it, said "ALL freezers went into defrost mode". A defence official confirmed to Military Times "a possible refrigeration disruption at some Defense Commissary Agency commissaries", and Stars and Stripes reported that the Army Criminal Investigation Division's (CID) Cyber Field Office and the Air Force Office of Special Investigations had opened investigations. In late October, a CID spokesman told Newsweek the division was working with the Defense Criminal Investigative Service "and French law enforcement in understanding the refrigeration outages in Europe". No official has said publicly what caused the American outages, which equipment was involved, or whether they are connected to Europe, and I do not assume they are.

## The same lesson, a decade of times

None of this is new in kind. What is new is the scale and that the payload was food. Here are the cases I would put in front of a class, each from the documents of the people who handled it, and each for the one thing it teaches.

| Year | Incident | Entry point, per the record | What it teaches |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2010 | Stuxnet | Industrial control systems; code modified on programmable logic controllers | Physical processes can be attacked through their controllers |
| 2013 | Target | A refrigeration contractor's network credentials | The contractor's access is part of your perimeter |
| 2015 | Jeep Cherokee | The infotainment unit, reachable over the cellular network | A block in the carrier's network closed the remote path before owners patched |
| 2016 | Mirai | Cameras, routers and video recorders with factory logins | You cannot patch a default |
| 2017 | Abbott pacemakers | Radio-frequency authentication that could be bypassed | Some updates carry their own risk and need a human decision |
| 2018 | Casino fish tank (an anecdote, told in 2018) | A connected aquarium thermometer, as told by Darktrace's chief executive | Every connected thing on a flat network is a door |
| 2021 | Oldsmar water plant | Remote desktop software on an outdated operating system, as first reported | Independent physical safeguards, and how first accounts can change |
| 2024 | Verkada (settlement) | The company's alleged failures in access management and data protection | Cloud-managed means the vendor's security is yours |
| 2024 | Integrity Tech botnet (Flax Typhoon) | Over 260,000 routers, firewalls, storage and IoT devices, many likely still supported | Botnets do not need end-of-life devices, only exposed ones |
| 2025 | BADBOX 2.0 | Devices compromised before purchase or during set-up | The supply chain can deliver the compromise in the box |

**Stuxnet**, briefly, because it is industrial control rather than IoT: Symantec's dossier of February 2011 describes a threat "targeting a specific industrial control system likely in Iran" that modified code on Siemens programmable logic controllers to make them operate "most likely out of their specified boundaries". It belongs here as the proof, from 2010, that the controller is the target.

**Target, 2013**, is the case closest to this month's in its anatomy. The US Senate Commerce Committee's majority staff report of 26 March 2014 says the attackers "reportedly first gained access to Target's system by stealing credentials from an HVAC and refrigeration company, Fazio Mechanical Services" (HVAC being heating, ventilation and air conditioning), a contractor the report describes as specialising in refrigeration for supermarkets, which had remote access to Target's network for billing and project management. The report's findings include that Target "gave network access to a third-party vendor" whose security was weak, and that the attackers then appear to have moved to the areas storing consumer data, "suggesting that Target failed to properly isolate its most sensitive network assets". Thirteen years apart, the same kind of company holds the same kind of key.

**The Jeep Cherokee, 2015.** Charlie Miller and Chris Valasek's paper of 10 August 2015, "Remote Exploitation of an Unaltered Passenger Vehicle", records that they disclosed to Fiat Chrysler from October 2014, that Chrysler released a patch on 16 July 2015, and that on 24 July the cellular carrier's network was reconfigured to block the traffic and Chrysler recalled 1.4 million vehicles. The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) recall record, 15V-461, counts 1,416,903 vehicles with radios whose software "can allow third-party access to certain networked vehicle control systems", and states that Chrysler had the wireless provider close the open cellular connection to the vehicle, adding that the measure may not have reached every vehicle. That is the detail I would put in front of a class: one change in the carrier's network closed the long-distance path to unpatched cars before most owners could have installed the update, and the researchers note that what remained needed an attacker close to the vehicle.

**Mirai, 2016**, has its own article, [Mirai: the botnet that did not exploit anything](/learn/mirai-and-the-default-password), so only two records here. CISA's alert TA16-288A of October 2016 says the bot used "a short list of 62 common default usernames and passwords" to find its devices. And the US Department of Justice announced on 13 December 2017 that the three authors had pleaded guilty in the District of Alaska on 8 December, that the botnet targeted "wireless cameras, routers, and digital video recorders", that at its peak it held "hundreds of thousands of compromised devices", and that after the source code was posted "other criminal actors have used Mirai variants". The [distributed denial-of-service family history](/learn/ddos-family-history) places the Dyn attack of October 2016 in that lineage.

**Abbott's pacemakers, 2017.** CISA's advisory ICSMA-17-241-01 of 29 August 2017 describes pacemakers whose radio-frequency authentication "can be compromised or bypassed, which may allow a nearby attacker to issue unauthorized commands". The fix was firmware, applied to an implanted device through a programmer in a clinic, and the advisory asks healthcare providers to weigh "the potential risk of a cybersecurity attack along with the risk of performing a firmware update". It is the cleanest example I know that patching is a decision with its own risk, which is exactly the decision a supermarket faces when an update needs a technician on site.

**The casino fish tank** is an anecdote, and I tell it as one. In April 2018, Darktrace's chief executive Nicole Eagan told the Wall Street Journal's CEO Council conference, as Business Insider reported, that in one case her company worked on, attackers got into a casino through a thermometer in a lobby aquarium, "found the high-roller database and then pulled that back across the network, out the thermostat, and up to the cloud". The casino is unnamed, and I know of no public technical record of it. It survives in every IoT talk because it is the flat-network lesson in one sentence.

**Oldsmar, 2021**, is the one that teaches humility. The advisory that the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), CISA, the Environmental Protection Agency and the Multi-State Information Sharing and Analysis Center issued in February 2021, AA21-042A, still reads that on 5 February "unidentified cyber actors obtained unauthorized access" to a water plant's supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) system and raised the sodium hydroxide dose, that plant personnel noticed and corrected it at once, and that the access likely came through poor password security, an outdated operating system and possibly desktop-sharing software. The same advisory, as published today, also says the FBI "was not able to confirm that this incident was initiated by a targeted cyber intrusion", and in 2023 the city manager at the time, Al Braithwaite, said it was a "nonevent" likely caused by employee error, as the Tampa Bay Times reported. Two lessons, then: the advisory's own best recommendation, independent physical safety systems that stop a dangerous dose whatever the software says; and that the first account of an OT incident is a hypothesis.

**Verkada, settled in 2024.** The Department of Justice announced on 4 September 2024 that Verkada, a cloud-based security camera company, would pay a $2.95 million civil penalty, and set up a comprehensive information security programme with regular third-party assessments, to resolve allegations, brought with the Federal Trade Commission (FTC), that it had failed to implement reasonable security, such as appropriate access management, and that this exposed customers' camera footage, from places such as hospitals and schools, to unauthorised access. When the device is managed from the vendor's cloud, the vendor's access controls are part of your camera.

**The Integrity Tech botnet, 2024, and BADBOX 2.0, 2025**, are the current shape. The joint advisory of 18 September 2024 from the FBI, the Cyber National Mission Force and the National Security Agency describes a botnet controlled by Integrity Technology Group, a company based in the People's Republic of China, whose control addresses, the FBI says, were also used in intrusions consistent with the group known publicly as Flax Typhoon: over 260,000 routers, firewalls, network storage and IoT devices as of June 2024, recruited with malware of the Mirai family. It adds a sentence that should worry anyone who thinks only old kit is at risk: "many of the compromised devices ... are likely still supported by their respective vendors". The FBI's public service announcement of 5 June 2025 on BADBOX 2.0 describes "millions of infected devices", streaming boxes, projectors, picture frames, compromised either before purchase or while downloading apps during set-up. That is the supply chain delivering the attacker's access in the box.

## The causes that keep coming back

Read the table again and the same handful of causes appear, in different clothes.

- **Logins that the owner did not choose.** Mirai's factory passwords, the hidden "code of the day" Team82 found, the default passwords that CERT-FR tells Danfoss owners to change, and the default password that Marta Rybczyńska, a security researcher quoted by the Guardian, says many of the French freezers appeared to run with.
- **Management interfaces that face the internet.** Team82's 2,765 AK-SM 800A interfaces in Censys, the XWEB units that Copeland says must have no port forwarding, no DMZ and no public address, the cellular interface on the Jeep, and CERT-FR's general rule that exposing a management interface on the internet is contrary to good practice.
- **No update path, or no update.** The AK-SC355, phased out in 2016, and the AK-SM800, end of life since 2021, get no updates, the XWEB EVO is end of service, and Oldsmar's advisory warns about Windows 7. Equally common is the supported device that nobody updated: Danfoss's AK-SM800A units on old firmware, after fixes published in 2023 and 2025.
- **Access that belongs to someone else.** Fazio Mechanical at Target, the *frigoristes* in France, the refrigeration installers in Belgium, Verkada's cloud. The people who need remote access are not the people who own the network, and their access is often the least watched path in.
- **Flat networks.** Target's report says it plainly. The casino anecdote says it memorably. ICA's stores "operating outside ICA's central network" say it from the other side, as I read it: whatever controls the central network had, those stores were not behind them.
- **Alarms that run on the same system as the thing they watch.** If the reported detail holds, the French attack disabled the alarms or extended the defrost programme so that they did not trigger, and nobody knew until morning. My assessment: a temperature alarm that the attacker can switch off from the same console is a configuration setting, not a safeguard. Oldsmar's advisory has the better model, an independent safety system.

## What changed in law and standards, and what it reaches

The Mirai article tells how regulation became the answer to defaults that no market would fix. Here is where the main texts stand today, from the bodies that publish them.

| Text | What it requires | Applies to | Status, as read on 2 November 2026 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| UK Product Security and Telecommunications Infrastructure (PSTI) Act 2022 and its 2023 security requirements regulations | Passwords "unique per product" or "defined by the user"; a published contact for reporting security issues; a published minimum period of security updates | Relevant connectable products for UK consumers | In force since 29 April 2024 |
| ETSI EN 303 645, "Cyber Security for Consumer Internet of Things", from the European Telecommunications Standards Institute | Thirteen groups of provisions, starting with "No universal default passwords" and a means to manage vulnerability reports | Consumer IoT devices | Version 3.1.3, September 2024 |
| EU Radio Equipment Directive delegated regulation (EU) 2022/30 | Activates the essential requirements of Articles 3(3)(d), (e) and (f), for cybersecurity, personal data protection and privacy | Certain categories of radio equipment | In application since August 2025; repealed with effect from 11 December 2027 by (EU) 2026/339, when the CRA takes over |
| EU Cyber Resilience Act (CRA), Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 | Essential cybersecurity requirements in design and production, vulnerability handling for a declared support period, reporting, CE marking | Hardware and software "products with digital elements" on the EU market | In force since 10 December 2024; reporting obligations since 11 September 2026; main obligations from 11 December 2027 |
| US Cyber Trust Mark | A voluntary label | Wireless consumer IoT products | Rules adopted 14 March 2024; the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) says it is "now in the process of standing up" the programme |

Two things stand out when a supermarket controller is held against that table. First, most of these texts are consumer texts by their own words: the PSTI Act gives the power to set requirements for products "made available to consumers in the United Kingdom", ETSI EN 303 645 is a standard for consumer IoT, and the FCC's programme is for "wireless consumer IoT products". A refrigeration system manager sold to a contractor for a supermarket is not what they were written for. The CRA is different: the Commission's summary calls it a horizontal framework for hardware and software products made available on the EU market, and its reporting obligations, in the Commission's words, apply to products "including those already placed on the market before 11 December 2027". Danfoss's own disclosure page already cites it.

Second, and this is my assessment, every one of these texts sets its design and update requirements for products as they are placed on the market, with the CRA's reporting duty the one exception that reaches what is already installed, and the controllers hit in October and November were installed years ago, some before any of these texts existed. The CRA's design requirements will shape the controllers sold from December 2027. The AK-SC355 phased out in 2016 will not get a security update from any regulation. For the installed base, the remedy is operational, and it is ours.

## What I would do this week

If I ran refrigeration, building management or any other OT in a store, a warehouse or a plant, this is the list, in this order. Most of it comes straight from CERT-FR's alert and Copeland's bulletin; the rest is the network practice I teach, labelled where it is my addition.

1. **Find every controller, by what is reachable, not by what is on the asset register.** Ask each contractor, in writing, which devices they reach remotely and how. Then test from outside, the way Copeland's bulletin suggests: from a phone with Wi-Fi turned off, type the site's public address into a browser; the controller's login page must not appear. Inside, an [OUI lookup](/tools/oui-lookup) on the MAC addresses in the switch tables (the organisationally unique identifier is the manufacturer's prefix) will show you manufacturers you did not know were on the network.
2. **Remove inbound exposure, completely.** No port forwarding, no DMZ, no public address, as Copeland puts it; CERT-FR asks for the same of every unit exposed on a public IP address. If the controller has a public IP address today, that is the first change.
3. **Give the contractor a controlled path in.** A VPN or a zero trust network access (ZTNA) service that knows who the technician is, uses multi-factor authentication (MFA) where it can, and logs each session; the [zero trust article](/learn/zero-trust-ztna-and-sase-without-the-marketing) covers the options. CERT-FR accepts filtering on the contractor's source addresses only as an emergency measure, and so would I: an address says where a connection came from, not who made it.
4. **Put the controllers on their own segment.** A dedicated virtual local area network (VLAN), separate from the office, the tills and the guest Wi-Fi, as Copeland's bulletin says, with outbound traffic limited to what the controller needs, its examples being the mail server, DNS, the Network Time Protocol (NTP) and the vendor's update server, and everything else blocked. Write the rules as an access control list (ACL) and check its first-match behaviour before you deploy it with the [ACL first-match evaluator](/tools/acl-first-match-evaluator); the [CIDR calculator](/tools/cidr) helps with the addressing of the new segment. The [segmentation article](/learn/network-segmentation-from-vlans-to-microsegmentation) explains why the hard part is knowing what talks to what.
5. **Change every credential, and keep changing it.** Named accounts, no shared ones, the least privilege each person needs, and new passwords after every contractor intervention with a privileged account, all from Copeland's list. On Danfoss AK-SM 800A, update the firmware and set the security level to Strict, as CERT-FR says.
6. **Decide about the end-of-life units, deliberately.** The AK-SM800 and the AK-SC355 get no updates, and the XWEB EVO gets no more security fixes (Copeland asks owners to install its last software release in the meantime), and CERT-FR says a reset alone will not keep them safe. Off the internet and segmented is the plan until they are replaced, and the replacement date belongs in a budget.
7. **Give the alarm a second channel.** My assessment, building on the Oldsmar advisory's independent safety systems: a temperature probe and alert path that does not depend on the controller an attacker can reconfigure, so that a warm freezer at 2 a.m. wakes someone up.
8. **Read the contracts.** CERT-FR's note about insurance is a reminder that the remote feed to the contractor may be a condition of the insurance contract. Who is responsible for the controller's security, who patches it, and who is told when something changes belongs in the maintenance contract, not in an assumption.

## What I take from it

I wrote last week about [PHP2Shell and the layer nobody thinks they own](/blog/php2shell-and-the-layer-nobody-owns). A supermarket's refrigeration controller is that layer in its purest form. IT does not buy it, facilities does not configure it, the contractor maintains it under a service contract, and the manufacturer stopped fixing it years ago. Everyone is reasonable, and the freezer faces the internet.

Thirty years in this industry have taught me that the controls that would have stopped the attacks of October and November are not new: know what you own, do not publish management interfaces, give third parties a controlled door instead of an open one, separate what does not need to talk, and do not let the thing you are protecting also be the only thing that raises the alarm. What has changed is the cost of forgetting them. Ten years ago an exposed controller was a risk on a slide. This year it was food in the bins in two waves across eight countries, losses that Intermarché's chief executive put at possibly tens of millions of euros, and an alert from the French government's response team that is still open.
