<MethodNote />

Two kinds of warning met in 2026. One came from companies and security agencies: artificial intelligence (AI) models now find, and sometimes exploit, software vulnerabilities faster than people can fix them. The other is older: the forecast that a technology, a date or a war will end civilisation. What follows sets out both in their authors' words, then what research has measured about fear, two predicted ends that did not arrive, who owns the feeds that carry the warnings, and what "the algorithm" is.

Rodolfo Nützmann says he knows a lot of people who are scared and who are getting their information from apocalyptic propaganda.

## The present wave: AI that finds and exploits flaws

AI-powered vulnerability discovery is software, built on AI models, that reads code or exercises a running program, reports flaws an attacker could use and, in some systems, writes the exploit or the patch. Each organisation counts its own results, and no end-to-end audit of any of the counts was found.

On 7 April 2026 Anthropic announced Claude Mythos Preview and Project Glasswing, stating that "AI models have reached a level of coding capability where they can surpass all but the most skilled humans at finding and exploiting software vulnerabilities", and warning that "it will not be long before such capabilities proliferate, potentially beyond actors who are committed to deploying them safely" ([Anthropic, April 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/glasswing)). On 22 May Anthropic reported that it and about 50 partners had found <Claim status="reported" by="by Anthropic">"more than ten thousand high- or critical-severity vulnerabilities"</Claim> ([Anthropic, May 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/glasswing-initial-update)); on 6 October, "at least 129,000 verified software vulnerabilities between April and July 2026", with a patch rate it called "significantly undercounted" ([Anthropic, 6 October 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/cyber-verification-program)). On 8 October it added that "we haven't yet achieved a sufficient reduction in cyber risk" ([Anthropic, 8 October 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-cyber-mission)). The older argument over releasing such tools is in [the argument that comes back every time the tool gets better](/blog/satan-to-mythos-the-argument-that-comes-back).

Access was suspended in June and restored in July. On 12 June Anthropic said the government of the United States had issued "an export control directive to suspend all access to Fable 5 and Mythos 5 by any foreign national, whether inside or outside the United States", and that it disagreed that "a narrow potential jailbreak should be cause for recalling a commercial model" ([Anthropic, 12 June 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/fable-mythos-access)). Unable to verify nationality in real time, it suspended both models for all users; on 1 July it announced that "Access to Claude Fable 5 and Mythos 5 is now restored", Mythos 5 first for a set of American organisations ([Anthropic, 30 June 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/redeploying-fable-5)). The directive's text was not found published; a policy tracker records that "On 30 June 2026, the United States Commerce Department terminated the export control directive" ([Digital Policy Alert, 2026](https://digitalpolicyalert.org/event/41240-commerce-department-terminated-export-controls-suspending-anthropics-claude-fable-5-and-mythos-5-models)).

Outside evaluators confirmed part of the capability and bounded it. The United Kingdom's AI Security Institute (AISI) reported that Mythos Preview was "the first model to solve TLO from start to finish, in 3 out of its 10 attempts", TLO (The Last Ones) being a 32-step simulated network attack, though its ranges "lack security features that are often present, such as active defenders" ([AISI, 2026](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/our-evaluation-of-claude-mythos-previews-cyber-capabilities)). The blogger Davi Ottenheimer applied the old word for scare marketing, fear, uncertainty and doubt (FUD), to the launch: <Claim status="disputed">"That is the worst form of FUD: anchor to something true, then extend the credibility to something unproven"</Claim> ([Ottenheimer, 2026](https://www.flyingpenguin.com/the-boy-that-cried-mythos-verification-is-collapsing-trust-in-anthropic/)). The system card says that in its Firefox exploit test "almost every successful run relies on the same two now-patched bugs", and that without them "Claude Mythos Preview continues to outperform past models" ([Anthropic, system card, 2026](https://anthropic.com/claude-mythos-preview-system-card)). AISLE, which sells AI security analysis, reported that "Eight out of eight models detected Mythos's flagship FreeBSD exploit" ([Fort, 2026](https://aisle.com/blog/ai-cybersecurity-after-mythos-the-jagged-frontier)). Counting public disclosure records, Patrick Garrity of VulnCheck found that only 202 Glasswing findings, 0.8% of the total, had been fixed ([Garrity, 2026](https://www.vulncheck.com/blog/anthropic-glasswing-receipts)), and TechTarget that only 14 of 1,061 vulnerabilities attributed to AI-assisted discovery had been listed as exploited in the wild by June 2026 ([TechTarget, 2026](https://www.techtarget.com/it-infrastructure/news/366651381/Glasswing-results-put-AI-vulnpocalypse-to-the-test)).

Earlier systems reported smaller numbers. Google called Big Sleep's 2024 SQLite finding "the first public example of an AI agent finding a previously unknown exploitable memory-safety issue in widely used real-world software" ([Project Zero, 2024](https://googleprojectzero.blogspot.com/2024/10/from-naptime-to-big-sleep.html)). At the 2025 final of the AI Cyber Challenge (AIxCC) of the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA), systems found 54 unique synthetic vulnerabilities across the final's 63 challenges and patched 43 of them ([DARPA, 2025](https://www.darpa.mil/news/2025/aixcc-results)). OpenAI said its Aardvark agent had found flaws in open-source code, "ten of which have received Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE) identifiers" ([OpenAI, 2025](https://openai.com/index/introducing-aardvark/)). XBOW said it had <Claim status="disputed">"reached the top spot on the US leaderboard"</Claim> of HackerOne ([XBOW, 2025](https://xbow.com/blog/top-1-how-xbow-did-it/)); a former HackerOne triage analyst, Utku Sen, replied that "XBOW topped the VDP (Vulnerability Disclosure Program) leaderboard and not the Bug Bounty program" ([Sen, 2025](https://utkusen.substack.com/p/does-xbow-ai-hacker-deserve-the-hype)).

The cyber agencies addressed defenders. The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) of the United States said in June 2026 that attackers' "use of AI may further narrow the time defenders have to react between patch release and possible exploitation" ([CISA, 2026](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/directives/bod-26-04-prioritizing-security-updates-based-risk)). The British National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) told defenders to "assume that at least some attackers already have access to capable AI tools" ([NCSC, March 2026](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/blogs/why-cyber-defenders-need-to-be-ready-for-frontier-ai)), but noted that of more than 40,000 vulnerabilities given CVEs in 2025 about 400 were tracked as exploited, and that an unpatched known flaw or unauthorised access is much more likely to affect security than a zero-day ([NCSC, May 2026](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/blogs/10-questions-ask-using-ai-models-find-vulnerabilities)). No publication on the subject was found from the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA), which got access to Mythos through Glasswing, The Next Web reported ([The Next Web, 2026](https://thenextweb.com/news/anthropic-mythos-eu-enisa-cybersecurity-access)).

In Brazil, nothing on AI-found vulnerabilities was found from the Gabinete de Segurança Institucional (GSI) or its Centro de Prevenção, Tratamento e Resposta a Incidentes Cibernéticos de Governo (CTIR Gov). The GSI counted 6,774 incidents in federal bodies up to early June 2026 ([Folhapress, 2026](https://www.acessa.com/noticias/2026/06/amp/331600-sistemas-do-governo-registram-cerca-de-6-700-incidentes-em-2026-aponta-gsi.html)), and a joint note by CTIR Gov says that "os incidentes cibernéticos ocorridos em 2026 exploraram vulnerabilidades comuns em todas as instituições vítimas", that is, the year's incidents ran on common weaknesses ([CTIR Gov, 2026](https://www.gov.br/gsi/pt-br/assuntos/ctir/recomendacoes/nota_conjunta_ctir_cisc-1.pdf)).

In the early hours of 20 June 2026, Paraná's justice secretariat said, the Defesa Civil Alerta platform was taken offline <Claim status="attributed" by="by Paraná's government">"após ter sofrido uma invasão"</Claim>, after an intrusion, having sent an extreme alert with the word "misantropia", hatred of humanity ([Paraná, 2026](https://www.justica.pr.gov.br/sites/default/arquivos_restritos/files/documento/2026-06/Nota%20oficial%20-%20Misantropia.pdf)). Ten messages went out to cities with about 30 million inhabitants combined, a population rather than a measured reach ([Agência Brasil, 2026](https://www.dgabc.com.br/Noticia/4330413/falso-alerta-da-defesa-civil-atingiu-cerca-de-30-milhoes-de-pessoas)). One announced an alien attack on Belo Horizonte, Folhapress reported; the Federal Police are investigating ([Folhapress, 2026](https://www.acessa.com/noticias/2026/06/amp/331600-sistemas-do-governo-registram-cerca-de-6-700-incidentes-em-2026-aponta-gsi.html)). No finding on the sender was found, and no source links the case to AI.

## How a capability became an apocalypse

The apocalyptic framing predates Mythos. In May 2023 the Center for AI Safety (CAIS) published one sentence: "Mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside other societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war" ([CAIS, 2023](https://safe.ai/work/statement-on-ai-risk)). In April 2025 the AI Futures Project published AI 2027, a forecast whose "race" ending has an AI that "releases a dozen quiet-spreading biological weapons in major cities" ([AI 2027, race ending](https://ai-2027.com/race)). The critic titotal argued that <Claim status="disputed">"The scenario in the short story is not the median forecast for any AI futures author"</Claim> ([titotal, 2025](https://titotal.substack.com/p/a-deep-critique-of-ai-2027s-bad-timeline)); the authors replied that "we disagree with most of titotal's criticisms", while conceding a fix that "would have pushed back the median Superhuman Coder arrival time by about 9 months" ([Lifland, 2025](https://aifuturesnotes.substack.com/p/response-to-titotals-critique-of)). Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nate Soares's 2025 book is presented by its publisher with the line "The scramble to create superhuman AI has put us on the path to extinction" ([Hachette, 2025](https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/titles/eliezer-yudkowsky/if-anyone-builds-it-everyone-dies/9780316595643/)); Clara Collier's review found that "both authors are persistently unable to update their priors" ([Collier, 2025](https://asteriskmag.com/issues/11/iabied)).

In January 2026 the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists set its Doomsday Clock at 85 seconds to midnight, "the closest it has ever been to catastrophe", citing among other risks "the AI-aided design of new pathogens to which humans have no effective defenses"; the Bulletin says the clock uses "the imagery of apocalypse (midnight)" ([Bulletin, 2026](https://thebulletin.org/doomsday-clock/2026-statement/)). Lawrence Krauss, who chaired the board that sets it from 2009 to 2018, called it "a publicity stunt" and said "the factors of its setting are now dominated more by policy questions than scientific ones" ([Fortune, 2023](https://fortune.com/2023/01/25/doomsday-clock-2023-90-seconds-to-midnight-who-sets-it-does-anyone-care/)).

The counter-positions come from researchers and editors. Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor reject visions that treat AI "akin to a separate species" and propose resilience as "the overarching approach to catastrophic risks" ([Narayanan and Kapoor, 2025](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology)). A Nature editorial argued that "Talk of artificial intelligence destroying humanity plays into the tech companies' agenda, and hinders effective regulation of the societal harms AI is causing right now" ([Nature, 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-023-02094-7)). The authors of the Stochastic Parrots paper, writing for the Distributed AI Research (DAIR) Institute, said the focus should be "the very real and very present exploitative practices of the companies claiming to build them" ([DAIR, 2023](https://www.dair-institute.org/blog/letter-statement-March2023/)). Yann LeCun said in October 2026 that he was not worried "at all" about AI wiping out humanity ([Fortune, 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/10/01/ai-godfather-yann-lecun-has-zero-concerns-about-human-extinction-says-anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-is-deuded/)). Neither side has an observation of the future to offer; each cites scenarios, trend lines and arguments about what present models imply.

## How many are afraid

The Pew Research Center asked adults in 25 countries "Overall, how would you say the increased use of artificial intelligence in daily life makes you feel?"; in Brazil 48% said more concerned than excited and 10% more excited ([Pew, topline, 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2025/10/pg_2025.10.15_ai_topline.pdf)), and the 25-country median of the more concerned was 34%: "In no country surveyed is the largest share more excited than concerned" ([Pew, 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2025/10/15/concern-and-excitement-about-ai/markdown)). It interviewed 28,333 adults in 2025, face to face in Brazil ([Pew, overview, 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2025/10/15/how-people-around-the-world-view-ai/markdown)). YouGov asked 18,238 American adults online in September 2026 about "the possibility that AI will cause the end of the human race on Earth": 50% were very or somewhat concerned, against 42% in November 2023 ([YouGov, 2026](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/55558-liberals-are-increasingly-likely-to-worry-about-ai-ending-humanity)); in 2023 it varied the question order so as not to "force respondents to take as a given that AI could end humanity" ([YouGov, 2023](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/45565-ai-nuclear-weapons-world-war-humanity-poll)). Ipsos, polling 23,532 adults online in 32 countries in 2026, found that "Excitement and nervousness about AI have reached near parity globally" ([Ipsos, 2026](https://www.ipsos.com/en-us/node/1076651)); in Brazil, about 1,000 people, 41% agreed with the statement that products and services using artificial intelligence make them nervous, down from 51% in 2023 ([Ipsos, country charts](https://www.ipsos.com/sites/default/files/ct/publication/documents/2026-06/Ipsos-AI-Monitor-2026-Country%20Charts.pdf); [Ipsos Brasil, 2026](https://www.ipsos.com/pt-br/node/1076841)). Datafolha's 2,004 face-to-face interviews in June 2026 found 48% of those aware of AI afraid it would replace their profession, down from 56% a year before, Folha reported ([Folha, 2026](https://www.bahianoticias.com.br/folha/noticia/398581-uso-de-ia-no-trabalho-cresce-e-medo-de-substituicao-cai-entre-os-brasileiros-diz-datafolha)). No Brazilian survey on the end of civilisation was found, and the Pew and Ipsos figures for Brazil, from different questions and methods, cannot be compared directly.

## Fear as a tool: what research measured

FUD names fear used as a sales weapon. The Jargon File credits the term to Gene Amdahl after he left International Business Machines (IBM), as "the fear, uncertainty, and doubt that IBM sales people instill in the minds of potential customers", a usage that later "has become generalized to refer to any kind of disinformation used as a competitive weapon" ([Jargon File, 2003](http://www.catb.org/jargon/html/F/FUD.html)); the phrase itself appears in a 1920 book ([Yarbrough, 1920](https://archive.org/stream/romancatholicchu00yarb/romancatholicchu00yarb_djvu.txt)).

Whether fear persuades has a split answer. Kim Witte's 1992 model tied the effect of a fear appeal to "the relationship between threat and efficacy" ([Witte, 1992](https://doi.org/10.1080/03637759209376276)). A meta-analysis of 127 papers and 27,372 participants by Melanie Tannenbaum and colleagues concluded that <Claim status="disputed">"fear appeals are effective at positively influencing attitude, intentions, and behaviors"</Claim>, noting that "the present results concern fear appeals rather than fear" ([Tannenbaum et al., 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5789790/)); Gerjo Kok and colleagues replied that the evidence "does not support positive effects of fear appeals" ([Kok et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1080/17437199.2017.1415767)). Both studied health and safety messages that recommend a protective act; neither measured suggestibility.

News is another matter. Analysing about 105,000 headline variants on Upworthy, Claire Robertson and colleagues found that "each additional negative word increased the click-through rate by 2.3%", but that "the effect of 'fear' was significantly related to article consumption, but in the opposite direction of our pre-registered hypotheses": fear words lowered clicks ([Robertson et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-023-01538-4)).

George Gerbner's cultivation theory held that heavy television viewers absorb "a sense of danger, of mistrust, of meanness in the world", which leaves them, in his words, "more likely to accept and even welcome repression if it promises to relieve their insecurities" ([Media Education Foundation, 2010](https://www.mediaed.org/transcripts/Mean-World-Syndrome-Transcript.pdf)). Paul Hirsch's reanalysis concluded that <Claim status="disputed">"the 'cultivation' hypothesis lacks empirical support"</Claim> ([Hirsch, 1980](https://doi.org/10.1177/009365028000700401)); a 2021 meta-analysis of 406 samples found "an overall effect size of .107" ([Hermann, Morgan and Shanahan, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/joc/jqab014)).

On uncertainty and conspiracy belief, Jan-Willem van Prooijen and Karen Douglas report that conspiracy content in 104,803 letters to American newspapers from 1890 to 2010 "fluctuated but did not increase over time" ([van Prooijen and Douglas, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/1750698017701615)). Douglas and colleagues found that exposure to conspiracy theories "appears to immediately suppress people's sense of autonomy and control" ([Douglas et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721417718261)), while a meta-analysis found <Claim status="disputed">"limited support for the hypothesis that conspiracy beliefs arise as a compensatory control"</Claim> ([Stojanov and Halberstadt, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.2690)). Michael Hogg proposes that self-uncertainty can draw people to groups "that have strong and directive leadership" ([Hogg, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721414540168)). How repetition makes a claim feel true is treated in [The human attack surface: why repetition works](/learn/the-human-attack-surface-why-repetition-works).

## The apocalypse that did not come, and the one that was prevented

The Year 2000 problem (Y2K) is cited by both sides. In February 1999 a Senate special committee wrote that "talk of the death of civilization, to borrow from Mark Twain, has been greatly exaggerated", while the problem was "very real" ([Senate committee, 1999](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CPRT-106sprt10/pdf/GPO-CPRT-106sprt10.pdf)). Its final report estimated that the country "spent an estimated $100 billion ($8.5 billion within the federal government alone)" and judged <Claim status="disputed">"that the level of effort was justified and the expenditure of funds was indeed necessary"</Claim>, though in countries that spent less per head "the low level of disruption internationally was somewhat surprising" ([Senate committee, 2000](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-Y1_3-PURL-LPS90964/pdf/GOVPUB-Y1_3-PURL-LPS90964.pdf)). The General Accounting Office (GAO) concluded that without that work "critical services could have been disrupted" ([GAO, 2000](https://www.gao.gov/assets/aimd-00-290.pdf)). In 2009 Farhad Manjoo wrote that the response "is remembered as an overreaction" and cited Leon Kappelman's estimate that "80 percent to 90 percent of the money spent on Y2K was right on target" ([Manjoo, 2009](https://www.slate.com/articles/technology/technology/features/2009/apocalypse_then/was_y2k_a_waste.html)). What would have happened without the work cannot be observed; the longer account is in [Y2K: what it costs to be right about a disaster that then does not happen](/learn/y2k-and-the-prevention-paradox).

Harold Camping's prediction left a dated record. The radio evangelist predicted the end of the world for 21 May 2011, then for 21 October 2011; in March 2012 he called the prediction "incorrect and sinful" ([Religion News Service, 2012](https://reporter.lcms.org/2012/harold-camping-may-21-prediction-was-incorrect-sinful/)) and wrote that "God may not tell his people the date when Christ will return" ([npr.org, 2012](https://www.npr.org/sections/thetwo-way/2012/03/09/148323322/doomsday-prophet-camping-says-predictions-were-incorrect-and-sinful)).

## "History is written by the victors"

The saying is usually credited to Winston Churchill, but Matthew Phelan, drawing on the quotation researcher Garson O'Toole, found "no concretely documented instance in which he's known to have uttered 'History is written by the victors.'" What Churchill did say, in January 1948, was that the past should be left to history, "especially as I propose to write that history myself". The earliest general form they found is Senator George Graham Vest's in 1891: "history is written by the victors and framed according to the prejudices and bias existing on their side" ([Phelan, 2019](https://slate.com/culture/2019/11/history-is-written-by-the-victors-quote-origin.html)).

## Who owns the feed

The largest services that carry such warnings are privately owned. Meta reported 3.64 billion daily active people across its apps on average for September 2026 ([Meta, 2026](https://static.poder360.com.br/uploads/2026/10/Meta-Meta-Reports-Third-Quarter-2026-Results.pdf)). The Digital Services Act (DSA) of the European Union (EU) designates as very large any platform with 45 million or more average monthly active recipients in the EU ([DSA, article 33](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065)); the European Commission's list, updated on 6 November 2026, gives as reported by the providers YouTube 416.6 million, Facebook and Instagram 259 million each, TikTok 135.9 million and X 115.1 million ([European Commission, 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/list-designated-vlops-and-vloses)).

At Twitter the rules changed with the owner. When Elon Musk closed his purchase of Twitter on 27 October 2022 he "fired several of the company's top executives" ([Semafor, 2022](https://www.semafor.com/article/10/27/2022/elon-musk-buys-twitter-for-44-bil-fires-top-executives)). In November the company stopped enforcing its coronavirus misinformation policy, and after an online poll Musk announced an account amnesty: "Amnesty begins next week. Vox Populi, Vox Dei" ([cnbc.com, 2022](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/11/29/twitter-stops-policing-covid-19-misinformation-under-ceo-elon-musk.html)). X calls its approach "Freedom of Speech, not Freedom of Reach", limiting the reach of violating posts ([X, 2023](https://blog.x.com/en/product/freedom-of-speech-not-reach-an-update-on-our-enforcement-philosophy)). A peer-reviewed study found <Claim status="reported" by="by Hickey et al.">"the weekly rate of hate speech being approximately 50% higher than the months preceding his purchase"</Claim>, though "this increase cannot be directly attributed to any policy at X"; it cites Musk's post that "Hate speech impressions (# of times tweet was viewed) continue to decline, despite significant user growth!" ([Hickey et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0313293)). No reply from X to the study was found.

In Brazil an owner's rules met a country's courts. On 30 August 2024 Justice Alexandre de Moraes of the Supremo Tribunal Federal (STF) suspended X nationwide until court orders were obeyed, fines paid and a legal representative named, citing <Claim status="established" by="by the STF">"o desaparecimento de seus representantes legais no Brasil para fins de intimação"</Claim>, the disappearance of its legal representatives ([STF, 2024](https://noticias.stf.jus.br/postsnoticias/stf-determina-suspensao-do-x-antigo-twitter-em-todo-o-territorio-nacional-2/)); the court's First Panel confirmed the order unanimously ([STF, 2024b](https://noticias.stf.jus.br/?p=93576)). X had said on 29 August that it expected suspension <Claim status="alleged" by="by X">"simply because we would not comply with his illegal orders to censor his political opponents"</Claim> ([cnnbrasil.com.br, 2024](https://www.cnnbrasil.com.br/politica/x-diz-que-vai-descumprir-ordem-do-ministro-alexandre-de-moraes/)). After X paid the fines ([Columbia Global Freedom of Expression](https://globalfreedomofexpression.columbia.edu/cases/the-case-of-the-x-ban-in-brazil/)), a decision of 8 October 2024 found every requirement for its return met ([STF, Pet 12.404](https://static.poder360.com.br/2024/10/decisao-desbloquar-X-Moraes.pdf)).

## Algorithms: what the word means, and what it hides

The word comes from a name. Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi, a scholar at the House of Wisdom in Baghdad, wrote a treatise on Hindu-Arabic numerals whose Latin translation, Algoritmi de numero Indorum, "gave rise to the word algorithm deriving from his name in the title" ([MacTutor](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Al-Khwarizmi/)). The Dictionary of Algorithms and Data Structures of the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) calls him a Persian author "who wrote a book with arithmetic rules dating from about 825 A.D." and defines an algorithm as "A computable set of steps to achieve a desired result" ([NIST](https://xlinux.nist.gov/dads/HTML/algorithm.html)).

The classic definition is narrow. Donald Knuth's five properties, as a Virginia Tech course states them, are finiteness ("The algorithm must always terminate after a finite number of steps"), definiteness, input, output and effectiveness ([McQuain, Virginia Tech](https://courses.cs.vt.edu/cs2104/Fall13/notes/T16_Algorithms.pdf)). The engineering view is in [What Is an Algorithm? A Working Primer](/learn/what-is-an-algorithm).

What people call "the algorithm" of a social network is something else, and European law has a name for it. The DSA defines a "recommender system" as "a fully or partially automated system used by an online platform to suggest in its online interface specific information to recipients of the service or prioritise that information", including by "determining the relative order or prominence of information displayed" (article 3(s)); the largest platforms must offer "at least one option for each of their recommender systems which is not based on profiling" (article 38) ([DSA](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065)).

The platforms describe their own systems. Meta writes that <Claim status="attributed" by="by Meta">"Our AI systems predict how valuable a piece of content might be to you, so we can show it to you sooner"</Claim>, adding "there's a limit to what we can disclose safely" ([Meta, 2023](https://about.fb.com/news/2023/06/how-ai-ranks-content-on-facebook-and-instagram/)); its terms state that "businesses and organizations, and other persons pay us to show you ads for their products and services" ([Meta, terms](https://www.facebook.com/terms)). YouTube draws on "a user's viewing behaviour, likes, dislikes, subscriptions and feedback" ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/howyoutubeworks/product-features/recommendations/)), and TikTok on "User interactions such as the videos you like or share, accounts you follow" ([TikTok, 2020](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you)).

X publishes its ranking code. When Twitter opened it in 2023, it described a network trained "to optimize for positive engagement (e.g. Likes, Retweets, and Replies)" ([Twitter, 2023](https://web.archive.org/web/20240109224831/https://blog.twitter.com/engineering/en_us/topics/open-source/2023/twitter-recommendation-algorithm)); the current repository says the ranking model <Claim status="attributed" by="by X">"reads the viewer's recent engagement history and predicts, for each post, how likely the viewer is to take each action on it"</Claim>, likes, replies, dwell time and blocks among them, and combines the predictions with weights held in the code; some files stay private because "people could use it to try to game the system". From August 2026 the feed ran a Brazil election filter "which removed posts from accounts reported to Brazil's Electoral Court for the 2026 election, unless the viewer explicitly follows the account"; it was removed on 26 October ([xai-org/x-algorithm](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/xai-org/x-algorithm/main/README.md)). No independent audit of the published code against the running feed was found.

Whether ranking for engagement does harm is disputed. Frances Haugen, a former Facebook product manager whose documents underlay The Wall Street Journal's Facebook Files ([Hao, 2021](https://www.technologyreview.com/2021/10/05/1036519/facebook-whistleblower-frances-haugen-algorithms/)), told the Senate in October 2021 that <Claim status="alleged" by="by Frances Haugen">"The result has been a system that amplifies division, extremism, and polarization"</Claim> and that "only Facebook gets to look under the hood" ([Haugen, 2021](https://www.commerce.senate.gov/services/files/FC8A558E-824E-4914-BEDB-3A7B1190BD49)). Meta's Nick Clegg called the series' central allegation <Claim status="denied" by="by Meta">"just plain false"</Claim> ([Meta, 2021](https://about.fb.com/news/2021/09/what-the-wall-street-journal-got-wrong/)). Experiments with Meta in 2020 found that a chronological feed did not significantly change polarisation, political knowledge or other key attitudes over the three months of the study ([Guess et al., 2023](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=37498999&rettype=abstract&retmode=text)), though Science later noted that "the default algorithm was in flux during the experiment" ([Thorp and Vinson, 2024](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adt2983)); a 2025 study that reranked X feeds without the platform's help shifted animosity towards the other party by more than 2 points on a 100-point scale ([Piccardi et al., 2025](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=41308156&rettype=abstract&retmode=text)).

Scholars of media treat these systems as more than code. Tarleton Gillespie writes that "Algorithms need not be software: in the broadest sense, they are encoded procedures for transforming input data into a desired output, based on specified calculations", and names among their dimensions "The promise of algorithmic objectivity", the way their technical character is presented "as an assurance of impartiality" ([Gillespie, 2014](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2014/01/Gillespie_2014_The-Relevance-of-Algorithms.pdf)). Nick Seaver, an anthropologist, argues that "algorithms are not technical rocks in a cultural stream, but are rather just more water" ([Seaver, 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2053951717738104)). Read together, the sources say what "the algorithm" of a feed is: a set of models, weights, filters and policies, retrained on behaviour and changed by its owners, sometimes under law. They also say what it is not: a single fixed formula, a neutral measure of what matters, or a system outsiders can fully inspect.

## What is known, what is disputed, what is unknown

Known: AI systems have found real vulnerabilities; agencies in the United States and the United Kingdom tell defenders to assume capable attackers; and in Brazil's official record the 2026 incidents ran on common weaknesses. Disputed: how many reported findings are severe, fixed or exploitable; whether extinction forecasts describe a likely future; whether fear persuades or provokes defence; whether engagement ranking harms public debate. Unknown: the text of the June directive, what the Y2K effort prevented, and how the running feeds differ from the code and explanations their owners publish. The series on psychological operations (PSYOP) runs through [Psychological operations: from the small lie to the campaign](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign), [PSYOP, propaganda and disinformation: the words](/learn/psyop-propaganda-and-disinformation-the-words), [The human attack surface: why repetition works](/learn/the-human-attack-surface-why-repetition-works) and [Scams at scale: why a tiny success rate pays](/learn/scams-at-scale-why-a-tiny-success-rate-pays).
