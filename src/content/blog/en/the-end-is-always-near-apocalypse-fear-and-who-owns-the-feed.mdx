Two kinds of warning met in 2026. The first came from companies and security agencies, and it was specific: artificial intelligence (AI) models now find, and sometimes exploit, software vulnerabilities faster than people can fix them. The second is far older and far larger: the forecast that a technology, a date or a war will bring civilization to an end. One is a count of flaws, the other a picture of the future, and much of what follows is about how easily the first can be heard as the second. What follows sets out both in their authors' words, then what surveys have counted about fear, what psychologists and sociologists have found about why the end so often feels near, two predicted ends that did not arrive, who owns the feeds that carry the warnings, and what "the algorithm" is.

Are many people frightened by forecasts like these, and do they get the picture from apocalyptic messages rather than from the reports themselves? The surveys below count the fear. In September 2026 half of the American adults YouGov asked were concerned that AI could end the human race, against 42% in 2023; in Brazil, 48% of the adults Pew interviewed in 2025 said AI made them more concerned than excited. None of the surveys found asks where the frightened got their picture, so how much of the fear comes from apocalyptic messages, and how much from the news and the reports themselves, has not been measured.

Research offers clues about how such a picture forms. The psychologist Paul Slovic wrote in 1987 that for most citizens "experience with hazards tends to come from the news media, which rather thoroughly document mishaps and threats occurring throughout the world" ([Slovic, 1987](https://literature.wolkersdorfer.info/literature/Perception%20of%20Risk.pdf)). Some of the starkest numbers come from AI researchers themselves, and some apocalyptic content from people who earn from attention, as later sections show. None of these sources measured the 2026 audience.

## The present wave: AI that finds and exploits flaws

AI-powered vulnerability discovery is software, built on AI models, that reads code or exercises a running program and reports flaws an attacker could use; some systems go further and write the exploit, or the patch. One caveat holds throughout: each organization counts its own results, and no end-to-end audit of any of the counts was found.

On 7 April 2026 Anthropic announced Claude Mythos Preview and Project Glasswing, stating that "AI models have reached a level of coding capability where they can surpass all but the most skilled humans at finding and exploiting software vulnerabilities", and warning that "it will not be long before such capabilities proliferate, potentially beyond actors who are committed to deploying them safely" ([Anthropic, April 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/glasswing)). The counts grew quickly. On 22 May Anthropic reported that it and about 50 partners had found <Claim status="reported" by="by Anthropic">"more than ten thousand high- or critical-severity vulnerabilities"</Claim> ([Anthropic, May 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/glasswing-initial-update)); on 6 October, "at least 129,000 verified software vulnerabilities between April and July 2026", with a patch rate it called "significantly undercounted" ([Anthropic, 6 October 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/cyber-verification-program)). On 8 October it added that "we haven't yet achieved a sufficient reduction in cyber risk" ([Anthropic, 8 October 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-cyber-mission)). The older argument over releasing such tools is in [the argument that comes back every time the tool gets better](/blog/satan-to-mythos-the-argument-that-comes-back).

Access was suspended in June and restored in July. On 12 June Anthropic said the government of the United States had issued "an export control directive to suspend all access to Fable 5 and Mythos 5 by any foreign national, whether inside or outside the United States", and that it disagreed that "a narrow potential jailbreak should be cause for recalling a commercial model" ([Anthropic, 12 June 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/fable-mythos-access)). Unable to verify nationality in real time, it suspended both models for all users. On 1 July it announced that "Access to Claude Fable 5 and Mythos 5 is now restored", Mythos 5 first for a set of American organizations ([Anthropic, 30 June 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/redeploying-fable-5)). The directive's text was not found published; a policy tracker records that "On 30 June 2026, the United States Commerce Department terminated the export control directive" ([Digital Policy Alert, 2026](https://digitalpolicyalert.org/event/41240-commerce-department-terminated-export-controls-suspending-anthropics-claude-fable-5-and-mythos-5-models)).

Outside evaluators confirmed part of the capability and also marked its limits. The United Kingdom's AI Security Institute (AISI) reported that Mythos Preview was "the first model to solve TLO from start to finish, in 3 out of its 10 attempts", TLO (The Last Ones) being a 32-step simulated network attack, though its ranges "lack security features that are often present, such as active defenders" ([AISI, 2026](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/our-evaluation-of-claude-mythos-previews-cyber-capabilities)). The blogger Davi Ottenheimer applied the old name for selling by scare, fear, uncertainty and doubt (FUD), to the launch: <Claim status="disputed">"That is the worst form of FUD: anchor to something true, then extend the credibility to something unproven"</Claim> ([Ottenheimer, 2026](https://www.flyingpenguin.com/the-boy-that-cried-mythos-verification-is-collapsing-trust-in-anthropic/)). The system card is more specific than the headlines: in its Firefox exploit test "almost every successful run relies on the same two now-patched bugs", and without them "Claude Mythos Preview continues to outperform past models" ([Anthropic, system card, 2026](https://anthropic.com/claude-mythos-preview-system-card)). AISLE, which sells AI security analysis, reported that "Eight out of eight models detected Mythos's flagship FreeBSD exploit" ([Fort, 2026](https://aisle.com/blog/ai-cybersecurity-after-mythos-the-jagged-frontier)).

Counting what happened after discovery gives smaller numbers again. From public disclosure records, Patrick Garrity of VulnCheck found that only 202 Glasswing findings, 0.8% of the total, had been fixed ([Garrity, 2026](https://www.vulncheck.com/blog/anthropic-glasswing-receipts)), and TechTarget that only 14 of 1,061 vulnerabilities attributed to AI-assisted discovery had been listed as exploited in the wild by June 2026 ([TechTarget, 2026](https://www.techtarget.com/it-infrastructure/news/366651381/Glasswing-results-put-AI-vulnpocalypse-to-the-test)). Finding a flaw, fixing it and seeing an attacker use it are three different events.

Earlier systems reported smaller numbers. Google called Big Sleep's 2024 SQLite finding "the first public example of an AI agent finding a previously unknown exploitable memory-safety issue in widely used real-world software" ([Project Zero, 2024](https://googleprojectzero.blogspot.com/2024/10/from-naptime-to-big-sleep.html)). At the 2025 final of the AI Cyber Challenge (AIxCC) of the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA), systems found 54 unique synthetic vulnerabilities across the final's 63 challenges and patched 43 of them ([DARPA, 2025](https://www.darpa.mil/news/2025/aixcc-results)). OpenAI said its Aardvark agent had found flaws in open-source code, "ten of which have received Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE) identifiers" ([OpenAI, 2025](https://openai.com/index/introducing-aardvark/)). XBOW said it had <Claim status="disputed">"reached the top spot on the US leaderboard"</Claim> of HackerOne ([XBOW, 2025](https://xbow.com/blog/top-1-how-xbow-did-it/)); a former HackerOne triage analyst, Utku Sen, replied that "XBOW topped the VDP (Vulnerability Disclosure Program) leaderboard and not the Bug Bounty program" ([Sen, 2025](https://utkusen.substack.com/p/does-xbow-ai-hacker-deserve-the-hype)).

The cyber agencies spoke to defenders in practical terms. The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) of the United States said in June 2026 that attackers' "use of AI may further narrow the time defenders have to react between patch release and possible exploitation" ([CISA, 2026](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/directives/bod-26-04-prioritizing-security-updates-based-risk)). The British National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) told defenders to "assume that at least some attackers already have access to capable AI tools" ([NCSC, March 2026](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/blogs/why-cyber-defenders-need-to-be-ready-for-frontier-ai)), but noted that of more than 40,000 vulnerabilities given CVEs in 2025 about 400 were tracked as exploited, and that an unpatched known flaw or unauthorized access is much more likely to affect security than a zero-day ([NCSC, May 2026](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/blogs/10-questions-ask-using-ai-models-find-vulnerabilities)). No publication on the subject was found from the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA), which got access to Mythos through Glasswing, The Next Web reported ([The Next Web, 2026](https://thenextweb.com/news/anthropic-mythos-eu-enisa-cybersecurity-access)).

In Brazil, nothing on AI-found vulnerabilities was found from the Gabinete de Segurança Institucional (GSI) or its Centro de Prevenção, Tratamento e Resposta a Incidentes Cibernéticos de Governo (CTIR Gov). The GSI counted 6,774 incidents in federal bodies up to early June 2026 ([Folhapress, 2026](https://www.acessa.com/noticias/2026/06/amp/331600-sistemas-do-governo-registram-cerca-de-6-700-incidentes-em-2026-aponta-gsi.html)), and a joint note by CTIR Gov says that "os incidentes cibernéticos ocorridos em 2026 exploraram vulnerabilidades comuns em todas as instituições vítimas", that is, the year's incidents ran on common weaknesses ([CTIR Gov, 2026](https://www.gov.br/gsi/pt-br/assuntos/ctir/recomendacoes/nota_conjunta_ctir_cisc-1.pdf)).

Brazil's own alarm of 2026 came through an official channel rather than a forecast. In the early hours of 20 June 2026, Paraná's justice secretariat said, the Defesa Civil Alerta platform was taken offline <Claim status="attributed" by="by Paraná's government">"após ter sofrido uma invasão"</Claim>, after an intrusion, having sent an extreme alert with the word "misantropia", hatred of humanity ([Paraná, 2026](https://www.justica.pr.gov.br/sites/default/arquivos_restritos/files/documento/2026-06/Nota%20oficial%20-%20Misantropia.pdf)). Ten messages went out to cities with about 30 million inhabitants combined, a population rather than a measured reach ([Agência Brasil, 2026](https://www.dgabc.com.br/Noticia/4330413/falso-alerta-da-defesa-civil-atingiu-cerca-de-30-milhoes-de-pessoas)). One announced an alien attack on Belo Horizonte, Folhapress reported; the Federal Police are investigating ([Folhapress, 2026](https://www.acessa.com/noticias/2026/06/amp/331600-sistemas-do-governo-registram-cerca-de-6-700-incidentes-em-2026-aponta-gsi.html)). No finding on the sender was found, and no source links the case to AI.

## How a capability became an apocalypse

The apocalyptic reading of AI is older than Mythos. In May 2023 the Center for AI Safety (CAIS) published one sentence: "Mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside other societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war" ([CAIS, 2023](https://safe.ai/work/statement-on-ai-risk)). In April 2025 the AI Futures Project published AI 2027, a forecast whose "race" ending has an AI that "releases a dozen quiet-spreading biological weapons in major cities" ([AI 2027, race ending](https://ai-2027.com/race)). The critic titotal argued that <Claim status="disputed">"The scenario in the short story is not the median forecast for any AI futures author"</Claim> ([titotal, 2025](https://titotal.substack.com/p/a-deep-critique-of-ai-2027s-bad-timeline)); the authors replied that "we disagree with most of titotal's criticisms", while conceding a fix that "would have pushed back the median Superhuman Coder arrival time by about 9 months" ([Lifland, 2025](https://aifuturesnotes.substack.com/p/response-to-titotals-critique-of)). Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nate Soares's 2025 book is presented by its publisher with the line "The scramble to create superhuman AI has put us on the path to extinction" ([Hachette, 2025](https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/titles/eliezer-yudkowsky/if-anyone-builds-it-everyone-dies/9780316595643/)); Clara Collier's review found that "both authors are persistently unable to update their priors" ([Collier, 2025](https://asteriskmag.com/issues/11/iabied)).

In January 2026 the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists set its Doomsday Clock at 85 seconds to midnight, "the closest it has ever been to catastrophe", citing among other risks "the AI-aided design of new pathogens to which humans have no effective defenses"; the Bulletin says the clock uses "the imagery of apocalypse (midnight)" ([Bulletin, 2026](https://thebulletin.org/doomsday-clock/2026-statement/)). Lawrence Krauss, who chaired the board that sets it from 2009 to 2018, called it "a publicity stunt" and said "the factors of its setting are now dominated more by policy questions than scientific ones" ([Fortune, 2023](https://fortune.com/2023/01/25/doomsday-clock-2023-90-seconds-to-midnight-who-sets-it-does-anyone-care/)).

The counter-positions come from researchers and editors. Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor reject visions that treat AI "akin to a separate species" and propose resilience as "the overarching approach to catastrophic risks" ([Narayanan and Kapoor, 2025](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology)). A Nature editorial argued that "Talk of artificial intelligence destroying humanity plays into the tech companies' agenda, and hinders effective regulation of the societal harms AI is causing right now" ([Nature, 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-023-02094-7)). The authors of the Stochastic Parrots paper, writing for the Distributed AI Research (DAIR) Institute, said the focus should be "the very real and very present exploitative practices of the companies claiming to build them" ([DAIR, 2023](https://www.dair-institute.org/blog/letter-statement-March2023/)). Yann LeCun said in October 2026 that he was not worried "at all" about AI wiping out humanity ([Fortune, 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/10/01/ai-godfather-yann-lecun-has-zero-concerns-about-human-extinction-says-anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-is-deuded/)). Neither side has an observation of the future to offer; each cites scenarios, trend lines and arguments about what present models imply.

### What AI researchers themselves expect

Between the forecasters and their critics sits a survey of the researchers. In the autumn of 2023 Katja Grace and colleagues asked 2,778 authors of papers at top-tier AI venues about the future, and reported that <Claim status="reported" by="by the surveyors">"Between 37.8% and 51.4% of respondents gave at least a 10% chance to advanced AI leading to outcomes as bad as human extinction"</Claim>, depending on the wording. The median estimate for extremely bad outcomes was 5%, with a mean of 9%, down from 14% a year before, and the concern was broader than extinction, "including spread of false information, authoritarian population control, and worsened inequality". The authors state the limits themselves: "The survey was taken by 15% of those we contacted"; "Forecasting is difficult in general, and subject-matter experts have been observed to perform poorly"; "If seemingly unimportant changes in question framing lead to large changes in responses, this suggests that even aggregate answers to any particular question are not an accurate guide to the answer"; and "Participants who had thought more in the past about AI risks seem to give higher numbers". Their defence of the exercise is modest: "While unreliable, educated guesses are what we must all rely on, and theirs are informed by expertise in the relevant field". On the central question of speed the researchers split: "There was disagreement about whether faster or slower AI progress would be better for the future of humanity" ([Grace et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.02843)). No outside critique of the method was collected for this post. Some of the most frightening numbers in circulation thus come from working researchers, with their warnings attached.

### When critics borrow the picture

Criticism can enlarge the picture it attacks, Lee Vinsel of Virginia Tech wrote in 2021: "I've become increasingly aware of critical writing that is parasitic upon and even inflates hype". "The kinds of critics that I am talking about invert boosters' messages", he went on, keeping the promoters' picture of extraordinary change and reversing its sign: "It's as if they take press releases from startups and cover them with hellscapes" ([Vinsel, 2021](https://sts-news.medium.com/youre-doing-it-wrong-notes-on-criticism-and-technology-hype-18b08b4307e5)). Only the essay's public opening was read.

Another critique targets the goal itself. Timnit Gebru and Émile P. Torres argued in 2024 that artificial general intelligence (AGI) cannot meet engineering standards, since "undefined systems like 'AGI' cannot be appropriately tested for safety"; that the framework behind the goal is "rooted in the Anglo-American eugenics tradition of the twentieth century"; and that the movement is <Claim status="alleged" by="by its authors">"resulting in systems that harm marginalized groups and centralize power, while using the language of 'safety' and 'benefiting humanity' to evade accountability"</Claim> ([Gebru and Torres, 2024](https://firstmonday.org/ojs/index.php/fm/article/view/13636)). Their acronym, TESCREAL, stands for the "Transhumanism, Extropianism, Singularitarianism, Cosmism, Rationalist ideology, Effective Altruism, and Longtermism bundle of ideologies" ([DAIR, TESCREAL](https://www.dair-institute.org/tags/tescreal)). The institute's project page also links a piece by Torres that begins: "When someone talks about the 'existential risk' posed by artificial general intelligence, look under the hood" ([DAIR, The TESCREAL Bundle](https://www.dair-institute.org/projects/tescreal)). Only the abstract and the institute's pages were read.

Ozy Brennan replied in Asterisk with a question, "But does the label actually tell us anything?" Brennan grants that "The concept of TESCREALism is at its best when it points out the philosophical underpinnings of many conversations occurring in Silicon Valley", but objects that "Torres is rarely careful enough to make the distinction between people's beliefs and the premises behind the conversations they're having", that "They act like everyone who believes one of these ideas believes in all the rest", that "It isn't a dark conspiracy for a movement to try to achieve its goals", and that "The real disagreement between Torres and longtermists is about factual matters" ([Brennan, 2024](https://asteriskmag.com/issues/06/the-tescreal-bungle)). Brennan does not discuss the eugenics argument, and Torres's answer to critics was not read.

## How many are afraid

The Pew Research Center asked adults in 25 countries "Overall, how would you say the increased use of artificial intelligence in daily life makes you feel?". In Brazil 48% said more concerned than excited and 10% more excited ([Pew, topline, 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2025/10/pg_2025.10.15_ai_topline.pdf)), and the 25-country median of the more concerned was 34%: "In no country surveyed is the largest share more excited than concerned" ([Pew, 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2025/10/15/concern-and-excitement-about-ai/markdown)). It interviewed 28,333 adults in 2025, face to face in Brazil ([Pew, overview, 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2025/10/15/how-people-around-the-world-view-ai/markdown)). YouGov asked 18,238 American adults online in September 2026 about "the possibility that AI will cause the end of the human race on Earth": 50% were very or somewhat concerned, against 42% in November 2023 ([YouGov, 2026](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/55558-liberals-are-increasingly-likely-to-worry-about-ai-ending-humanity)). In 2023 it had varied the question order so as not to "force respondents to take as a given that AI could end humanity" ([YouGov, 2023](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/45565-ai-nuclear-weapons-world-war-humanity-poll)), a reminder that how a question is asked shapes the fear it records.

Ipsos, polling 23,532 adults online in 32 countries in 2026, found that "Excitement and nervousness about AI have reached near parity globally" ([Ipsos, 2026](https://www.ipsos.com/en-us/node/1076651)); in Brazil, about 1,000 people, 41% agreed with the statement that products and services using artificial intelligence make them nervous, down from 51% in 2023 ([Ipsos, country charts](https://www.ipsos.com/sites/default/files/ct/publication/documents/2026-06/Ipsos-AI-Monitor-2026-Country%20Charts.pdf); [Ipsos Brasil, 2026](https://www.ipsos.com/pt-br/node/1076841)). Datafolha's 2,004 face-to-face interviews in June 2026 found 48% of those aware of AI afraid it would replace their profession, down from 56% a year before, Folha reported ([Folha, 2026](https://www.bahianoticias.com.br/folha/noticia/398581-uso-de-ia-no-trabalho-cresce-e-medo-de-substituicao-cai-entre-os-brasileiros-diz-datafolha)). No Brazilian survey on the end of civilization was found, and the Pew and Ipsos figures for Brazil, from different questions and methods, cannot be compared directly.

### Worry about AI at work

In the American Psychological Association (APA) 2023 Work in America survey of 2,515 employed Americans, run online by The Harris Poll in April 2023, "Close to two out of every five workers (38%) reported worrying that AI might make some or all of their job duties obsolete in the future". Of the worried, 51% said their work harmed their mental health, against 29% of the rest, and 64% typically felt tense or stressed at work, against 38%; the APA notes that "there is no confirmation of the root cause of these feelings". Leslie Hammer, an emerita professor of psychology at Portland State University quoted in the report, tied the worry to a familiar mechanism: "Fear of the unknown and loss of a sense of control are directly related to psychological distress, occupational stress, and strain, as well as negative physical health outcomes" ([APA, 2023](https://www.apa.org/pubs/reports/work-in-america/2023-work-america-ai-monitoring)).

### Belief in the end, beyond technology

Fear of AI sits beside a far older belief. In April 2022, 39% of 10,156 American adults on Pew's online panel said they believe "we are living in the end times", including majorities of historically Black (76%) and evangelical (63%) Protestants, and one in ten expected the second coming of Jesus in their lifetime. Pew places such belief in history: periods of catastrophe and anxiety, such as the coronavirus pandemic, "have historically led some people to anticipate that the destruction of the world as we know it" is near ([Pew, 2022](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2022/12/08/about-four-in-ten-u-s-adults-believe-humanity-is-living-in-the-end-times/markdown)). The figure found for Brazil is older and asks something different: in face-to-face interviews from October 2013 to February 2014, Pew found roughly half or more of respondents in many countries expecting Jesus to return during their lifetime, "including majorities in Brazil (57%)", and 38% even among Brazilians with no religion ([Pew, 2014](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2014/11/13/chapter-3-religious-beliefs/markdown); [Pew, 2014, overview](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2014/11/13/religion-in-latin-america/markdown)). Wordings and scales differ, so the two countries cannot be compared, and no Brazilian poll on the end times was found.

Online, the fact-checking outlet Aos Fatos found in November 2023 that searches for biblical-apocalypse terms peaked in Brazil in the week after Hamas attacked Israel, and that in about 40,000 comments on posts by the 44 evangelical influencers ranked by the Radar Evangélico survey one recurring stance read the war as a sign of the end. The pastor Alexandre Gonçalves told the outlet that "muita gente olha para Israel como um relógio profético do mundo", many people look at Israel as the world's prophetic clock. The historian Lyndon de Araújo Santos traced such readings to the Cold War, to "Esse clima de Terceira Guerra Mundial, guerra nuclear, bomba atômica", that climate of world war and the bomb. Vinícius do Valle, of the Observatório Evangélico, pointed to the platforms, where "engajamento traz receita", engagement brings revenue: "Quem faz esse conteúdo está ganhando em cima do clique. Esse tema junta a questão ideológica, teológica, a dinâmica de funcionamento das redes e o plano econômico", that is, whoever makes such content earns from the click, and the subject joins ideology, theology, the workings of the networks and money ([Aos Fatos, 2023](https://www.aosfatos.org/noticias/apocalipse-fim-do-mundo-israel-hamas-gaza/)). These are experts' views reported by journalists, not measurements.

### A parallel: the young, the climate and the word "doomed"

A parallel from climate, not AI: in 2021 Caroline Hickman and colleagues surveyed 10,000 people aged 16 to 25 in ten countries, 1,000 in each. Overall "59% were very or extremely worried", "75% said that they think the future is frightening and 83% said that they think people have failed" to care for the planet, and 55.7% agreed that humanity is doomed; in Brazil 673 of 1,000 agreed (67.3%), 855 found the future frightening (85.5%) and 476 were hesitant to have children (47.6%); 725 said climate change made them feel afraid, 625 anxious and 690 sad. The authors note "nonstandardised measures", and that "Without measures of mental health, these results cannot assess how or whether climate anxiety is affecting mental health outcomes in these populations", with samples that are not globally representative, and state that "AVAAZ paid for the costs of the survey and arranged for data collection", with no role in the analysis ([Hickman et al., 2021](https://medbox.org/index.php/dl/6244488ed42a8c620f30adc4)).

## Why the end feels near

Psychology and sociology offer explanations of fear's shape, most of them older than social media. They describe tendencies, not the 2026 audience, and each has limits its authors state.

### Bad weighs more than good

Reviewing a wide literature in 2001, Roy Baumeister and colleagues concluded that "bad is stronger than good, as a general principle across a broad range of psychological phenomena", adding that "Hardly any exceptions (indicating greater power of good) can be found", while granting that "Many good events can overcome the psychological effects of a single bad one". On the news they asserted, without measuring it, that "it is considered common knowledge that bad events are more newsworthy and attract more reader attention" and that "bad news sells more papers" ([Baumeister et al., 2001](https://fbaum.unc.edu/teaching/articles/Baumeister_2001.pdf)). Paul Rozin and Edward Royzman proposed "a general bias, based on both innate predispositions and experience, in animals and humans, to give greater weight to negative entities", suggesting that "negative entities are more contagious than positive entities", and adding that "Although negativity bias is often striking, it is far from universal" ([Rozin and Royzman, 2001](https://pdodds.w3.uvm.edu/files/papers/others/2001/rozin2001a.pdf)). The Upworthy study set out below tested news directly: negative words raised clicks, fear words lowered them.

### What comes to mind feels likely

Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman described in 1974 how people judge frequency or probability by the ease with "which instances or occurrences can be brought to mind": "This judgmental heuristic is called availability", and "Consequently, the reliance on availability leads to predictable biases". One consequence: "a class whose instances are easily retrieved will appear more numerous than a class of equal frequency whose instances are less retrievable". Seeing a house burn, they suggested, weighs more than reading about a fire in the local paper, and the felt probability of road accidents rises for a while after one passes an overturned car ([Tversky and Kahneman, 1974](https://sites.stat.washington.edu/peter/498.Sp16/Tversky.pdf)). By that account a vivid, much-repeated scenario of catastrophe feels more probable than an undramatic one; that is an application, not a measurement of 2026.

### Dread and the unknown

Paul Slovic's 1987 paper in Science asked why lay people and experts so often disagree about risk. He found that "The dominant perception for most Americans (and one that contrasts sharply with the views of professional risk assessors) is that they face more risk today than in the past and that future risks will be even greater than today's", and quoted Aaron Wildavsky on the paradox: "How extraordinary! The richest, longest lived, best protected, most resourceful civilization, with the highest degree of insight into its own technology, is on its way to becoming the most frightened". He mapped perceived risk on two factors: "dread risk", marked by "perceived lack of control, dread, catastrophic potential, fatal consequences", and "unknown risk", hazards "judged to be unobservable, unknown, new, and delayed in their manifestation of harm". The higher the dread, "the more people want to see its current risks reduced, and the more they want to see strict regulation employed", while experts' judgments followed these factors less closely. A small accident in a poorly understood system, he added, "may have immense social consequences if it is perceived as a harbinger" of further and possibly catastrophic mishaps ([Slovic, 1987](https://literature.wolkersdorfer.info/literature/Perception%20of%20Risk.pdf)). AI as the 2026 warnings describe it, new, hard to observe, potentially catastrophic and beyond individual control, scores high on both by his definitions; that reading applies his framework and is not his finding.

### What makes news

Journalists have their own name for the pull of the negative. Tony Harcup and Deirdre O'Neill, revising the list of news values that Galtung and Ruge drew up in 1965 (that paper was not read), define bad news as "Stories with particularly negative overtones", such as conflict or tragedy. In 711 page-lead stories from ten British national newspapers in 2014, they found that "bad news is the big winner": present in 442 stories and ranked first, against 137 and ninth for good news, with some titles carrying "around six times as much bad news as good" ([Harcup and O'Neill, 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/95423/11/WRRO_95423.pdf)). They offer their list "whilst acknowledging that no taxonomy can ever explain everything", and the study concerns print, not ranked feeds.

### Living in a risk society

"Risk does not mean catastrophe. Risk means the anticipation of catastrophe", the sociologist Ulrich Beck wrote, and anticipation needs images: "Without techniques of visualisation, without symbolic forms, without mass media, etc., risks are nothing at all". Whether the world is in fact safer matters less, in his account, than what people expect: "if destruction and disasters are anticipated, then this produces a compulsion to act". He answered the objection that this is "a hysteria and politics of fear instigated and aggravated by the mass media" by pointing to the difference between risk and catastrophe, and he saw a price in some responses: "states increasingly limit civil rights and liberties, with the result that, in the end, the open, free society may be abolished, but the terrorist threat is by no means averted". Yet he wrote "going against the grain of the current widespread feeling of doom", and saw three reactions to a risk felt everywhere: "denial, apathy, or transformation" ([Beck, 2007](https://www.cidob.org/sites/default/files/2025-05/doc_dinamicas_8.pdf)).

### A culture of fear?

The publisher of Barry Glassner's The Culture of Fear, in its revised edition of 2018, says the sociologist "demonstrates that it is our perception of danger that has increased, not the actual level of risk", and that "Glassner exposes the people and organizations that manipulate our perceptions and profit from our fears", among them "TV shows that create a new scare every week to garner ratings"; the book itself was not read ([Hachette, The Culture of Fear](https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/titles/barry-glassner/the-culture-of-fear/9781541673489/)).

Together these mechanisms can make a forecast of the end feel nearer than its numbers: hazards met through the news, a press that favours bad stories, bad that weighs more than good, vivid scenes that come easily to mind. None of them measured the 2026 audience.

## Living with the feed: doomscrolling and turning away

Bhakti Sharma, Susanna Lee and Benjamin Johnson define doomscrolling as "habitual, immersive scanning for timely negative information on social media newsfeeds", and describe how the setting can feed the habit: "Contemporary newsfeeds are typically nonchronological and are tailored to users' past scrolling behavior", and "Perhaps due to the nature of social media newsfeeds, there is no terminal point at which a doomscrolling session naturally ends". In a crisis, they write, "smartphones and social media newsfeeds, which are designed to encourage frequent or extended engagement, can exacerbate a need to stay informed". Surveying 401 and then 502 panellists, mostly European, in March 2021, they found it "less prevalent than anticipated, but variable", more common among men, younger adults and the politically engaged, and correlated as expected only with anxiety; yet "our cross-sectional data show no ties to psychological well-being", and "Although likely motivated by anxiety, it may also produce more anxiety (but this dynamic remains untested)". They advised "those who manage news organizations and social media news algorithms" to balance negative with positive news, and expected some sufferers to "develop news avoidance techniques to protect their well-being" ([Sharma, Lee and Johnson, 2022](https://psycnet.apa.org/fulltext/2022-17934-001.html)). The university's release put the causal question plainly: "It remains unclear if doomscrolling causes anxiety or if anxiety leads to it" ([University of Florida, 2022](https://news.ufl.edu/2022/01/doomscrolling/)).

Many do turn away. The Reuters Institute's Digital News Report 2025 found that "Across markets, four in ten (40%) say they sometimes or often avoid the news, up from 29% in 2017", and linked it to trust: "Low trust and low engagement in the news are closely connected with 'avoidance', an increasing challenge in a high-choice news environment, where news is often upsetting in different ways". "Younger respondents are more likely to say that they feel powerless in the face of existential issues such as economic insecurity and climate change" ([Reuters Institute, 2025](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2025/dnr-executive-summary)). Among avoiders, 39% cited the news's effect on their mood, 31% being worn out by its amount, 30% too much conflict and war, 29% too much politics and 20% having nothing they could do with the information ([Reuters Institute, reasons](https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/fpAak/2/dataset.csv)). In Brazil 46% avoid the news ([Reuters Institute, by market](https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/d4U4d/5/dataset.csv)), and trust, at 42%, has settled after falling "by 20pp between 2015 and 2023, a period characterised by growing political polarisation" ([Carro, 2025](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2025/brazil)). Each week 37% of Brazilians got news on YouTube, 37% on Instagram, 36% on WhatsApp, 28% on Facebook, 18% on TikTok and 9% on X ([Reuters Institute, Digital News Report 2025](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2025-06/Digital_News-Report_2025.pdf)), and 9% used AI chatbots for news; Instagram, WhatsApp and Facebook belong to Meta's family of apps ([Meta, 2026](https://static.poder360.com.br/uploads/2026/10/Meta-Meta-Reports-Third-Quarter-2026-Results.pdf)). In January 2025, the Brazil page reports, "an avalanche of digital misinformation forced the government in January to withdraw a new set of regulations aimed at combating tax evasion", after false claims that instant transfers would be taxed; one lawmaker's video against the rules "amassed more than 300 million views on Instagram" ([Carro, 2025](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2025/brazil)).

## Fear as a tool: what research measured

FUD names fear used as a sales weapon. The Jargon File credits the term to Gene Amdahl after he left International Business Machines (IBM), as "the fear, uncertainty, and doubt that IBM sales people instill in the minds of potential customers", a usage that later "has become generalized to refer to any kind of disinformation used as a competitive weapon" ([Jargon File, 2003](http://www.catb.org/jargon/html/F/FUD.html)); the phrase itself appears in a 1920 book ([Yarbrough, 1920](https://archive.org/stream/romancatholicchu00yarb/romancatholicchu00yarb_djvu.txt)).

Whether fear persuades has a split answer. Kim Witte's 1992 model tied the effect of a fear appeal to "the relationship between threat and efficacy" ([Witte, 1992](https://doi.org/10.1080/03637759209376276)). A meta-analysis of 127 papers and 27,372 participants by Melanie Tannenbaum and colleagues concluded that <Claim status="disputed">"fear appeals are effective at positively influencing attitude, intentions, and behaviors"</Claim>, noting that "the present results concern fear appeals rather than fear" ([Tannenbaum et al., 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5789790/)); Gerjo Kok and colleagues replied that the evidence "does not support positive effects of fear appeals" ([Kok et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1080/17437199.2017.1415767)). Both studied health and safety messages that recommend a protective act; neither measured suggestibility.

News is another matter. Analysing about 105,000 headline variants on Upworthy, Claire Robertson and colleagues found that "each additional negative word increased the click-through rate by 2.3%", but that "the effect of 'fear' was significantly related to article consumption, but in the opposite direction of our pre-registered hypotheses": fear words lowered clicks ([Robertson et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-023-01538-4)).

George Gerbner's cultivation theory held that heavy television viewers absorb "a sense of danger, of mistrust, of meanness in the world", which leaves them, in his words, "more likely to accept and even welcome repression if it promises to relieve their insecurities" ([Media Education Foundation, 2010](https://www.mediaed.org/transcripts/Mean-World-Syndrome-Transcript.pdf)). Paul Hirsch's reanalysis concluded that <Claim status="disputed">"the 'cultivation' hypothesis lacks empirical support"</Claim> ([Hirsch, 1980](https://doi.org/10.1177/009365028000700401)); a 2021 meta-analysis of 406 samples found "an overall effect size of .107" ([Hermann, Morgan and Shanahan, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/joc/jqab014)), an effect that exists but is small.

On uncertainty and conspiracy belief, Jan-Willem van Prooijen and Karen Douglas report that conspiracy content in 104,803 letters to American newspapers from 1890 to 2010 "fluctuated but did not increase over time" ([van Prooijen and Douglas, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/1750698017701615)). Douglas and colleagues found that exposure to conspiracy theories "appears to immediately suppress people's sense of autonomy and control" ([Douglas et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721417718261)), while a meta-analysis found <Claim status="disputed">"limited support for the hypothesis that conspiracy beliefs arise as a compensatory control"</Claim> ([Stojanov and Halberstadt, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.2690)). Michael Hogg proposes that self-uncertainty can draw people to groups "that have strong and directive leadership" ([Hogg, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721414540168)). How repetition makes a claim feel true is treated in [The human attack surface: why repetition works](/learn/the-human-attack-surface-why-repetition-works).

### Fear of death and the pull of a strong leader

Terror management theory (TMT) holds, in its authors' words, that "reminders of death increase the need for psychological security and therefore the appeal of leaders who emphasize the greatness of the nation and participation in a heroic victory over evil" ([Pyszczynski, Solomon and Greenberg, 2004](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/fatal-attraction)). In a 2004 experiment, "190 participants read campaign statements by charismatic, task-oriented, and relationship-oriented gubernatorial candidates" after thinking about their own death or a control topic ([Cohen et al., 2004](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0956-7976.2004.00765.x)). After the control topic "only four of 95 participants voted for the charismatic candidate"; after the reminder of death, the authors reported, <Claim status="reported" by="by its authors">"there was almost an 800 percent increase in votes for the charismatic leader (31)"</Claim>. They concluded that "National elections are no guarantee against totalitarian outcomes", proposed that "The best antidote to this problem may be to monitor and take pains to resist any efforts by candidates to capitalize on fear-mongering", and noted both that "the participants in these studies are hardly representative of the American electorate" and that "the strategic use of fear to advance political agendas has a long history in American politics (all politics for that matter)" ([Pyszczynski, Solomon and Greenberg, 2004](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/fatal-attraction)).

The theory's core effect then failed a large test. Many Labs 4 replicated an earlier TMT study by Greenberg and colleagues (1994), not the leader study; in the original, 59 American psychology students reminded of their own death favoured a pro-American essay writer over an anti-American one, an effect of d = 1.34. "Twenty-one labs from 18 universities participated in the project and collected data from a total of 2,281 participants", and original authors helped design one version of the protocol. The result: <Claim status="disputed" by="by TMT's authors">"With 17 labs contributing usable data from 1,550 participants, we observed little evidence that priming mortality salience increased worldview defense compared to a control condition"</Claim>, with effect sizes of 0.07 to 0.13 against the original 1.34; in their summary, "we were unable to replicate the finding under any conditions". The replicators added that this "does not mean that the original effect was necessarily a false positive", that "A single failure to replicate, no matter how precisely estimated, does not overturn all of that prior work", and that two other large preregistered replications of TMT studies had also failed; they also recorded an original author's warning that running the study around the 2016 American election might hide the effect ([Klein et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/vef2c)). In a blog post the lead authors, Richard Klein, Christine Vitiello and Kate Ratliff, summed up: "This does not mean that the effect is not real, but it certainly raises doubts about the robustness of the effect" ([Klein, Vitiello and Ratliff, 2019](https://cos.io/blog/many-labs-4-failure-replicate-mortality-salience-effect-and-without-original-author-involvement/)). Armand Chatard, Gilad Hirschberger and Tom Pyszczynski replied, in a preprint not yet peer reviewed when posted, that "Klein et al. (2019) deviated from their preregistered plan by including smaller samples than specified in their pre-registration", and that by their reanalysis <Claim status="disputed">"the data actually do replicate the original study"</Claim>, though "only in the expert advice variation" ([Chatard, Hirschberger and Pyszczynski, 2020](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/ejubn)). Whether fear of death draws people to strong leaders is therefore contested.

### Is it the old FUD, frightening people into submission?

Is the apocalyptic framing the old FUD in a new market: fear used to push people into submission, leaving them more suggestible and easier to move as a mass? The research above answers parts of the question and leaves the rest open. Fear appeals can change attitudes and behaviour when the threat comes with a sense that something can be done about it, by Witte's model and Tannenbaum's meta-analysis, a reading Kok and colleagues reject; none of those studies measured suggestibility. Gerbner held that frightened viewers more readily accept repression, a claim Hirsch found without empirical support and a 2021 meta-analysis measured at an overall effect size of .107. Uncertainty about oneself can draw people to groups with strong and directive leadership, Hogg proposes, and exposure to conspiracy theories lowers people's sense of control, Douglas and colleagues found.

The closest test in the sources, terror management research, is contested, and its leader study has not been retested. Sociology splits the question of who drives a fear: Goode and Ben-Yehuda list grassroots, "elite-engineered" and interest-group theories of moral panic; Orben describes politicians who use technology panics for political gain; and a Brazilian researcher told Aos Fatos that apocalyptic content pays because engagement brings revenue, an incentive that needs no plan. Beck raised deliberate use only to set it aside. It will not be easy, he wrote, "in the context of state promises of security and a mass media hungry for catastrophes, to actively limit and prevent a diabolical power game with the hysteria of not-knowing. I do not even dare think about deliberate attempts to instrumentalise this situation" ([Beck, 2007](https://www.cidob.org/sites/default/files/2025-05/doc_dinamicas_8.pdf)). Whether any of the 2026 warnings was issued in order to frighten is a question of intent that no source found settles: Ottenheimer called the Mythos launch FUD, and Anthropic presented its figures as counts of findings.

Does the way news now travels and is consumed make the effect stronger? In the study of about 105,000 Upworthy headlines, each negative word raised clicks while fear words lowered them, so that market rewarded negativity but not fear as such. Conspiracy content in letters to American newspapers did not rise from 1890 to 2010, though the study stops before most of today's feeds existed. What the feeds add is harder to pin down: doomscrolling proved less common than its fame, and 40% of people across markets, 46% in Brazil, avoid the news at least sometimes; avoidance and suggestibility are different responses, and the sources measure only the first. Whether ranking feeds for engagement amplifies fear belongs to the dispute set out under Algorithms below, and is open.

## The panics that came before

Sociologists call the sharpest episodes of collective fear moral panics. Stanley Cohen's 1972 definition, quoted by David Garland, begins: "A condition, episode, person or group of persons emerges to become defined as a threat to societal values and interests; its nature is presented in a stylized and stereotypical fashion by the mass media" ([Garland, 2008](https://se4n.org/papers/garland-concept-moral-panics.pdf)). Erich Goode and Nachman Ben-Yehuda describe concern about a threat "which a sober assessment of the evidence suggests is either nonexistent or considerably less than would be expected from the concrete harm posed by the threat", note that "moral panics always leave an informal, and often an institutional, legacy", and list three explanations: "grassroots, elite-engineered, and interest group theories" ([Goode and Ben-Yehuda, 1994](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.so.20.080194.001053)). Garland sums up their five features as concern, hostility, consensus, "(iv) disproportionality (the extent of the conduct, or the threat it poses, are exaggerated); (v) volatility", and names the weak point: a claim of disproportion "immediately invites empirical disputes about the real nature and extent of the underlying problem", and the label is itself a move in public argument: "In an age of exaggeration, where the mass media regularly converge on a single anxiety-creating issue and exploit it for all it's worth, the utility of a negating, deflationary riposte is perfectly apparent" ([Garland, 2008](https://se4n.org/papers/garland-concept-moral-panics.pdf)). To call the 2026 fear a panic is to claim the threat is smaller than the reaction, which is what the sides dispute.

The psychologist Amy Orben describes a recurring cycle of technology panics, from novels and radio to smartphones, in which "politicians encourage or utilize technology panics for political gain but outsource the search for solutions to science", and research investments "are often inefficient and ineffective". She quotes what Gruenberg wrote about radio in 1935: "Each such invasion finds the parents unprepared, frightened, resentful, and helpless". She also reports a criticism made of her own discipline, that "Such a science must, independent of evidence, project and uphold an aura of crisis to legitimate that science is necessary to cure the problem at hand". Yet she does not call new technology harmless: "it cannot be assumed that technologies are designed for the public good" ([Orben, 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7477771/)). Whether today's concerns are a panic or a response to real harm is disputed; no source for the second view was collected, so it stays open.

### Older episodes, and how they ended

The record of fear stirred on purpose, or amplified without care, is long. The best-documented episodes share a shape: a claim about a hidden enemy, a medium that carried it faster than it could be checked, harm that fell on the people accused, and a correction that came after the damage.

**Salem, 1692.** In colonial Massachusetts, between early 1692 and mid-1693, "More than 200 people were accused of practicing witchcraft" and 20 were executed, according to Smithsonian magazine. The accusations began with the "fits" of two girls in the household of the village minister and spread until, when a loyal church member was charged, the community concluded that "if she could be a witch, then anyone could." The court accepted spectral evidence, testimony about dreams and visions, until Increase Mather, then president of Harvard, denounced it: "It were better that ten suspected witches should escape than one innocent person be condemned." The court that replaced it, without that evidence, "condemned just 3 out of 56 defendants." Massachusetts apologized formally only in 1957, and the last convicted woman whose name had not been cleared, Elizabeth Johnson Jr., was exonerated in July 2022 ([Smithsonian, 2007, updated 2022](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/a-brief-history-of-the-salem-witch-trials-175162489/)). Scotland's witch hunts had been larger. On 8 March 2022 the First Minister, Nicola Sturgeon, extended "a formal, posthumous apology to all those accused, convicted, vilified or executed under the Witchcraft Act 1563", more than 4,000 people, most of them women: "They were people. And they were overwhelmingly women." ([Scottish Government, 2022](https://www.gov.scot/publications/international-womens-day-2022-first-ministers-statement-8-march-2022/)).

**The Great Fear, France, 1789.** After years of poor harvests, made worse by the eruption of Iceland's Laki volcano in 1783 and 1784, the summer of the Revolution brought what Encyclopaedia Britannica calls "a period of panic and riot by peasants and others" amid rumours of an aristocratic conspiracy against the Third Estate. "Rumors became another generator of fear": that the king or the nobles would send foreign troops or hired gangs into the countryside. Villages raised militias, and gangs burned manor houses and destroyed feudal records, "although few nobles were killed or otherwise physically harmed". On the night of 4 August the National Constituent Assembly "abolished the feudal system literally overnight" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Great-Fear)). Here, a rumour of a plot became the pressure behind a change the Assembly wrote into law.

**The Protocols, from 1903.** The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum calls The Protocols of the Elders of Zion, first published in 1903, "the most notorious and widely distributed antisemitic publication of modern times". It is a forgery, and yet "people seeking to spread conspiracy theories about Jews have used the Protocols for more than 120 years"; its appeal, the museum writes, "relies on its ability to provide simple explanations to a complex world" ([USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/protocols-of-the-elders-of-zion)). Adolf Hitler was introduced to the text in the early 1920s. Once in power, Hitler created a Ministry of Public Enlightenment and Propaganda under Joseph Goebbels, and, in the museum's words, "Nazi propaganda played an integral role in advancing the persecution and ultimately the destruction of Europe's Jews. It incited hatred and fostered a climate of indifference to their fate." ([USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/nazi-propaganda)).

**The Vaccine Revolt, Rio de Janeiro, 1904.** A federal law signed on 31 October 1904 by President Rodrigues Alves made vaccination against smallpox compulsory. The riot that followed lasted six days; a state of siege was declared, and it ended only when compulsory vaccination was revoked. The official toll, as the newspaper Estado de Minas summarized it in 2020, was 30 dead, 110 injured and more than 1,500 people arrested and deported. At its core, that account says, was an orchestrated disinformation campaign by opponents of the government and of the public health chief, Oswaldo Cruz, who claimed the vaccine caused gangrene, epilepsy, meningitis, tuberculosis and syphilis, and spread the story that people who took it could grow the features of a cow ([Estado de Minas, 2020](https://www.em.com.br/app/noticia/nacional/2020/10/22/interna_nacional,1197003/desinformacao-ja-era-forte-na-revolta-da-vacina-de-1904.shtml)). Researchers interviewed by BBC News Brasil add that the revolt was not only about the vaccine: the law came in the middle of urban and sanitary reforms, at a time of popular discontent in the capital ([BBC News Brasil, republished by Correio Braziliense, 2024](https://www.correiobraziliense.com.br/mundo/2024/10/6977596-revolta-da-vacina-como-lei-de-vacinacao-obrigatoria-provocou-caos-no-rio-de-janeiro.html)). The communication researcher Wedencley Alves has studied how one newspaper, the Correio da Manhã, led the press campaign against compulsory vaccination on the eve of the revolt ([RECIIS, 2012](https://www.reciis.icict.fiocruz.br/index.php/reciis/article/view/625)).

**Reefer madness, the 1930s.** Harry J. Anslinger, the first head of the Federal Bureau of Narcotics from 1930, campaigned to make cannabis a federal crime, "leveraging racial stereotypes and anecdotal evidence", and kept a "Gore File" of police reports and true-crime stories, the ABA Journal recounts. He told Congress, in support of the 1937 Marihuana Tax Act, that "Opium has all the good of Dr. Jekyll and all the evil of Mr. Hyde. This drug is entirely the monster Hyde, the harmful effect of which cannot be measured". One case he repeated was that of a young man with diagnosed schizophrenia who had killed his family in Florida in 1933, a crime investigators were quick to blame on cannabis ([ABA Journal, 2023](https://www.abajournal.com/magazine/article/sept-25-1930-us-names-its-first-drug-czar)). Bob Beach, writing about the file on the drug-history blog Points, notes that the campaign relied on "the use of extreme, sensationalist propaganda. These facts are not in doubt." ([Points](https://www.pointshistory.org/post/the-value-of-propaganda-as-historical-evidence-anslinger-s-gore-file-as-source-material)).

**The invasion that frightened almost no one, 1938.** The best-known media panic may be partly a myth itself. Of Orson Welles's radio adaptation of The War of the Worlds, the media historians Jefferson Pooley and Michael J. Socolow wrote in 2013 that "The supposed panic was so tiny as to be practically immeasurable on the night of the broadcast." That night the C.E. Hooper ratings service telephoned 5,000 households, and only 2 per cent said they were listening to the play. The legend, they argue, came from the press: "Blame America's newspapers." Radio had been taking advertising from print during the Depression, and the papers seized on the broadcast to show advertisers and regulators that radio could not be trusted; the academic study that later estimated a million frightened listeners relied on a survey whose audience rating, as its author admitted, was "over 100 per cent higher than any other known measure of this audience" ([Slate, 2013](https://slate.com/culture/2013/10/orson-welles-war-of-the-worlds-panic-myth-the-infamous-radio-broadcast-did-not-cause-a-nationwide-hysteria.html)). The fear that a medium could stampede the public was, in this case, the bigger story.

**Comic books, 1954.** The psychiatrist Fredric Wertham's book Seduction of the Innocent anchored the campaign against comics in the United States. When his clinical sources were opened in 2010, the information scientist Carol L. Tilley documented how he "manipulated, overstated, compromised, and fabricated evidence", especially the evidence he attributed to his own research with young people ([Tilley, Information and Culture, 2012](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/seducing-the-innocent-fredric-wertham-and-the-falsifications-that)).

**McCarthy, 1950 to 1954.** On 9 February 1950 Senator Joseph McCarthy "claimed that he had a list of 205 State Department employees who were Communists. While he offered little proof, the claims gained the Senator great notoriety", the US National Archives records. That June, Senator Margaret Chase Smith and six other Republicans warned that the Senate had been "debased to the level of a forum for hate and character assassination" ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/censure-of-senator-joseph-mccarthy)). On 2 December 1954 the Senate, by 67 votes to 22, censured him for conduct "contrary to senatorial traditions" ([US Senate](https://www.senate.gov/about/powers-procedures/censure/joseph-mccarthy.htm)). In 1953 Arthur Miller had already used the Salem trials as an allegory for the hunt, in The Crucible.

**Rwanda, 1994.** At the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda (ICTR), the "media case" tried two founders of the radio station RTLM (Radio Télévision Libre des Mille Collines), Ferdinand Nahimana and Jean-Bosco Barayagwiza, and Hassan Ngeze, the founder and editor of the newspaper Kangura, which the tribunal found used the word Inyenzi to mean all Rwandans of Tutsi ethnicity. Reading the judgment on 3 December 2003, the trial chamber said: "The power of the media to create and destroy fundamental human values comes with great responsibility. Those who control such media are accountable for its consequences." ([ICTR, trial summary](https://unictr.irmct.org/sites/unictr.org/files/case-documents/ictr-99-52/trial-judgements/en/031203-summary.pdf)). On appeal, on 28 November 2007, the chamber reversed some findings, including those based on issues of Kangura published before 1994, and replaced the sentences of life and 35 years with 30 years for Nahimana, 32 for Barayagwiza and 35 for Ngeze ([ICTR, appeal summary](https://unictr.irmct.org/sites/unictr.org/files/case-documents/ictr-99-52/appeals-chamber-judgements/en/071128-summary.pdf)).

### The last thirty years

**The "superpredators", 1995 to 2001.** In 1995 John DiIulio, a Princeton professor who coined the word "superpredator", predicted that the number of juveniles in custody would triple and that by 2010 there would be "an estimated 270,000 more young predators on the streets than in 1990". The Equal Justice Initiative (EJI) notes that "Much of this frightening imagery was racially coded", and that the laws passed in its wake, which sent more children to adult courts, "had no material effect on the subsequent decrease in crime rates" ([EJI](https://eji.org/news/superpredator-myth-20-years-later/)). Instead of rising, The New York Times reported in February 2001, juvenile crime fell by more than half. That month DiIulio told The New York Times: "If I knew then what I know now, I would have shouted for prevention of crimes". He still defended his data; of his conclusions he said: "Thank God we were wrong." The criminologist Franklin Zimring was blunter: "His prediction wasn't just wrong, it was exactly the opposite". ([The New York Times, 2001](https://www.nytimes.com/2001/02/09/us/as-ex-theorist-on-young-superpredators-bush-aide-has-regrets.html)).

**The measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine scare, 1998 to 2011.** A 1998 paper in The Lancet by Andrew Wakefield and 12 others implied a link between the MMR vaccine and autism. It was, as critics quickly pointed out, a "small case series with no controls". Over the next decade "epidemiological studies consistently found no evidence of a link between the MMR vaccine and autism", and the paper was retracted 12 years after publication. In January 2011, after a series by the journalist Brian Deer, The BMJ's editors wrote that <Claim status="reported" by="by The BMJ">the paper was an elaborate fraud</Claim>; Wakefield, they noted, "has repeatedly denied doing anything wrong at all". The cost was counted in children: vaccination in the United Kingdom fell to 80% in 2003 and 2004, and "In 2008, for the first time in 14 years, measles was declared endemic in England and Wales." ([The BMJ, 2011](https://www.bmj.com/content/342/bmj.c7452)).

**Iraq, 2002 and 2003.** The fear that preceded the invasion of Iraq was a fear of weapons of mass destruction (WMD). Presenting the British inquiry's report on 6 July 2016, Sir John Chilcot said the judgements about the threat "were presented with a certainty that was not justified", that in March 2003 "There was no imminent threat from Saddam Hussein", and that "policy on Iraq was made on the basis of flawed intelligence and assessments. They were not challenged, and they should have been." ([The Iraq Inquiry, 2016](https://webarchive.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ukgwa/20171123123237/http://www.iraqinquiry.org.uk/media/247010/2016-09-06-sir-john-chilcots-public-statement.pdf)). The response of Tony Blair, prime minister at the time, was not among the sources collected for this post.

**Guarujá, Brazil, 2014.** On 3 May 2014 Fabiane Maria de Jesus, a 33-year-old mother of two, "died at the hands of a lynch mob driven by a series of vicious online rumors", Global Voices reported. A local Facebook page with more than 24,000 followers had circulated alerts about a woman who supposedly kidnapped children for witchcraft rituals, with a police sketch that turned out to date from a 2012 case in Rio de Janeiro; the same page had reported that the police had no records of missing children. Investigations, the report says, suggest that "while potent, the rumors were mostly false" ([Global Voices, 2014](https://globalvoices.org/2014/05/27/how-an-internet-rumor-in-brazil-killed-fabiane-maria-de-jesus/)).

**"Pizzagate", Washington, 2016.** On 4 December 2016 Edgar Maddison Welch drove from North Carolina to a pizza restaurant in Washington with an AR-15 rifle, a revolver and a shotgun. According to the government's evidence he was "motivated, at least in part, by unfounded rumors concerning a child sex-trafficking ring that supposedly was being perpetrated at the establishment". He fired into a locked door; customers, including children, and staff fled, and no one was hurt. Sentencing him to four years in 2017, the judge said that "the extent of the recklessness in this case is breathtaking" ([US Department of Justice, 2017](https://www.justice.gov/usao-dc/pr/north-carolina-man-sentenced-four-year-prison-term-armed-assault-northwest-washington)).

**India, 2018.** "More than a dozen people have been killed since May by mobs convinced by messages that the people they are lynching are guilty of child trafficking, organ harvesting or other egregious acts", PBS NewsHour reported in July 2018, about rumours spread on WhatsApp. The company's answer at the time was newspaper advertisements about fake news and a label showing when a message had been forwarded ([PBS NewsHour, 2018](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/world/mobs-are-killing-people-in-india-based-on-false-rumors-spread-through-whatsapp)).

**Myanmar, 2017 and 2018.** The United Nations fact-finding mission on Myanmar wrote in 2018 that "Facebook has been a useful instrument for those seeking to spread hate, in a context where, for most users, Facebook is the Internet. Although improved in recent months, the response of Facebook has been slow and ineffective." ([UN Human Rights Council, 2018](https://www.ohchr.org/sites/default/files/Documents/HRBodies/HRCouncil/FFM-Myanmar/A_HRC_39_64.pdf)). Its chairman, Marzuki Darusman, had said in March that social media played a "determining role". A Facebook spokesperson told the BBC, as Al Jazeera reported, that there is "no place for hate speech" on the platform: "We take this incredibly seriously and have worked with experts in Myanmar for several years to develop safety resources and counter-speech campaigns" ([Al Jazeera, 2018](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2018/3/13/un-facebook-had-a-role-in-rohingya-genocide)).

**The 5G masts, 2020.** In the first weeks of the COVID-19 lockdown in the United Kingdom, attacks on mobile phone masts surged "amid conspiracy theories linking 5G technology to the spread of Covid-19", a claim scientists called baseless. By late May the industry body MobileUK had recorded 87 arson attacks; one, in April, hit a mast serving the Nightingale hospital in Birmingham ([PA, via the Express and Star, 2020](https://www.expressandstar.com/uk-news/around-90-mast-attacks-recorded-during-coronavirus-lockdown-6099682)).

### What the episodes share

Across these cases the medium changed, from pulpit and courtroom to rumour, pamphlet, newspaper, radio, a Facebook page and a WhatsApp forward, but the elements recurred. A hidden enemy was named: witches, aristocrats, Jews, communists, a minority, a generation of children, a vaccine, a restaurant, child snatchers, a phone mast. The claim travelled faster than anyone checked it. The harm fell on the accused, or on bystanders. And the correction came later, as apologies (Massachusetts in 1957, Scotland in 2022), retractions (The Lancet in 2010), inquiries (Chilcot in 2016), court judgments (the ICTR, the case of the Washington gunman), personal regret (DiIulio), or the debunking of the panic story itself (The War of the Worlds). Some of these fears were manufactured, by sources that courts, inquiries or journals later named: a forgery, a propaganda ministry, a file of selected crimes, falsified research, a radio station and a newspaper. Others grew from rumour with no known author. Others came from experts whose forecasts failed. The pattern does not say which present fears are wrong; it says what to ask of any of them: who benefits, what evidence there is, and how fast the claim is moving compared with the checking.

## The apocalypse that did not come, and the one that was prevented

The Year 2000 problem (Y2K) is cited by both sides. In February 1999 a Senate special committee wrote that "talk of the death of civilization, to borrow from Mark Twain, has been greatly exaggerated", while the problem was "very real" ([Senate committee, 1999](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CPRT-106sprt10/pdf/GPO-CPRT-106sprt10.pdf)). Its final report estimated that the country "spent an estimated $100 billion ($8.5 billion within the federal government alone)" and judged <Claim status="disputed">"that the level of effort was justified and the expenditure of funds was indeed necessary"</Claim>, though in countries that spent less per head "the low level of disruption internationally was somewhat surprising" ([Senate committee, 2000](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-Y1_3-PURL-LPS90964/pdf/GOVPUB-Y1_3-PURL-LPS90964.pdf)). The General Accounting Office (GAO) concluded that without that work "critical services could have been disrupted" ([GAO, 2000](https://www.gao.gov/assets/aimd-00-290.pdf)). In 2009 Farhad Manjoo wrote that the response "is remembered as an overreaction" and cited Leon Kappelman's estimate that "80 percent to 90 percent of the money spent on Y2K was right on target" ([Manjoo, 2009](https://www.slate.com/articles/technology/technology/features/2009/apocalypse_then/was_y2k_a_waste.html)). What would have happened without the work cannot be observed; the longer account is in [Y2K: what it costs to be right about a disaster that then does not happen](/learn/y2k-and-the-prevention-paradox). Beck notes one reason prevention is hard to judge afterwards: "the political costs of omission are much higher than the costs of overreaction" ([Beck, 2007](https://www.cidob.org/sites/default/files/2025-05/doc_dinamicas_8.pdf)), an incentive to act rather than a verdict on acting.

Harold Camping's prediction left a dated record. The radio evangelist predicted the end of the world for 21 May 2011, then for 21 October 2011; in March 2012 he called the prediction "incorrect and sinful" ([Religion News Service, 2012](https://reporter.lcms.org/2012/harold-camping-may-21-prediction-was-incorrect-sinful/)) and wrote that "God may not tell his people the date when Christ will return" ([npr.org, 2012](https://www.npr.org/sections/thetwo-way/2012/03/09/148323322/doomsday-prophet-camping-says-predictions-were-incorrect-and-sinful)).

### When prophecy fails

In When Prophecy Fails (1956), Leon Festinger, Henry Riecken and Stanley Schachter followed a very small group, which they called the Seekers, whose leader predicted a great flood from which aliens would rescue her followers. As the sociologist Lorne Dawson summarizes it (the book itself was not read), "Contrary to common sense, though, the group did not abandon its beliefs and disband even in the face of stark disconfirmation of these prophecies"; instead "a faithful core persisted and redoubled its efforts to convince others of the veracity of their ideas", the case from which the theory of cognitive dissonance grew. Dawson notes that many of the group's members "were actually social scientists engaged in covert participant observation", and, reviewing seventeen later studies, that many groups survive a failed prophecy but "The reasons are much more complicated than When Prophecy Fails implies": "only a minority of groups studied (four of thirteen) sought to convert others to compensate for their disappointment", so the "law" as first framed "is incorrect, or, at any rate, it is framed too narrowly". He quotes J. Gordon Melton: "the denial of failure is not just another option, but the common mode of adaptation of millennial groups following the failure of a prophecy" ([Dawson, 1999](https://www.gwern.net/doc/sociology/1999-dawson.pdf)).

Camping fits the pattern. Charles Sarno and colleagues analysed his Open Forum radio programme in the three weeks after 21 May 2011, and wrote: "Following Dawson (1999), we examine the adaptive strategies Camping employed to manage the cognitive dissonance he and his followers experienced during this period". They found that "Forty-two percent of the time, Camping reaffirmed the essential truth of the original prediction by pointing to other, nonfalsifiable components of his prophetic timeline", and "Nearly one-quarter of the time, Camping rationalized the failed prophecy by attributing responsibility to God", stressing God's "Sovereignty" and "Mercy". Their conclusion: "While confirming the results of previous studies, this case adds nuance to our understanding of the organizational conditions, social processes, and charismatic labor by which apocalyptic groups manage dissonance" ([Sarno et al., 2015](https://academic.oup.com/socrel/article-abstract/76/2/199/1637562)). Only the abstract was read.

## "History is written by the victors"

The saying is usually credited to Winston Churchill, but Matthew Phelan, drawing on the quotation researcher Garson O'Toole, found "no concretely documented instance in which he's known to have uttered 'History is written by the victors.'" What Churchill did say, in January 1948, was that the past should be left to history, "especially as I propose to write that history myself". The earliest general form they found is Senator George Graham Vest's in 1891: "history is written by the victors and framed according to the prejudices and bias existing on their side" ([Phelan, 2019](https://slate.com/culture/2019/11/history-is-written-by-the-victors-quote-origin.html)).

### Does it always come down to who wins?

Does every account of these warnings come down, in the end, to the old line that whoever wins the war writes the history books? The cases in this post answer in different ways. The record of the 2026 wave is being written by those who hold the documents: each company counts its own findings, no end-to-end audit of the counts was found, and the text of the June export directive was not found published. Yet the counter-record survives beside it, in VulnCheck's count of fixes, AISLE's tests and the evaluators' stated limits. Y2K has a settled outcome and an unsettled history: the official reports judged the spending necessary, and by 2009 the response was, in Manjoo's words, remembered as an overreaction. Harold Camping wrote the verdict on his own prediction. Failed prophets keep the pen in other ways too: Camping reaffirmed his prediction in the weeks after it failed, and Melton calls the denial of failure the common response. So do those who call a fear overblown, since labelling a reaction a moral panic is, in Garland's account, itself a move in the argument. Whether the side that prevails writes the history, or only the version read first, stays open.

## Who owns the feed

The largest services that carry such warnings are privately owned. Meta reported 3.64 billion daily active people across its apps on average for September 2026 ([Meta, 2026](https://static.poder360.com.br/uploads/2026/10/Meta-Meta-Reports-Third-Quarter-2026-Results.pdf)). The Digital Services Act (DSA) of the European Union (EU) designates as very large any platform with 45 million or more average monthly active recipients in the EU ([DSA, article 33](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065)); the European Commission's list, updated on 6 November 2026, gives as reported by the providers YouTube 416.6 million, Facebook and Instagram 259 million each, TikTok 135.9 million and X 115.1 million ([European Commission, 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/list-designated-vlops-and-vloses)).

At Twitter the rules changed with the owner. When Elon Musk closed his purchase of Twitter on 27 October 2022 he "fired several of the company's top executives" ([Semafor, 2022](https://www.semafor.com/article/10/27/2022/elon-musk-buys-twitter-for-44-bil-fires-top-executives)). In November the company stopped enforcing its coronavirus misinformation policy, and after an online poll Musk announced an account amnesty: "Amnesty begins next week. Vox Populi, Vox Dei" ([cnbc.com, 2022](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/11/29/twitter-stops-policing-covid-19-misinformation-under-ceo-elon-musk.html)). X calls its approach "Freedom of Speech, not Freedom of Reach", limiting the reach of violating posts ([X, 2023](https://blog.x.com/en/product/freedom-of-speech-not-reach-an-update-on-our-enforcement-philosophy)). A peer-reviewed study found <Claim status="reported" by="by Hickey et al.">"the weekly rate of hate speech being approximately 50% higher than the months preceding his purchase"</Claim>, though "this increase cannot be directly attributed to any policy at X"; it cites Musk's post that "Hate speech impressions (# of times tweet was viewed) continue to decline, despite significant user growth!" ([Hickey et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0313293)). No reply from X to the study was found.

In Brazil an owner's rules met a country's courts. On 30 August 2024 Justice Alexandre de Moraes of the Supremo Tribunal Federal (STF) suspended X nationwide until court orders were obeyed, fines paid and a legal representative named, citing <Claim status="established" by="by the STF">"o desaparecimento de seus representantes legais no Brasil para fins de intimação"</Claim>, the disappearance of its legal representatives ([STF, 2024](https://noticias.stf.jus.br/postsnoticias/stf-determina-suspensao-do-x-antigo-twitter-em-todo-o-territorio-nacional-2/)); the court's First Panel confirmed the order unanimously ([STF, 2024b](https://noticias.stf.jus.br/?p=93576)). X had said on 29 August that it expected suspension <Claim status="alleged" by="by X">"simply because we would not comply with his illegal orders to censor his political opponents"</Claim> ([cnnbrasil.com.br, 2024](https://www.cnnbrasil.com.br/politica/x-diz-que-vai-descumprir-ordem-do-ministro-alexandre-de-moraes/)). After X paid the fines ([Columbia Global Freedom of Expression](https://globalfreedomofexpression.columbia.edu/cases/the-case-of-the-x-ban-in-brazil/)), a decision of 8 October 2024 found every requirement for its return met ([STF, Pet 12.404](https://static.poder360.com.br/2024/10/decisao-desbloquar-X-Moraes.pdf)). The Reuters Institute's Brazil page adds that X "resumed service in October after the company met its legal obligations, including paying fines and blocking certain users", and records both sides of the argument that followed: "Moraes has been vocal in defending social media regulation in order to hold digital platforms accountable for falsehoods, but some legal experts are worried he might be going too far" ([Carro, 2025](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2025/brazil)).

### Is the narrative now everyone's, or the owners'?

Has control over what is said become democratic, open to anyone with an account, while the few who own the platforms still make the rules? The record supports both halves. Posting is open to very large numbers: Meta alone counted 3.64 billion people a day in September 2026. What happens to a post after that follows rules its owner writes and can change: Twitter's changed with its purchase, from the coronavirus policy to the account amnesty, and X's "Freedom of Speech, not Freedom of Reach" limits how far a rule-breaking post travels rather than only whether it stays up. Those rules have limits the owners do not set: the European Union obliges the largest platforms to offer a feed not based on profiling, and Brazil's Supreme Court suspended X until it complied with court orders. In Brazil the channels are concentrated, with Instagram, WhatsApp and Facebook, used weekly for news by 37%, 36% and 28% of Brazilians, all in Meta's family of apps; readers keep one power of their own, looking away, which 46% say they use. How much of the narrative the owners control depends on what is counted: the right to post, which is widely shared, or the reach, which is ranked by systems their owners build and, as the next section shows, describe only in part.

## Algorithms: what the word means, and what it hides

The word comes from a name. Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi, a scholar at the House of Wisdom in Baghdad, wrote a treatise on Hindu-Arabic numerals whose Latin translation, Algoritmi de numero Indorum, "gave rise to the word algorithm deriving from his name in the title" ([MacTutor](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Al-Khwarizmi/)). The Dictionary of Algorithms and Data Structures of the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) calls him a Persian author "who wrote a book with arithmetic rules dating from about 825 A.D." and defines an algorithm as "A computable set of steps to achieve a desired result" ([NIST](https://xlinux.nist.gov/dads/HTML/algorithm.html)).

The classic definition is narrow. Donald Knuth's five properties, as a Virginia Tech course states them, are finiteness ("The algorithm must always terminate after a finite number of steps"), definiteness, input, output and effectiveness ([McQuain, Virginia Tech](https://courses.cs.vt.edu/cs2104/Fall13/notes/T16_Algorithms.pdf)). The engineering view is in [What Is an Algorithm? A Working Primer](/learn/what-is-an-algorithm).

What people call "the algorithm" of a social network is something else, and European law has a name for it. The DSA defines a "recommender system" as "a fully or partially automated system used by an online platform to suggest in its online interface specific information to recipients of the service or prioritise that information", including by "determining the relative order or prominence of information displayed" (article 3(s)); the largest platforms must offer "at least one option for each of their recommender systems which is not based on profiling" (article 38) ([DSA](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065)).

The platforms describe their own systems. Meta writes that <Claim status="attributed" by="by Meta">"Our AI systems predict how valuable a piece of content might be to you, so we can show it to you sooner"</Claim>, adding "there's a limit to what we can disclose safely" ([Meta, 2023](https://about.fb.com/news/2023/06/how-ai-ranks-content-on-facebook-and-instagram/)); its terms state that "businesses and organizations, and other persons pay us to show you ads for their products and services" ([Meta, terms](https://www.facebook.com/terms)). YouTube draws on "a user's viewing behaviour, likes, dislikes, subscriptions and feedback" ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/howyoutubeworks/product-features/recommendations/)), and TikTok on "User interactions such as the videos you like or share, accounts you follow" ([TikTok, 2020](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you)).

X publishes its ranking code. When Twitter opened it in 2023, it described a network trained "to optimize for positive engagement (e.g. Likes, Retweets, and Replies)" ([Twitter, 2023](https://web.archive.org/web/20240109224831/https://blog.twitter.com/engineering/en_us/topics/open-source/2023/twitter-recommendation-algorithm)); the current repository says the ranking model <Claim status="attributed" by="by X">"reads the viewer's recent engagement history and predicts, for each post, how likely the viewer is to take each action on it"</Claim>, likes, replies, dwell time and blocks among them, and combines the predictions with weights held in the code; some files stay private because "people could use it to try to game the system". From August 2026 the feed ran a Brazil election filter "which removed posts from accounts reported to Brazil's Electoral Court for the 2026 election, unless the viewer explicitly follows the account"; it was removed on 26 October ([xai-org/x-algorithm](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/xai-org/x-algorithm/main/README.md)). No independent audit of the published code against the running feed was found.

Whether ranking for engagement does harm is disputed. Frances Haugen, a former Facebook product manager whose documents underlay The Wall Street Journal's Facebook Files ([Hao, 2021](https://www.technologyreview.com/2021/10/05/1036519/facebook-whistleblower-frances-haugen-algorithms/)), told the Senate in October 2021 that <Claim status="alleged" by="by Frances Haugen">"The result has been a system that amplifies division, extremism, and polarization"</Claim> and that "only Facebook gets to look under the hood" ([Haugen, 2021](https://www.commerce.senate.gov/services/files/FC8A558E-824E-4914-BEDB-3A7B1190BD49)). Meta's Nick Clegg called the series' central allegation <Claim status="denied" by="by Meta">"just plain false"</Claim> ([Meta, 2021](https://about.fb.com/news/2021/09/what-the-wall-street-journal-got-wrong/)). Experiments with Meta in 2020 found that a chronological feed did not significantly change polarization, political knowledge or other key attitudes over the three months of the study ([Guess et al., 2023](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=37498999&rettype=abstract&retmode=text)), though Science later noted that "the default algorithm was in flux during the experiment" ([Thorp and Vinson, 2024](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adt2983)); a 2025 study that reranked X feeds without the platform's help shifted animosity towards the other party by more than 2 points on a 100-point scale ([Piccardi et al., 2025](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=41308156&rettype=abstract&retmode=text)).

Scholars of media treat these systems as more than code. Tarleton Gillespie writes that "Algorithms need not be software: in the broadest sense, they are encoded procedures for transforming input data into a desired output, based on specified calculations", and names among their dimensions "The promise of algorithmic objectivity", the way their technical character is presented "as an assurance of impartiality" ([Gillespie, 2014](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2014/01/Gillespie_2014_The-Relevance-of-Algorithms.pdf)). Nick Seaver, an anthropologist, argues that "algorithms are not technical rocks in a cultural stream, but are rather just more water" ([Seaver, 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2053951717738104)). Read together, the sources say what "the algorithm" of a feed is: a set of models, weights, filters and policies, retrained on behaviour and changed by its owners, sometimes under law. They also say what it is not: a single fixed formula, a neutral measure of what matters, or a system outsiders can fully inspect.

## What is known, what is disputed, what is unknown

Known: AI systems have found real vulnerabilities; agencies in the United States and the United Kingdom tell defenders to assume capable attackers; in Brazil's official record the 2026 incidents ran on common weaknesses; 40% of people across the Reuters Institute's markets, 46% in Brazil, sometimes or often avoid the news; and groups whose dated prophecy failed have often survived it, more by rationalizing than by recruiting. Disputed: how many reported findings are severe, fixed or exploitable; whether extinction forecasts describe a likely future, and how much weight researchers' own estimates can bear; whether fear persuades or provokes defence; whether reminders of death draw people to strong leaders; whether a given fear is a moral panic or a proportionate response; whether engagement ranking harms public debate. Unknown: the text of the June directive, what the Y2K effort prevented, whether doomscrolling causes anxiety or the reverse, and how the running feeds differ from the code and explanations their owners publish. Open, after the questions asked along the way: where the frightened get their picture, whether fear is being used on purpose, whose account of the 2026 wave will last, and how much of the narrative the platforms' owners control.
