## What "record" means here

A bug bounty record can mean three different things: the largest single payment for one report, the most money a programme paid in a year, or the highest amount a programme says it is willing to pay. The three rarely coincide, and the numbers come from the payers themselves. Each figure below carries its source and its basis. For the concepts (what a bounty is, how it differs from a vulnerability disclosure programme, how scope, safe harbour and disclosure work, and what the law says in the United States and Brazil), see the companion Learn article, [Bug bounty programmes](/learn/bug-bounties-and-vulnerability-disclosure).

## The largest single payouts on record

### Cryptocurrency: the eight-figure bounties

The biggest bounties ever paid were not paid by software companies but by cryptocurrency projects, through the Web3 bounty platform Immunefi. In its 2023 report on crypto bounties, Immunefi listed the top public payments: US$10 million for a vulnerability in Wormhole, "a generic cross-chain messaging protocol", US$6 million for one in Aurora, "a bridge and scaling solution for Ethereum", and US$2.2 million for one in Polygon; the top five together came to US$21,700,042 ([Immunefi, Top Crypto Bounty and Ransom Payments Report, 1 February 2023](https://immunefi.com/blog/research/top-crypto-bounty-and-ransom-payments-report/)). Immunefi's own comparison: "the Wormhole reward alone is larger than the total amount of bounties paid across all Google Vulnerability Reward Programs in 2021, at $8.7 million" (same source). By June 2024 the platform said it had paid more than US$100 million to researchers and had <Claim status="attributed" by="by Immunefi">"facilitated the largest bounty payments in the history of software"</Claim> ([Immunefi, 20 June 2024](https://immunefi.com/blog/company-announcement/immunefi-surpasses-100-million-in-whitehat-rewards-a-milestone-in-web3-security/)).

The reason it gives is the size of what is at stake: in decentralized finance a flaw can mean the direct loss of funds held in a smart contract, so "If the direct loss of funds for a web3 vulnerability could be up to $50 million dollars, then it makes sense to offer a much larger bounty size to incentivize good behavior" ([Immunefi, 2023](https://immunefi.com/blog/research/top-crypto-bounty-and-ransom-payments-report/)). The same report also counts the ransoms projects paid to attackers to recover part of stolen funds, which is a reminder that a bounty and a ransom can look alike when the money moves after the damage.

### The large technology programmes

Outside crypto, single rewards are smaller but rising. The highest single reward of Google's Vulnerability Reward Program (VRP) in 2025 was US$250,000 ([Google, VRP 2025 Year in Review, 31 March 2026](https://blog.google/security/vrp-2025-year-in-review/)). In 2024 the largest Chrome reward was US$100,115, for a bypass of MiraclePtr, Chrome's mitigation against use-after-free bugs, and Google raised the Chrome maximum for a single issue to US$250,000 for remote code execution in the browser or another non-sandboxed process ([Google Online Security Blog, Vulnerability Reward Program: 2024 in Review, March 2025](https://security.googleblog.com/2025/03/vulnerability-reward-program-2024-in.html)). Apple says that since its public bounty opened in 2020 "multiple individual reports" have earned US$500,000 ([Apple Security Research, 10 October 2025](https://security.apple.com/blog/apple-security-bounty-evolved/)).

### The ceilings

What programmes say they will pay is higher again. Apple's new top award, announced in October 2025 and in effect from November, is US$2 million "for exploit chains that can achieve similar goals as sophisticated mercenary spyware attacks", and its bonus system "can more than double this reward, with a maximum payout in excess of $5 million"; Apple calls it "the largest payout offered by any bounty program we're aware of" ([Apple, 10 October 2025](https://security.apple.com/blog/apple-security-bounty-evolved/)). For comparison, the private broker Crowdfense advertises "rewards ranging from USD 10,000 to USD 7 million for full exploit chains", for exclusive acquisition and according to "client interest" ([Crowdfense, Exploit Acquisition Program](https://www.crowdfense.com/exploit-acquisition-program/)). A bounty buys a report and a fix; a broker buys the exploit and the silence.

## Stories on the record

### A Beetle for a bug, 1983

The oldest documented offer is a 1983 poster from Hunter & Ready, held by the Computer History Museum: a Volkswagen Beetle under the line "Get a bug if you find a bug", offering one to anyone who found a bug in its VRTX real-time operating system and assuring readers that "There isn't one in your operating system either" ([Computer History Museum, catalogue 102782474](https://www.computerhistory.org/collections/catalog/102782474)). No record of anyone collecting the car was found.

### Netscape, 1995: the bounty after the embarrassment

Netscape's US$1,000 "Bugs Bounty" for its Navigator 2.0 beta, reported by the Wall Street Journal on 11 October 1995 ([Edupage, 12 October 1995](https://seclists.org/bugtraq/1995/Oct/12)), came three weeks after the company had to tell customers that two Berkeley students had shown its session keys could be recovered "in a few hours of computation time" ([Netscape, as forwarded on 19 September 1995](https://diswww.mit.edu/MENELAUS.MIT.EDU/cpunks/39522)). The bounty followed a public flaw found by outsiders.

### Chromium's "leet" reward, 2010

When Google's Chromium project launched its programme in January 2010 it set the top reward at US$1,337 for bugs that were "particularly severe or particularly clever", and credited Mozilla for "their long-running and successful vulnerability reward program" ([Chromium Blog, 28 January 2010](https://blog.chromium.org/2010/01/encouraging-more-chromium-security.html)); Google's web programme that November capped rewards at US$3,133.7 ([Google, 1 November 2010](https://security.googleblog.com/2010/11/rewarding-web-application-security.html)). By 2024 the Chrome maximum for a single issue had reached US$250,000 ([Google, March 2025](https://security.googleblog.com/2025/03/vulnerability-reward-program-2024-in.html)).

### Microsoft's beta-phase bet, 2013

Microsoft's 2013 programmes paid during the beta of Internet Explorer 11 and Windows 8.1 because, Moussouris said, that is when "white market" brokers were not buying; the Mitigation Bypass Bounty offered up to US$100,000 for a new exploitation technique, at a time when she estimated that people able to find such techniques numbered "fewer than 1,000 worldwide" ([Microsoft, 31 July 2013](https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2013/07/31/microsofts-new-programs-attract-bounty-hunters-who-help-make-safer-products/)).

### Hack the Pentagon, 2016

The US Department of Defense's pilot invited "more than 1,400 hackers", more than 250 of whom submitted at least one report, confirmed 138 "legitimate, unique, and eligible" vulnerabilities, fixed all of them and spent US$150,000; Defense Secretary Ash Carter said the usual outside audit would have cost more than US$1 million ([Carter, 17 June 2016, reproduced by the Belfer Center](https://www.belfercenter.org/publication/pentagons-first-bug-bounty-exceeded-all-expectations)).

### Zoom and DJI: the bounties that were turned down

Two researchers became known for refusing money. CSO Online reported in 2020 that Jonathan Leitschuh declined a Zoom bounty offered with a non-disclosure agreement (NDA), gave the company 90 days and then published; and that Kevin Finisterre walked away from US$30,000 offered by the drone maker DJI because it came with an NDA ([CSO Online, 2 April 2020](https://www.csoonline.com/article/3535888/bug-bounty-platforms-buy-researcher-silence-violate-labor-laws-critics-say.html)). Leitschuh's verdict, in the same article: "What I end up feeling like is that they are trying to buy researcher silence."

### Uber, 2016: US$100,000 that was not a bounty

Uber paid US$100,000 through its bounty programme to people who had downloaded the names and driver's licence numbers of some 600,000 people from its cloud storage, in exchange for an NDA and a promise to delete the data; its former chief security officer, Joseph Sullivan, was convicted of obstructing a Federal Trade Commission (FTC) investigation and of misprision of a felony, the Ninth Circuit affirmed, and the Supreme Court declined to hear his appeal on 29 June 2026 ([Ninth Circuit, amended opinion, 12 November 2025](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2025/11/12/23-927.pdf); [Supreme Court, docket 25-1082](https://www.supremecourt.gov/search.aspx?filename=/docket/docketfiles/html/public/25-1082.html)). The court held that the hackers' access "could not be laundered through an NDA" and left open whether a bounty can authorize research in advance (same opinion). The Learn article sets out both sides of the case.

### curl, 2019 to 2026: the programme that closed

curl started its first real bounty in April 2019 with HackerOne, paid more than US$100,000 for 87 confirmed vulnerabilities, and closed it in January 2026, when its maintainer wrote that the confirmed rate had fallen below 5% amid "an explosion in AI slop reports", low-quality submissions produced with artificial intelligence (AI) tools ([Daniel Stenberg, 26 January 2026](https://daniel.haxx.se/blog/2026/01/26/the-end-of-the-curl-bug-bounty/)). Two months later the Internet Bug Bounty, which had funded curl's rewards, paused new submissions ([HackerOne, Internet Bug Bounty policy](https://hackerone.com/ibb/policy_versions)). In the other direction, HackerOne reported that fully autonomous agents had submitted "560+ valid reports" in its 2025 data ([HackerOne, 1 October 2025](https://www.hackerone.com/press-release/hackerone-report-finds-210-spike-ai-vulnerability-reports-amid-rise-ai-autonomy)).

## How to start safely

The rules below come from the programmes and authorities cited; they are what those parties ask of researchers, not legal advice.

1. **Only test where a published programme invites you.** Scope is the authorization: "Testing is only authorized on the targets listed as in scope", as QuintoAndar's brief puts it ([Bugcrowd, QuintoAndar](https://bugcrowd.com/engagements/quintoandar)). In Brazil, article 154-A of the Penal Code turns on the absence of "autorização expressa ou tácita" and has carried up to four years of reclusão since 2021 ([Senado Federal, Lei nº 14.155/2021](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/34016289/publicacao/34016562)).
2. **Read the whole policy, not only the safe-harbour heading.** VTEX publishes a safe harbour and, in the same document, says that "The existence of this policy does not grant permission to conduct penetration tests or active scanning" ([VTEX, Security Disclosure Policy](https://vtex.com/.well-known/security-disclosure-policy.txt)). The disclose.io safe-harbour text also states that it "does not bind independent third parties" ([disclose.io](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/disclose/dioterms/master/terms/safe-harbor/en-US.md)).
3. **Use your own or test accounts.** "Please, only ever target your own account or a test account" has been Google's rule since 2010 ([Google, 1 November 2010](https://security.googleblog.com/2010/11/rewarding-web-application-security.html)); iFood asks for registration with a Bugcrowd-provided address ([Bugcrowd, iFood](https://bugcrowd.com/engagements/ifood-og)).
4. **Do not touch other people's data, and stop when you see it.** Microsoft: "If you encounter sensitive information or are unsure whether an action is safe, stop immediately and contact us" ([MSRC, Microsoft Bounty Program](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/msrc/bounty)). VTEX forbids exploiting "beyond what is strictly necessary to demonstrate" a flaw ([VTEX](https://vtex.com/.well-known/security-disclosure-policy.txt)).
5. **No denial of service and no heavy automated scanning unless the programme allows it.** Google asked researchers in 2010 to "refrain from using automated testing tools" ([Google, 2010](https://security.googleblog.com/2010/11/rewarding-web-application-security.html)); VTEX bans "automated or high-volume scanning that impacts service stability" ([VTEX](https://vtex.com/.well-known/security-disclosure-policy.txt)).
6. **Validate by hand, especially anything an AI tool produced.** VTEX rejects reports "that consist solely of tool output without manual analysis and a demonstrable PoC" (proof of concept) ([VTEX](https://vtex.com/.well-known/security-disclosure-policy.txt)); curl bans submitters of what it calls AI slop ([Stenberg, 2026](https://daniel.haxx.se/blog/2026/01/26/the-end-of-the-curl-bug-bounty/)).
7. **Write a complete report.** Microsoft says reports with "clear reproduction steps, proof-of-concept code, and detailed analysis" not only speed triage but "qualify for higher awards" ([MSRC](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/msrc/bounty)); HackerOne asks for "clear, concise reproducible steps or a working proof-of-concept" without third-party personal data ([HackerOne, Vulnerability Disclosure Guidelines](https://www.hackerone.com/disclosure-guidelines)).
8. **Know the disclosure terms before you join.** On HackerOne, private programmes are "subject to strict non-disclosure by default", and researchers who intend any public disclosure "should not participate in private Programs" ([HackerOne, guidelines](https://www.hackerone.com/disclosure-guidelines)).
9. **Know the payment rules.** HackerOne pays in US dollars, cannot pay residents of countries under US sanctions, pays minors through a parent or guardian, and leaves taxes to the researcher ([HackerOne, guidelines](https://www.hackerone.com/disclosure-guidelines)).
10. **Do not rely on a prosecutor's policy as immunity.** The US Department of Justice's (DOJ) 2022 policy tells federal prosecutors not to charge good-faith research but warns that "claiming to be conducting security research is not a free pass for those acting in bad faith" ([DOJ, 19 May 2022](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/department-justice-announces-new-policy-charging-cases-under-computer-fraud-and-abuse-act)). Brazil has no equivalent policy or statute.

## The Brazilian scene

### The local platform

BugHunt presents itself on its home page as a "Referência em Bug Bounty na América Latina" and says "Conectamos especialistas em segurança com empresas que querem testar seu software. É grátis e você recompensa apenas os resultados" ([BugHunt, home page](https://bughunt.com.br/)). Its founders, Bruno Telles and Caio Telles, describe it as <Claim status="attributed" by="by its founders">"primeira plataforma de bug bounty do Brasil"</Claim> ([Baguete, 26 August 2026](https://www.baguete.com.br/noticias/inseguranca-juridica-tambem-e-inseguranca-da-informacao)). In a July 2026 column they cited their company's Brazilian Security Index survey: 72% of Brazilian companies invested in cybersecurity in 2021 and 96% now, and 56% of companies with high cybersecurity maturity are "promotoras ativas do modelo de bug bounty"; the column does not give the survey's sample or method ([Baguete, 15 July 2026](https://www.baguete.com.br/noticias/a-relacao-com-hackers-precisa-acompanhar-o-mercado-de-ciberseguranca)). No public list of the programmes BugHunt hosts could be found on its website on 15 November 2026, so those programmes could not be verified on their own pages and are not listed below.

### The legal backdrop

The founders argue that Brazilian law addresses companies and not researchers, and that platform contracts fill the gap ([Baguete, 26 August 2026](https://www.baguete.com.br/noticias/inseguranca-juridica-tambem-e-inseguranca-da-informacao)). The Senate's main cybersecurity bill, projeto de lei (PL) 4752/2025, does not address researchers in its text as presented ([Senado Federal, PL 4752/2025](https://legis.senado.gov.br/sdleg-getter/dadosabertos/documento/fbe682b0-5a29-41c7-aa0b-b219f76b4ea8)). The Learn article covers article 154-A, the Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados (LGPD) and the federal incident channel.

## What the record shows

The money in bug bounties has grown from a car and US$1,000 to eight-figure crypto payouts and nine-figure platform totals, while the median hunter's income remains unpublished. The stories that stuck are not the large cheques but the arguments over silence: the NDAs refused at Zoom and DJI, the NDA that became evidence at Uber, and a maintainer closing his programme because paying for reports attracted more noise than signal. In Brazil the practice exists through international platforms, one local platform and a handful of published security.txt files, under a criminal law that recognizes only authorization.
