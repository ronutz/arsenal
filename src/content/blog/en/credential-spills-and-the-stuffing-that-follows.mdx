## Two events that share one word

When a headline says that billions of passwords have leaked, it can mean two different things, and the difference decides almost everything about the risk.

The first is a **spill**. A copy of usernames or email addresses together with passwords, or with password hashes, leaves the system that held it and begins to circulate: as the dump of one service's database, as a compilation of hundreds of such dumps, or as the output of malware that copied what people typed into their browsers. The second is what attackers do with the spill. **Credential stuffing** is the automated testing of those pairs against other services, in the hope that someone used the same password twice. The Open Worldwide Application Security Project (OWASP) defines it in one line in its prevention cheat sheet: "Testing username/password pairs obtained from the breach of another site." The spill happens once. The stuffing can go on for a decade, against any service that still accepts a password on its own.

This report follows both. It sets out the largest spills on the public record, with what each contained and what Have I Been Pwned (HIBP), the breach-notification service run by the security researcher Troy Hunt, recorded about it. It then sets out the account takeovers (ATO, in the industry's shorthand) that companies, prosecutors and regulators attributed to credential stuffing, and, labelled separately, the near relatives that are not strictly stuffing: credentials harvested by infostealer malware and used where no second factor was required, password spraying, and single reused passwords. Finally it crosses the two lists, to see where a spill can be connected to a takeover and where it cannot, and closes with defences that each come from a named standard or regulator.

### How to read a number

Every source counts something different, and headlines tend to pick the largest number available. Collection #1, the compilation Troy Hunt analysed in January 2019, shows how far apart the counts can be for a single file:

| What is counted | Collection #1 | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rows (lines in the files) | 2,692,818,238 | The same pair can appear on many rows |
| Unique email and password combinations | 1,160,253,228 | Exact duplicates removed |
| Unique email addresses | 772,904,991 | The figure HIBP lists, because it can notify an address |
| Unique passwords | 21,222,975 | Distinct strings, regardless of who used them |

So "2.7 billion records" and "773 million" describe the same files, and neither is a count of people: one person has several addresses, and many addresses are abandoned or fake. Some files hold **only passwords**, with no usernames at all; the lists called "RockYou2021" and "RockYou2024" are of that kind, and they serve guessing rather than stuffing until someone joins them to identities.

The same caution applies to HIBP's own totals. Its public catalogue, read through its application programming interface (API) on 13 November 2026, held 1,051 breaches whose account counts add up to 17,858,196,501. That is a sum of per-breach counts in which the same address appears over and over, not a number of people. HIBP also marks some entries as unverified; the large combo lists of 2016 and 2017 and Collection #1 carry that mark, because no single service can confirm a list assembled from many.

Read the table as a list of what was counted, by whom and how, not as a sum. The same person appears in many rows; some rows count addresses, others pairs, others passwords alone. Three things stand out. The early entries are breaches of one company; the later ones are aggregations whose origin is many companies at once. The passwords move from hashes that had to be cracked to plain text that never needed cracking. And since 2023 the largest new material comes from malware on people's own computers, not from any company's database.

## 2012 to 2014: the original breaches

### LinkedIn

In June 2012 a list of about 6.5 million unique password hashes from LinkedIn was posted to a password-cracking forum, KrebsOnSecurity reported; LinkedIn forced password resets for those accounts and not for the rest. Four years later the rest appeared. In May 2016 a seller on a cybercrime forum offered 117 million records from the 2012 breach, and LinkedIn's Cory Scott wrote that the company was "taking immediate steps to invalidate the passwords of the accounts impacted"; a spokesman said LinkedIn believed the data were "from the 2012 breach". HIBP loaded the data on 21 May 2016 and describes it plainly: 164 million email addresses and passwords, "stored as SHA1 hashes without salt, the vast majority of which were quickly cracked in the days following the release of the data." SHA-1 is fast by design, and without a salt the same password always produces the same hash, which is why one cracking run recovers every account that shared it.

The spill itself began with a stolen credential. On 30 September 2020 the United States (US) Attorney's Office for the Northern District of California announced that Yevgeniy Nikulin, a Russian national convicted by a jury after a trial that resumed on 7 July 2020, had been sentenced to 88 months in prison for intrusions at LinkedIn, Dropbox and the now-defunct Formspring. According to the release, he infected a LinkedIn employee's computer with malware, used that employee's credentials to reach LinkedIn's corporate virtual private network (VPN), and stole a database of user login information.

### Dropbox

On 31 July 2012 Dropbox explained a wave of spam to its users. "Our investigation found that usernames and passwords recently stolen from other websites were used to sign in to a small number of Dropbox accounts," it wrote, and "a stolen password was also used to access an employee Dropbox account containing a project document" with user email addresses. Dropbox promised two-factor authentication "in a few weeks". In the company's own words, that was credential stuffing. Dropbox did not name the other websites, and no public source examined here links its 2012 logins to LinkedIn's spill of the same summer.

Dropbox's own spill surfaced in August 2016, when it forced password resets; HIBP recorded 68,648,009 records of email addresses and salted hashes, "half of them SHA1, half of them bcrypt". The Justice Department's release on Nikulin says the evidence showed he was behind "similar intrusions and thefts of data" at Dropbox.

### Adobe

In October 2013, according to HIBP, 153 million Adobe accounts were breached, "each containing an internal ID, username, email, encrypted password and a password hint in plain text." HIBP's count is 152,445,165. The passwords were encrypted rather than hashed, and "the password cryptography was poorly done and many were quickly resolved back to plain text", while the plain-text hints "disclosed much about the passwords". Encryption is reversible by design for whoever holds the key; a password store should not be.

### Yahoo

On 3 October 2017 Yahoo, by then part of Oath, said it now believed that "all Yahoo user accounts were affected by the August 2013 theft": about three billion accounts, up from the more than one billion it had disclosed on 14 December 2016. The stolen information included names, email addresses, telephone numbers, dates of birth, passwords hashed with MD5 and some security questions and answers; Yahoo said it did not include clear-text passwords, payment card data or bank account information.

A second theft, in late 2014, reached as many as 500 million users. On 24 April 2018 the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) settled with Altaba, the former Yahoo, for a US$ 35 million civil penalty: Yahoo had learned of the breach in late 2014 and did not disclose it in its public filings until September 2016, while its risk disclosures still spoke of breaches only as a future possibility. Neither Yahoo theft is in HIBP. What HIBP does hold is a smaller 2012 breach of Yahoo's Voices publishing service, 453,427 usernames and passwords stored in plain text and taken by SQL (Structured Query Language) injection, with one of the earliest public measurements of reuse: "of the compromised accounts, a staggering 59% of people who also had accounts in the Sony breach reused their passwords across both services."

## 2016 to 2021: the combo-list years

### Exploit.In and Anti Public

In May 2017 Troy Hunt loaded two lists that changed the scale of the problem. Anti Public, about 17 gigabytes, held 457,962,538 unique email addresses; in a random sample of 10,000, 75.78 per cent were already in HIBP, which put about 111 million addresses on record for the first time. Exploit.In, 111 text files of just over 24 gigabytes, held 593,427,119 unique addresses. About 222 million addresses appeared in both. Together they added 1,051,389,657 accounts, a 39 per cent increase that took HIBP to 3.75 billion. Both were, in Hunt's words, "just masses of email address and password pairs" in plain text, the raw material of stuffing, and HIBP's descriptions say so directly: the lists were "broadly circulated and used for 'credential stuffing'".

Hunt confirmed the data the only way such data can be confirmed: more than 130,000 of his 1.1 million subscribers at the time were in Anti Public, several of those he asked recognized the passwords, and his own address appeared in Exploit.In with an old password. His post also recorded the defensive side of a spill. Spotify and DigitalOcean users had received warnings about suspicious sign-ins, and MEGA said it flagged accounts whose addresses appeared in HIBP, without checking the leaked passwords. Spills feed defenders as well as attackers.

### Collection #1, and #2 to #5

On 17 January 2019 Hunt described Collection #1: more than 12,000 files totalling more than 87 gigabytes, found on the MEGA file-sharing service (since removed) and shared on a hacking forum that advertised "2000+ dehashed databases and Combos". The counts are the ones in the table above: 2,692,818,238 rows, 1,160,253,228 unique email and password combinations, 772,904,991 unique email addresses (about 140 million of them new to HIBP, by sample) and 21,222,975 unique passwords, about half of them new to his Pwned Passwords list, which grew to 551,509,767. Most of the source passwords had originally been hashed; the collection held them "dehashed", cracked back to plain text. Hunt verified his own address and a former password, recognized many of the 2,890 file names as known breaches, and did not claim to have verified them all.

About two weeks later, Olhar Digital reported on 1 February 2019, a larger set called Collections #2 to #5 appeared: about 2.2 billion usernames paired with passwords, 845 gigabytes, shared freely. The Hasso Plattner Institute (HPI) of the University of Potsdam catalogued it and found 750 million credentials it had not seen before; Wired quoted the security researcher Chris Rouland calling it the largest collection of stolen credentials seen so far. HIBP has no entry for it.

### Cit0day, COMB and the RockYou lists

In November 2020 a collection called Cit0day, data from more than 23,000 allegedly breached websites, was made available on several hacking forums. HIBP, which received it from dehashed.com, counted 226 million unique email addresses with passwords "often represented as both password hashes and the cracked, plain text versions", and noted that independent verification "established it contains many legitimate, previously undisclosed breaches."

On Tuesday 2 February 2021, Cybernews reported, a file called COMB, the "Compilation of Many Breaches", was posted on a popular hacking forum: more than 3.2 billion unique email and password pairs in clear text (the bundled counting script reported more than 3.27 billion), sorted alphabetically in a folder tree, packed in a password-protected container with scripts to count, query and sort it. Cybernews identified credentials from past leaks at Netflix and LinkedIn, the Exploit.In list and Bitcoin, and described COMB as a compilation, not a new breach, comparable to a 2017 "Breach Compilation" of 1.4 billion pairs. HIBP has no entry for COMB.

The RockYou lists are a different object. The original RockYou breach of 2009 exposed "more than 32 million user passwords stored in plain text", Cybernews recalled in June 2021, when a forum user posted a 100-gigabyte text file called RockYou2021 and claimed 82 billion entries. Cybernews counted 8,459,060,239 unique entries; the poster said they were 6 to 20 characters long, with whitespace and any character outside the basic ASCII (American Standard Code for Information Interchange) set removed. On 4 July 2024 a file called rockyou2024.txt was posted with 9,948,575,739 unique plain-text passwords, about 1.5 billion more than its predecessor according to Cybernews. Neither file contains usernames or email addresses, and Cybernews's articles do not establish how many of the billions of strings were ever a real person's password. A list of strings without owners is a guessing dictionary: it serves brute force and spraying directly, and stuffing only once someone joins it to identities, which is how Cybernews itself framed the risk.

## 2023 to 2026: the infostealer years

### What a stealer log is

An infostealer is malware that copies what a computer already knows: the usernames and passwords saved in its browsers, its cookies, its system details and, where present, its cryptocurrency wallets. BleepingComputer described the product in June 2025: an infostealer log "is generally an archive containing numerous text files and other stolen data", with credentials usually saved as web address (URL), username and password on one line. The United States Department of Justice (DOJ), announcing Operation Magnus against the RedLine and META infostealers on 28 October 2024, added the commercial detail. The stolen data, called "logs", are sold on cybercrime forums; the malware can also help criminals bypass multi-factor authentication (MFA) by stealing authentication cookies; it is sold as malware-as-a-service, in which affiliates buy a licence and run their own campaigns through malicious advertising, phishing email, fraudulent software downloads and sideloading, with sales channels that offer customer support and updates. The two families had infected millions of computers, and "The United States does not believe it is in possession of all the stolen data."

Microsoft's Digital Crimes Unit put prices on it when it moved against Lumma, announced on 21 May 2025. Lumma had been sold as a service since at least 2022, stealing passwords, card and bank details and cryptocurrency wallets. Microsoft identified more than 394,000 infected Windows computers worldwide between 16 March and 16 May 2025, and a court order led to about 2,300 malicious domains being taken down, suspended or blocked, with the Justice Department, Europol's European Cybercrime Centre and Japan's Cybercrime Control Center among the partners. Buyers chose a tier: US$ 250, US$ 500 or US$ 1,000, or US$ 20,000 for the source code. Mandiant, investigating the 2024 Snowflake intrusions, named the families it found behind the stolen credentials: VIDAR, RISEPRO, REDLINE, RACOON STEALER, LUMMA and METASTEALER.

Two properties make a stealer log worse than a breach dump. It pairs each credential with the site it belongs to, so the attacker does not have to guess where to try it. And it captures the password as typed, in plain text, on the victim's own device, so the way the service stored the password, salted or not, slow or fast, does not matter on this path at all.

### Naz.API, the Telegram lists and Synthient

On 18 January 2024 Troy Hunt described Naz.API: 319 files totalling 104 gigabytes, posted to a hacking forum almost four months earlier and reported to him by a well-known technology company that had received a bug bounty submission based on it. It combined stealer logs (website, username, password) with classic stuffing pairs. The largest file had 312 million rows. HIBP counted 70,840,771 unique email addresses, about 35 per cent of them new by sample, and about 100 million unique passwords that appeared 1.3 billion times across the corpus; 427,308 of HIBP's own subscribers were in it. Hunt confirmed accounts by running password-reset flows on the listed services, and subscribers he contacted recognized their old passwords.

The corpora grew from there. In June 2024 HIBP loaded 2 billion rows with 361 million unique email addresses, 122 gigabytes across 1,700 files "collated from malicious Telegram channels", a mix of existing combo lists and stealer output. In February 2025 it loaded 23 billion rows of stealer logs taken from one Telegram channel, with 284 million unique addresses, searchable by the domain of the target website. In October 2025 it loaded 183 million unique addresses from stealer-log data aggregated by the threat-intelligence firm Synthient.

Then, on 5 November 2025, Hunt announced the largest load in HIBP's history: 1,957,476,021 unique email addresses gathered by Synthient from credential-stuffing lists, with 1.3 billion unique passwords, 625 million of which Pwned Passwords had never seen. The corpus spanned about 32 million email domains; gmail.com addresses were about 20 per cent of them, 394 million, which, Hunt stressed, does not make it a Gmail breach. Of HIBP's 5.9 million subscribers, 2.9 million were in it. Several of those he asked confirmed the passwords, including old ones they had since changed; one replied "maybe when i was a kid lol", and Hunt noted that presence in a stuffing list does not guarantee that every listed password was really the person's. The load made each Pwned Passwords hash range about 50 per cent larger.

In June 2026 HIBP added a further collection of stealer logs from various sources, 56 million unique addresses across hundreds of millions of log records and 124 million unique passwords, and pointed individuals to a stealer-log section of their dashboard and organizations to a stealer-log API for their domains.

### Law enforcement as a source

The police now feed HIBP directly. Its catalogue holds four entries for Operation Endgame, the international action against malware infrastructure supported by Europol and Eurojust: 16,466,858 addresses (May 2024), 15,436,844 (May 2025), 2,046,030 (November 2025) and 4,348,526 (June 2026). The last describes a phase against the SocGholish malware distribution network that remediated almost 15,000 compromised websites and disrupted more than 100 servers and domains, followed by 4 million addresses and 9 million passwords from the StealC infostealer.

### The "16 billion credentials" of June 2025

In June 2025 Cybernews reported datasets that together held 16 billion credentials, and coverage described it as "one of the largest data breaches in history". On 19 June 2025 BleepingComputer's Lawrence Abrams answered under the headline that it was "not a new data breach, or a breach at all": a compilation "of previously leaked credentials stolen by infostealers, exposed in data breaches, and via credential stuffing attacks", made of multiple datasets in a format associated with infostealers, with "no evidence this compilation contains new or previously unseen data", and no recent compromise of the websites involved. HIBP has no distinct entry for it. How much of the 16 billion was new, and how much was the same material counted again, is a question neither side answered with a published count.

Three of the seven stored passwords as fast hashes without salt and one in plain text; only two used bcrypt, which is designed to be slow to crack. The closest Brazilian case to the takeovers below is not a spill of customer passwords at all. On 24 June 2024 the National Social Security Institute (Instituto Nacional do Seguro Social, INSS) said that the registration data of about 40 million retirees and pensioners had been exposed to uncontrolled access through Suibe, a benefits information system, by logins issued to servants of other public bodies who had since retired, been dismissed or resigned, and whose access had never been revoked; it was still assessing whether data had actually leaked. The system had no two-factor authentication; the INSS suspended the external passwords and required a digital certificate for outside access. That is a stale-credential failure, a near relative of stuffing. This report did not locate a Brazilian company's or regulator's published finding of a credential-stuffing takeover documented to the standard of the cases that follow.

### Dunkin', 2015 to 2020: the first regulator's case

The New York Attorney General's office set out the earliest fully documented case. Beginning in early 2015, it said on 15 September 2020, Dunkin' customer accounts were targeted in "credential stuffing attacks" that used usernames and passwords stolen from other breaches, and tens of thousands of accounts were compromised within months. A third-party app developer alerted Dunkin' repeatedly and handed over a list of nearly 20,000 accounts compromised in a single five-day sample. According to the office, Dunkin' did not investigate to find the other compromised accounts or to learn whether funds had been taken, did not notify customers, reset their passwords or freeze their stored-value cards, and did not add adequate safeguards to its app, so the attacks continued for years; during the litigation the office found thousands more accounts compromised between 2015 and 2018.

The settlement required Dunkin' to reset passwords, notify customers with registered cards that they might be eligible for refunds, maintain reasonable safeguards against future credential stuffing, follow an incident-response procedure for each new attack (investigate, notify, reset, move balances to new card accounts) and pay US$ 650,000 in penalties and costs. The office relied on New York's breach-notification law: an account opened with a stolen password was treated as a breach the company had to report, even though no Dunkin' database had been taken. "For years, Dunkin' hid the truth and failed to protect the security of its customers, who were left paying the bill," Attorney General Letitia James said.

### Ring, 2017 to 2023: stuffing as an unfair practice

On 31 May 2023 the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) announced a complaint and proposed order against Ring, Amazon's home-camera company. Among its allegations, as The Record reported, Ring failed to block credential stuffing and brute-force logins even after its staff and outside researchers raised concerns; it suffered multiple credential-stuffing attacks in 2017 and 2018; it did not add multi-factor authentication until 2019, and "even then, Ring's sloppy implementation of the additional security measures hampered their effectiveness." Hackers reached videos and other information in about 55,000 US accounts and used the cameras to harass customers, including children and elderly people. The proposed order, which also covered employees' access to customer videos, required US$ 5.8 million in refunds and a new privacy and security programme. Ring neither admitted nor denied the allegations and said it had "promptly addressed these issues on its own years ago."

### 2019 and 2020: streaming, video calls and games

Shortly after Disney+ launched in November 2019, ZDNet found its logins on sale on hacking forums for US$ 3 each. Disney said it had "found no evidence of a security breach", that the problems affected "a very small percentage of users" and, as the Associated Press reported, that the credentials came from earlier breaches at other companies.

In April 2020 the intelligence firm Cyble found more than 500,000 Zoom accounts being sold or given away on hacking forums, and bought about 530,000 at roughly a fifth of a US cent each; each record had an email address, password, personal meeting link and host key. BleepingComputer reported on 13 April 2020 that attackers had tested pairs from older breaches against Zoom's login. Zoom said such attacks "generally" did not affect large enterprise customers using their own single sign-on (SSO), and that it was locking compromised accounts.

Nintendo announced on 24 April 2020 that about 160,000 Nintendo Network IDs (NNID) might have been accessed with login IDs and passwords obtained illegally from sources outside its services, and on 9 June found about 140,000 more. Fewer than 1 per cent had unauthorized purchases made through linked Nintendo Accounts. The company removed the ability to sign in to a Nintendo Account through an NNID, reset passwords, recommended two-step verification and told users not to reuse passwords from other services.

In July 2020 researchers at vpnMentor found an unsecured, internet-facing Elasticsearch database holding more than 380 million records, which a third party was using to store Spotify login credentials; an estimated 300,000 to 350,000 accounts were affected. Spotify reset the affected passwords between 10 and 21 July. The researchers said the incident "didn't originate from Spotify", and the database's owners were never identified.

### Seventeen companies and 1.1 million accounts, 2022

On 5 January 2022 the New York Attorney General's office announced the result of several months spent monitoring online communities dedicated to credential stuffing. It had found thousands of posts containing customer credentials and compiled more than 1.1 million compromised accounts at 17 well-known retailers, restaurant chains and food-delivery services. Most of the attacks had not been detected by the companies. The office alerted each of them, worked through how the attackers had bypassed existing safeguards, and published a business guide whose recommendations are set out in the defences section.

### The North Face, 2022 and 2025

Attacks on The North Face's website began on 26 July 2022, were detected on 11 August and shut down by 19 August, according to BleepingComputer's report as cited by Malwarebytes; at least 194,905 accounts were compromised, with names, billing and shipping addresses, purchase history and phone numbers exposed and no payment details accessed. On 23 April 2025 the company detected what its notice called "a small-scale credential stuffing attack against our Website", disabled passwords to stop it and required new ones; Malwarebytes counted it as the company's fourth such notice.

### DraftKings, 2022 to 2026: the case with convictions

On or about 18 November 2022, according to the United States Attorney's Office for the Southern District of New York, attackers ran a "credential stuffing attack" against a fantasy sports and betting website, which the press identified as DraftKings, using username and password pairs reportedly bought on the dark web. They accessed about 60,000 accounts. On about 1,600 of them they added a new payment method, made a US$ 5 verification deposit and withdrew the balance to accounts they controlled, about US$ 600,000 in all; they also discussed selling access to the rest. On 31 January 2024 a 19-year-old from Wisconsin who had pleaded guilty to conspiring to commit computer intrusion was sentenced to 18 months in prison and ordered to pay US$ 1,327,061 in restitution. Help Net Security reported on 25 June 2026 that a second defendant had received 30 months in April 2026 and a third, 18 months.

### PayPal, 2022 to 2025: the regulator's penalty

On 6 December 2022 a credential-stuffing attack gave intruders access to PayPal accounts, and through them to tax documents that, after changes made in 2022, displayed customers' information unmasked. The Record, reporting the New York Department of Financial Services (DFS) consent order announced on 23 January 2025, said 34,942 people were affected, with names, addresses, Social Security numbers, individual taxpayer identification numbers and dates of birth exposed; PayPal's team saw the spike in access attempts on 7 December and fixed the problem. The DFS found that PayPal had violated New York's cybersecurity regulation for financial services, including its requirements to use qualified personnel for key cybersecurity functions and to train staff, and that the change behind the exposure had skipped PayPal's own risk review through a clerical error. PayPal agreed to a US$ 2 million penalty that insurance cannot cover, and the DFS noted that it now requires multi-factor authentication for all US customer logins.

### Norton, Chick-fil-A and Hot Topic

In January 2023 Gen Digital, Norton's owner, told customers that a credential-stuffing attack using usernames and passwords obtained elsewhere had begun in early December 2022, with unusual failed logins noticed in mid-December, and that it had secured 925,000 inactive and active accounts that may have been targeted. "We strongly believe that an unauthorized third party knows and has utilized your username and password for your account," its notice said, adding that the intruders may have viewed names, phone numbers and mailing addresses, and warning about the data protected by Norton Password Manager.

In March 2023 Chick-fil-A confirmed that attackers had accessed personal information and used the stored rewards balances of "over 71,000 customers" in credential-stuffing attacks between December 2022 and February 2023. It happened again: on 22 July 2026 BleepingComputer reported a second automated attack, run against the website and app between 17 and 19 June 2026 with "account credentials (e.g., email addresses and passwords) obtained from a third-party source". Chick-fil-A logged out the affected accounts, removed their payment methods and restored balances; its state filings counted 2,182 Texans and 39 Massachusetts residents.

On 31 July 2023 Hot Topic reported a series of automated credential-stuffing attacks on its Rewards accounts between 7 February and 21 June 2023, twelve days of breaches in five waves by Cybersecurity Dive's count, using valid credentials from an unknown third party. "Hot Topic was not the source of the account credentials used in these attacks," it said, but also: "we are not able to determine which accounts were accessed by unauthorized third parties." It said it would notify every customer whose account was accessed during the attacks. A separate Hot Topic breach in October 2024, which HIBP lists with 56,904,909 addresses, is not described by HIBP as stuffing.

### 23andMe, 2023 to 2025: from 14,000 accounts to a bankruptcy

The genetic-testing company 23andMe began investigating on 1 October 2023, when a hacker's claim to hold user profile data surfaced online. Its filing with the SEC, as the HIPAA Journal reported, put the directly breached accounts at about 0.1 per cent of its 14 million users, about 14,000; through the DNA Relatives feature those accounts exposed information on about 5.5 million people, and family-tree information on a further 1.4 million, about 6.9 million in all. The company forced a password reset for every user and made two-step verification, previously optional, mandatory.

The United Kingdom (UK) Information Commissioner's Office (ICO), investigating jointly with the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada, fined 23andMe £2.31 million on 17 June 2025. Its findings: a hacker used credential stuffing from April to September 2023; 155,592 UK residents were affected, with data that included names, birth years, locations, profile images, race, ethnicity, family trees and health reports; 23andMe lacked appropriate authentication and verification measures, "such as mandatory multi-factor authentication, secure password protocols, or unpredictable usernames"; it had no additional verification for access to raw genetic data and no effective systems to detect threats; and its response was slow. In August 2023 a claim of data theft affecting more than 10 million users had been dismissed as a hoax; a full investigation began only in October, after an employee found the data advertised for sale. "23andMe failed to take basic steps to protect this information," Information Commissioner John Edwards said.

By then the company was in Chapter 11. It filed in March 2025 in the Eastern District of Missouri; the sale of nearly all its assets for US$ 305 million to TTAM Research Institute, a nonprofit led by its co-founder Anne Wojcicki, was contested by several states over consent for the sale of genetic data and allowed to proceed after an appeals-court decision in July 2025. The plan approved by the bankruptcy court, Paul, Weiss reported on 26 November 2025, includes a settlement of up to US$ 62 million for claims from the October 2023 incident: a US class fund of US$ 30 million to US$ 50 million, a Canadian class fund of US$ 3.25 million and US$ 9 million for claimants in arbitration.

### Roku, 2024

In early March 2024 Roku notified about 15,000 customers whose accounts had been accessed with login credentials from another source. In April 2024 it announced that 576,000 more had been compromised in credential-stuffing attacks; "it is likely that login credentials used in these attacks were taken from another source," it said, and its systems were not the source. In fewer than 400 cases the attackers used stored payment methods to buy subscriptions and hardware. Roku reset passwords, refunded the purchases and turned on two-factor authentication for all accounts.

### Snowflake's customers, 2024

On 10 June 2024 Mandiant, part of Google Cloud, reported that a financially motivated group it tracks as UNC5537 was "systematically compromising Snowflake customer instances using stolen customer credentials, advertising victim data for sale on cybercrime forums, and attempting to extort many of the victims." With Snowflake it notified about 165 potentially exposed organizations. Mandiant found no evidence that the access "stemmed from a breach of Snowflake's enterprise environment"; every incident traced back to compromised customer credentials, stolen primarily by infostealers running on non-Snowflake systems, the earliest in November 2020. Three factors explained the success: the accounts had no MFA, so "successful authentication only required a valid username and password"; the credentials "had not been rotated or updated", in some cases for as long as four years; and the instances had no network allow lists limiting access to trusted locations. "At least 79.7% of the accounts leveraged by the threat actor in this campaign had prior credential exposure." In several cases the infection started on contractors' computers "that were also used for personal activities, including gaming and downloads of pirated software", and Mandiant observed that a single contractor's laptop "can facilitate threat actor access across multiple organizations".

The best-known victim, AT&T, said on 12 July 2024 that customer data had been "illegally downloaded from our workspace on a third-party cloud platform" between 14 and 25 April 2024, and a spokesperson confirmed the platform was Snowflake: records of calls and texts of nearly all its cellular customers between 1 May and 31 October 2022, without their content or Social Security numbers. Its filing said at least one person had been apprehended.

Is this credential stuffing? Not in the strict sense: these were not pairs from someone else's breach tried at random, but the victims' own credentials, stolen from their own or their contractors' machines and used directly. What it shares with stuffing is the condition that made it work, a password accepted on its own. Snowflake's answer, framed as part of its commitment to the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency's (CISA) Secure by Design pledge, was to change the default: MFA for all password sign-ins in new accounts created from October 2024, enforcement for human users from April 2025 and for all human sign-ins from August 2025, and, by November 2025, a block on single-factor password sign-in for every user, human or service. Whether a platform that allowed single-factor sign-in by default shares responsibility for intrusions into its customers' accounts is the question the case left open; Mandiant's finding was that the platform itself was not breached, and Snowflake's later change was that the default would no longer allow it.

### Colonial Pipeline, Change Healthcare and Microsoft

On 29 April 2021 attackers entered Colonial Pipeline's network through a VPN account that was no longer in use but still worked, Mandiant's Charles Carmakal told Bloomberg; it had no MFA, so the attackers needed only a username and password. The password had appeared in a batch of leaked passwords on the dark web, and an employee may have reused it on an account breached elsewhere, but Carmakal said he was not certain that was how the attackers obtained it, and investigators might never know. Colonial paid a US$ 4.4 million ransom.

"On February 12, criminals used compromised credentials to remotely access a Change Healthcare Citrix portal," UnitedHealth Group's chief executive, Andrew Witty, told a House of Representatives subcommittee in written testimony on 1 May 2024. "The portal did not have multi-factor authentication." Ransomware was deployed nine days later. How the credentials were compromised was not stated.

On 19 January 2024 Microsoft disclosed that the Russian state actor it calls Midnight Blizzard had, from November 2023, used a password spray (one password tried against many accounts, then another) to compromise "a legacy, non-production test tenant account", and from there reached a small percentage of corporate mailboxes, including senior leadership, cybersecurity and legal staff. Its follow-up of 25 January, as The Register reported, confirmed that the account lacked MFA, that the attackers routed their attempts through residential connections to look like home workers, and that they abused a legacy test application to grant themselves access to mailboxes. If the same team targeted the tenant today, Microsoft said, "mandatory Microsoft policy and workflows would ensure MFA and our active protections are enabled".

The table's most consistent entry is its last column. In this record, no company, prosecutor or regulator named the spill that fed a takeover. The reason is visible in the spills themselves: Collection #1 was built from thousands of breaches, COMB from Collection #1 and its successors, Synthient's corpus from lists that already contained all of them, and a reused password sits in most of them at once. For a defender the source is interchangeable. What matters is whether a password exposed anywhere still opens an account here.

### How often people reuse

Credential stuffing works only because of reuse, and three measurements on the record give its order of magnitude.

| Measurement | Finding | Source and date |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Accounts in both the Yahoo Voices and Sony breaches | 59 per cent used the same password on both | HIBP's Yahoo entry, 2012 data |
| Sign-ins checked by Google's Password Checkup extension | 316,000 of 21 million, or 1.5 per cent, used credentials already known to be breached; users reset 26 per cent of flagged passwords | Google Security Blog, 15 August 2019 |
| Passwords of the same user across services | In the median case only 49 per cent were distinct from each other | Verizon, 2025 Data Breach Investigations Report (DBIR) research |
| Credential stuffing in single sign-on authentication logs | A median of 19 per cent of daily attempts over two years; 25 per cent at enterprises of 500 or more employees, 12 per cent at small businesses; 44 per cent on the worst day | Verizon, 2025 DBIR research |

Google's study also showed what a warning achieves: of the passwords users did change, 60 per cent were strong enough to resist more than a hundred million guesses.

### Patterns

**The second factor arrived after the incident.** Ring added MFA in 2019, after the 2017 and 2018 attacks; 23andMe made two-step verification mandatory after October 2023; Roku turned it on for every account after April 2024; PayPal required it for all US customer logins after December 2022; Snowflake moved from optional MFA to a block on single-factor sign-in after the 2024 intrusions. In the near relatives the pattern is the same: Colonial's VPN account, Change Healthcare's portal, Microsoft's test tenant and the INSS's external logins all accepted a password alone. The ICO named "mandatory multi-factor authentication" as one of the measures 23andMe lacked.

**MFA is necessary and no longer sufficient.** Verizon's researchers wrote in 2025 that "MFA-enablement is not the panacea it used to be in stopping credential abuse", and the Justice Department noted in 2024 that infostealers help criminals bypass MFA by stealing authentication cookies: a session that was opened with a second factor can be taken over with the cookie alone, and no control applied only at login prevents that.

**Rate limiting alone does not stop stuffing.** The New York Attorney General's guide observes that attackers who rotate proxy addresses evade rate limits, and that bot detection is "one of the most effective controls"; one restaurant chain reported more than 271 million login attempts blocked in 17 months. Microsoft's attackers sprayed through residential connections for the same reason. The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) sets an upper bound, not a target: no more than 100 consecutive failed attempts before an authenticator is disabled.

**Breached-password screening is now a standard requirement.** NIST's Special Publication (SP) 800-63B-4, published on 31 July 2025, says a verifier "SHALL compare the prospective secret against a blocklist" of "known commonly used, expected, or compromised passwords", and lists passwords "obtained from previous breach corpuses" among the things the list may include. OWASP points to HIBP's Pwned Passwords as "a well known free service for this". Google's numbers show that a warning at the right moment changes behaviour in a quarter of cases.

**How a password was stored decides how fast a breach dump becomes a stuffing list.** LinkedIn's unsalted SHA-1 hashes were "quickly cracked"; Adobe's encryption was "poorly done" and its hints gave the rest away; Collection #1 held mostly "dehashed" passwords. Dropbox's bcrypt half and Vakinha's bcrypt were slower to give way. But storage quality is irrelevant to stealer logs, which capture the password as typed on the victim's device.

**Credentials age slowly.** Mandiant found Snowflake credentials still valid up to four years after theft; Synthient's corpus returned passwords that people recognized, some long since changed; LinkedIn's 2012 hashes resurfaced in 2016. A spill does not expire on its own; only a password change does.

**Passkeys remove the thing that is spilled.** The FIDO (Fast IDentity Online) Alliance describes passkeys as "designed so that there are no shared secrets", with "no passwords to steal and there is no sign-in data that can be used to perpetuate attacks." A FIDO-commissioned survey in 2024 found that 53 per cent of people had enabled passkeys on at least one account, and 22 per cent on every account they could. CISA has called phishing-resistant MFA "the gold standard for MFA", and FIDO with the Web Authentication standard (WebAuthn) "the only widely available phishing-resistant forms of MFA".

### What the record does not show

The record shows that spills are huge, that reuse is common and that stuffing is constant, but it does not show the rate at which a given spill turns into takeovers, because no organization in this sample published which spill it saw used. It does not show how much of each new compilation is new: Hunt published samples and new-to-HIBP percentages; Cybernews and the "16 billion" coverage did not. And it shows only the takeovers that someone disclosed. The New York Attorney General found that most of the 17 companies' attacks had not been detected before its office alerted them.

## What regulators now expect

| Date | Authority | Case | Expectation stated |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 24 April 2018 | SEC | Yahoo | A known breach must be disclosed to investors in time; risk factors cannot describe as hypothetical what has already happened |
| 15 September 2020 | New York Attorney General | Dunkin' | Credential stuffing is a breach to investigate and notify; maintain safeguards against it; reset, notify and refund |
| 5 January 2022 | New York Attorney General | 17 companies | Bot detection, MFA and passwordless sign-in as the most effective defences; screen new passwords against breached ones; re-authenticate at payment |
| 31 May 2023 | FTC | Ring | Failing to block stuffing and brute force after warnings is a security failure; a "sloppy implementation" of MFA "hampered" its effectiveness |
| 23 January 2025 | New York DFS | PayPal | Qualified personnel, training and change control; MFA for all customer logins |
| 17 June 2025 | ICO | 23andMe | Mandatory MFA, secure password protocols, unpredictable usernames, extra verification for the most sensitive data, monitoring that detects attacks |
| 31 July 2025 | NIST | SP 800-63B-4 | Compare new passwords against a blocklist that may include breach corpora; minimum length 15 for single-factor passwords; no forced periodic changes; no composition rules |

The direction is consistent. In 2012 a company could tell its users that their passwords had been stolen somewhere else. By 2020 a regulator treated an account opened with someone else's stolen password as the company's breach to report; by 2023 the FTC treated failure to stop it as an unfair practice; and by 2025 the ICO and the DFS named the specific controls they expected to find. Reuse is now treated as a risk the operator must anticipate, not as the customer's fault.

| Secure defaults | Make MFA the default and block single-factor password sign-in | Snowflake, 2024–2025 |

## What the record shows

Spills grew from one company's table of hashes to aggregations of billions of plain-text pairs and, since 2023, to logs taken from infected computers that name the site each password opens. The takeovers they enabled reach from coffee balances to genetic data and a telecom carrier's call records. In the record examined here, no organization named the spill that fed its takeover, because a reused password sits in most of them at once. What the cases share is simpler: a password accepted on its own, a second factor added afterwards, and, increasingly, a regulator willing to say that the operator should have expected it.

<ReaderQuestions>

- No company or regulator in this record named the spill behind a takeover: would naming it help people check their exposure, or only advertise the source?
- When a platform allows single-factor password sign-in by default and its customers are breached with stolen passwords, how should responsibility be shared between the platform, the customer and the contractor whose laptop was infected?
- Who should verify how much of a "16 billion credentials" compilation is new before the number becomes a headline, and what evidence should the first report be expected to publish?
- Infostealers capture passwords in plain text and steal session cookies that survive MFA; how much of that defence can a service provide, and how much belongs to the owner or employer of the infected device?
- Regulators now expect MFA and breached-password screening; should those become explicit legal duties for any service holding stored value, payment methods or sensitive data, rather than inferences from "reasonable security"?
- Passkeys remove the shared secret, yet most services keep a password as a fallback: what would it take to retire the fallback?

</ReaderQuestions>
