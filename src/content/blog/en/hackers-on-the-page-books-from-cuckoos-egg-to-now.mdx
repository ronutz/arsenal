A 1984 history gave hacker culture the phrase "the hacker ethic" ([O'Reilly Media, Hackers, 25th anniversary edition, May 2010](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/-/9781449390259/)), a 1989 memoir began with a 75-cent accounting error ([Publishers Weekly, Cuckoo's Egg, reviewed 1 September 1989](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780385249461)), and science fiction supplied the words worm, cyberspace and metaverse ([Spafford, The Internet Worm Incident, 19 September 1991](https://www.cerias.purdue.edu/techreports-ssl/public/933.pdf); [Merriam-Webster, cyberspace](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/cyberspace); [Merriam-Webster, What is the metaverse?](https://www.merriam-webster.com/wordplay/meaning-of-metaverse)). This post is the third of a set: films, series and documentaries are in **[Hackers on screen](/blog/hackers-on-screen-films-series-and-documentaries)**, and games, capture the flag and cyber ranges in **[Hackers at play](/blog/hackers-at-play-games-ctfs-and-cyber-ranges)**. Where a book became a film, this post gives the book and the screen post gives the film.

The story of the 75 cents itself is told in the Learn article **[The Cuckoo's Egg](/learn/the-cuckoos-egg)**, and the people behind most of these books in **[Hacking in the 20th century](/learn/hacking-in-the-20th-century)**. Here the subject is the books: who wrote and published them, what they cover, how they have aged, what they changed, and which of them reached Brazilian readers in Portuguese.

## How the list was built

Every entry was checked on 16 November 2026 against at least one of these: the publisher's own catalogue page, the author's own site, a dated trade review in *Publishers Weekly* or *Kirkus Reviews*, an awards database, or a court or government record. Brazilian editions are listed only where the Brazilian publisher's own page (or, for one translation, the author's page) confirms them; where only a bookseller lists an edition, the post says so. No Brazilian edition was found on the publishers' pages read for several classics, among them *The Cuckoo's Egg*, *Hackers* and *Countdown to Zero Day*, and those keep their original titles here; that is a statement about what was checked, not proof that no translation exists.

Reviewers are quoted for what they said at the time, including when they were wrong or harsh. The post takes no side among them.

**How "aged" is judged.** For narrative books, age is not a defect: a history of the 1980s describes the 1980s. For the practitioner's shelf, where readers want to know whether a handbook still teaches current practice, each title gets one of four labels, all of them observable on the publisher's or author's own page:

- **Kept current**: a new edition, update or free online version from the author or publisher dated 2016 or later.
- **Last revised before 2016**: still sold or offered free by its publisher or author, with examples as old as the last revision.
- **Replaced by its makers**: the author or publisher points readers to a successor.
- **Out of print**: the publisher's page says so.

A title with no page to check would be marked "not rated"; none of the handbooks below needed that label.

## The first shelf: histories and journalism, 1984 to 1997

**Hackers: Heroes of the Computer Revolution.** Steven Levy's book came out from Anchor/Doubleday on 9 November 1984, and *Kirkus* found that what begins as character sketches of the MIT (Massachusetts Institute of Technology) programmers on the ninth floor of Tech Square becomes "an interesting exercise in technological history and cultural change" ([Kirkus Reviews, Hackers: Heroes of the Computer Revolution](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/a/steven-levy-4/hackers-heroes-of-the-computer-revolution/)). Its lasting contribution is a phrase: its publisher still describes the people in it as sharing "the hacker ethic," and the 25th anniversary edition of May 2010 added updated material from Bill Gates, Mark Zuckerberg, Richard Stallman and Steve Wozniak and two afterwords, "Ten Years After" and "2010" ([O'Reilly Media, Hackers, 25th anniversary edition, May 2010](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/-/9781449390259/)). The glossary has an entry for the book: **[Hackers: Heroes of the Computer Revolution](/glossary/hackers-heroes-book)**.

**The Cuckoo's Egg.** Clifford Stoll's account was published by Doubleday in October 1989 at 326 pages; *Publishers Weekly* summed up the 75-cent billing discrepancy, the hunt for an intruder who reached "an estimated 30 of the 450 systems he attacked", and the identification of Markus Hess of Hanover, and noted that it was a Literary Guild selection ([Publishers Weekly, Cuckoo's Egg, reviewed 1 September 1989](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780385249461)). In 2015 the Cybersecurity Canon, a hall of fame for cybersecurity books whose advisory board brings together researchers, industry professionals and journalists, inducted it in its "Cyber Espionage" category ([Palo Alto Networks, Congratulations to this year's Cybersecurity Canon honorees, 2 April 2015](https://www.paloaltonetworks.com/blog/2015/04/congratulations-to-this-years-cybersecurity-canon-honorees/)). The investigation, the honeypot and what they taught incident response are in the Learn article **[The Cuckoo's Egg](/learn/the-cuckoos-egg)**, and the German side of the case in **[The Chaos Computer Club, the Btx heist, and the hack that ended in a forest](/learn/chaos-computer-club-btx-and-the-kgb)**; the television documentary made from the case is in the screen post.

**Cyberpunk: Outlaws and Hackers on the Computer Frontier.** Katie Hafner and John Markoff's book, from Simon & Schuster in 1991, collects three pieces of journalism on headline hacker cases; *Publishers Weekly* was cool on the title's promise and thought only one section, "Pengo and the Project Equalizer," about a West Berlin hacker, really earned it ([Publishers Weekly, Cyberpunk, reviewed 1 July 1991](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780671683221)). Glossary: **[Cyberpunk (Hafner and Markoff, 1991)](/glossary/cyberpunk-book)** and **[the KGB hack](/glossary/the-kgb-hack)**.

**The Hacker Crackdown.** Bruce Sterling, already a cyberpunk novelist, published his reporting on the 1990 raids with Bantam in 1992; *Publishers Weekly* called it "by far the most stylish report from the computer outlaw culture since Steven Levy's Hackers" and noted that it opens with the 15 January 1990 crash of AT&T's long-distance switching system ([Publishers Weekly, The Hacker Crackdown, reviewed 28 September 1992](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780553080582)). Project Gutenberg has carried it as eBook 101 since 1 January 1994 ([Project Gutenberg, The Hacker Crackdown](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/101)), because Sterling made the electronic text free, as the glossary entry **[The Hacker Crackdown](/glossary/the-hacker-crackdown)** explains; the trial at its centre is **[the E911 case](/glossary/the-e911-case)**, and the raids are in **[LOD, MOD and Operation Sundevil](/learn/lod-mod-and-operation-sundevil)**.

**Masters of Deception.** Michelle Slatalla and Joshua Quittner, reporters at *New York Newsday*, told the feud between the New York group Masters of Deception (MOD) and the Texas-based Legion of Doom (LOD) for HarperCollins in 1995; *Publishers Weekly* recorded that the Secret Service used "unprecedented authorized datataps" to bring MOD down in 1992, that four of its members received sentences of six months to a year, and that the first serial rights went to *Wired* ([Publishers Weekly, Masters of Deception, reviewed 2 January 1995](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780060170301)). Glossary: **[Masters of Deception](/glossary/masters-of-deception)**.

**Takedown and The Fugitive Game.** Two books on the pursuit of Kevin Mitnick came out within weeks of each other in early 1996. *Takedown*, by Tsutomu Shimomura with the *New York Times* reporter John Markoff, was published by Hyperion with a first printing of 100,000; its film rights had gone to Miramax and its foreign rights to 13 countries, Brazil among them ([Publishers Weekly, Take-Down, reviewed 29 January 1996](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780786862108)). The same reviewer called the authors "such interested parties" that readers should turn to Jonathan Littman's *The Fugitive Game*, from Little, Brown, for "a more disinterested account" ([Publishers Weekly, Take-Down](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780786862108)); Littman's book, drawn partly from Mitnick's calls to him while he was a fugitive, disputes several points of *Takedown*, and its foreign rights went to Japan and Brazil ([Publishers Weekly, The Fugitive Game, reviewed 1 January 1996](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780316528580)). The dispute, and what the case actually decided, are in **[Kevin Mitnick: the myth, the man, and what the case actually decided](/learn/kevin-mitnick-the-myth-and-the-man)**; the film made from *Takedown* is in the screen post.

**Underground.** Suelette Dreyfus wrote it "with research by Julian Assange", and Random House Australia published it in June 1997 at 475 pages; the book's own site offers the complete text as a free download and describes it as the first book published in Australia about the international computer hacker ([Underground, official book site](http://www.underground-book.net/); [Underground, About](http://www.underground-book.net/about.html)). Based on more than two years of research, it follows hackers from 8LGM, The Realm and the publishers of International Subversive, who gathered online from Australia, Europe, the United Kingdom, the United States and Canada, through the investigations of the Australian Federal Police, the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), the Secret Service, France's security service and Scotland Yard, and the trials that followed ([Underground, About](http://www.underground-book.net/about.html)). The Australian side of that world is in **[The WANK worm and the International Subversives](/learn/wank-worm-and-the-international-subversives)**. Glossary: **[Underground (Dreyfus and Assange, 1997)](/glossary/underground-book)**.

## Memoirs and first-person accounts

**Three of Kevin Mitnick's books.** *The Art of Deception*, written with William L. Simon and published by Wiley in 2002, is a set of social engineering tales; *Publishers Weekly* thought that as a manual for a would-be hacker it was "dated and nonspecific" but that "it teaches the timeless spirit of the hack" ([Publishers Weekly, The Art of Deception, reviewed 24 June 2002](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780471237129)). *The Art of Intrusion* (Wiley, 2005) collects other people's exploits under pseudonyms, among them a group that took more than a million dollars from Las Vegas casinos by mastering the patterns of slot machines ([Publishers Weekly, The Art of Intrusion, reviewed 14 February 2005](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780764569593)). *Ghost in the Wires* (Little, Brown, 2011) is his own story, from the intrusions into Sun Microsystems and Digital Equipment Corporation to the manhunt, and its reviewer found the heart of the book in his "social engineering" ([Publishers Weekly, Ghost in the Wires, reviewed 18 April 2011](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780316037709)). The method is explained in **[Social engineering: pretexting, vishing and baiting](/learn/social-engineering-pretexting-vishing-and-baiting)**; glossary: **[The Art of Deception](/glossary/the-art-of-deception)**, **[Ghost in the Wires](/glossary/ghost-in-the-wires)**, **[Kevin Mitnick](/glossary/kevin-mitnick)**.

**Hacking the Xbox.** Andrew "bunnie" Huang's book tells, among other things, how as an MIT graduate student he extracted security keys from the original Microsoft Xbox and received a letter from MIT's lawyers repudiating any association with the work, while his laboratory's faculty vowed to publish it as an official memo of the artificial intelligence (AI) laboratory ([No Starch Press, Hacking the Xbox, free ebook and open letter](https://nostarch.com/xboxfree)). He and No Starch Press later released a free ebook edition "in honor of Aaron Swartz", writing that Swartz's treatment by MIT was not unfamiliar to him ([No Starch Press, Hacking the Xbox](https://nostarch.com/xboxfree)). Glossary: **[bunnie Huang](/glossary/bunnie-huang)** and **[Aaron Swartz](/glossary/aaron-swartz)**.

**Permanent Record.** Edward Snowden's memoir was published by Metropolitan in September 2019; *Publishers Weekly* found the portrait of intelligence work well observed and his case against the surveillance state "less convincing" ([Publishers Weekly, Permanent Record, reviewed 17 September 2019](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9781250237231)). On 17 September 2019 the United States sued him, saying the suit did "not seek to stop or restrict the publication or distribution" of the book but the proceeds, because he had not submitted it for pre-publication review under his agreements with the Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) and the National Security Agency (NSA) ([US Department of Justice, 17 September 2019](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/united-states-files-civil-lawsuit-against-edward-snowden-publishing-book-violation-cia-and)). The court in the Eastern District of Virginia found him liable in December 2019 and, on 29 September 2020, entered judgment "in an amount exceeding $5.2 million" with a constructive trust over further royalties ([US Department of Justice, 1 October 2020](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/united-states-obtains-final-judgment-and-permanent-injunction-against-edward-snowden)). In Brazil, Planeta published it as *Eterna vigilância* on 1 September 2019, at 288 pages ([Planeta, Eterna vigilância](https://www.planetadelivros.com.br/livro-eterna-vigilancia/305254)). Background: **[Surveillance capability and restraint](/learn/surveillance-capability-and-restraint)**; glossary: **[Edward Snowden](/glossary/edward-snowden)**; the documentaries and the feature film about Snowden are in the screen post.

**Two insiders on Cambridge Analytica.** Christopher Wylie's *Mindf\*ck* (Random House, 2019) recounts his work for SCL Group and Cambridge Analytica, in a debut *Publishers Weekly* called "impactful, if somewhat self-serving" ([Publishers Weekly, Mindf\*ck, reviewed 16 October 2019](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9781984854636)). Brittany Kaiser's *Targeted* (Harper, 2019) tells the same years from inside the company, which she joined as a consultant before becoming the protégée of chief executive Alexander Nix ([Publishers Weekly, Targeted, reviewed 22 October 2019](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780062965790)); HarperCollins Brasil publishes it as *Manipulados*, translated by Bruno Fiuza and Roberta Karr ([HarperCollins Brasil, Manipulados](https://harpercollins.com.br/products/manipulados-brittany-kaiser)). The firms and their methods are in **[Influence for hire](/learn/influence-for-hire-the-firms-that-sell-influence-operations)**; glossary: **[Cambridge Analytica](/glossary/cambridge-analytica)**; the documentary on the case is in the screen post.

**A warning label from a reviewer.** Not every first-person story is documented. Of Jeremy N. Smith's *Breaking and Entering* (Houghton Mifflin Harcourt, 2019), the life of an MIT-trained hacker known as Alien, *Publishers Weekly* wrote that it was "poorly sourced", presenting decades-old dialogue without documentation, and that the author conceded he had changed certain facts ([Publishers Weekly, Breaking and Entering, reviewed 26 November 2018](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780544903210)).

## Investigations of real campaigns, 2010 to 2024

From 2010 the cases grow larger: card fraud at industrial scale, hacktivist collectives, a cyberweapon aimed at centrifuges, a malware outbreak that spread around the world, and a market in zero-day exploits.

**Kingpin.** Kevin Poulsen, who had served five years in prison for hacking before becoming a *Wired* editor, wrote the story of Max Butler, who stole access to 1.8 million credit card accounts and was serving a 13-year sentence with 27.5 million dollars in restitution when the book appeared from Crown ([Publishers Weekly, Kingpin, reviewed 20 December 2010](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780307588685)). The Cybersecurity Canon inducted it in 2016 ([Palo Alto Networks, Rejoice! Eight New Books Inducted into the Cybersecurity Canon, 5 April 2016](https://www.paloaltonetworks.com/blog/2016/04/ignite-canon)). Poulsen's own story is in **[Kevin Poulsen: the man who won a Porsche by owning the phone company](/learn/kevin-poulsen-dark-dante)**.

**We Are Anonymous and Hacker, Hoaxer, Whistleblower, Spy.** Parmy Olson's book (Little, Brown, 2012) traces Anonymous and LulzSec from the 2008 campaign against the Church of Scientology to the arrests, drawing on interviews including Hector "Sabu" Monsegur ([Publishers Weekly, We Are Anonymous, reviewed 18 June 2012](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780316213530)); it was the first book the Cybersecurity Canon inducted, in 2014 ([Palo Alto Networks, 5 April 2016](https://www.paloaltonetworks.com/blog/2016/04/ignite-canon)). The anthropologist Gabriella Coleman's account (Verso, 2014) is an ethnography built from interviews, chat logs and leaked documents ([Publishers Weekly, Hacker, Hoaxer, Whistleblower, Spy, reviewed 22 September 2014](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9781781685839)). The decade is in **[Anonymous, LulzSec and the hacktivist decade](/learn/anonymous-lulzsec-and-the-hacktivist-decade)**.

**Countdown to Zero Day.** Kim Zetter, then a senior writer at *Wired*, published the book-length account of Stuxnet with Crown in 2014; *Publishers Weekly* reported her judgment that unleashing the weapon was "remarkably reckless" ([Publishers Weekly, Countdown to Zero Day, reviewed 8 September 2014](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780770436179)), and the Cybersecurity Canon inducted it in 2015 under "Cyber Warfare" ([Palo Alto Networks, 2 April 2015](https://www.paloaltonetworks.com/blog/2015/04/congratulations-to-this-years-cybersecurity-canon-honorees/)). The case: **[Stuxnet: the first cyberweapon](/learn/stuxnet-and-the-first-cyberweapon)**; glossary: **[Countdown to Zero Day](/glossary/countdown-to-zero-day)**.

**Spam Nation.** Brian Krebs, a former *Washington Post* reporter, followed the spam, botnet and rogue pharmacy business back to operators sheltering in former Soviet states (Sourcebooks, 2014) ([Publishers Weekly, Spam Nation, reviewed 25 August 2014](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9781402295614)); the Canon inducted it in 2015 under "Cyber Crime" ([Palo Alto Networks, 2 April 2015](https://www.paloaltonetworks.com/blog/2015/04/congratulations-to-this-years-cybersecurity-canon-honorees/)).

**No Place to Hide.** Glenn Greenwald's account of the Snowden documents (Metropolitan, 2014) struck *Publishers Weekly* as an "exciting if overwrought exposé" ([Publishers Weekly, No Place to Hide, reviewed 26 May 2014](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9781627790734)). No Brazilian publisher's page confirming a Portuguese edition was read for this post.

**Dark Territory.** Fred Kaplan's history of cyber war (Simon & Schuster, March 2016) opens, in *Kirkus*'s summary, with President Ronald Reagan watching *WarGames* and then commissioning a 1984 directive that gave the NSA responsibility for securing computer networks ([Kirkus Reviews, Dark Territory, 4 January 2016](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/fred-kaplan/dark-territory/)). The documents behind that story are set out in the screen post, and the 1983 hearings in **[The 414s and the summer of 1983](/learn/the-414s-and-the-summer-of-1983)**.

**American Kingpin and Tracers in the Dark.** Nick Bilton's account of Ross Ulbricht and the Silk Road was published by Portfolio on 2 May 2017 ([Kirkus Reviews, American Kingpin, 6 March 2017](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/nick-bilton/american-kingpin/)). Andy Greenberg's *Tracers in the Dark* (Doubleday, 2022) starts from the same takedown in 2013 and follows the investigators who learned to trace cryptocurrency ([Publishers Weekly, Tracers in the Dark, reviewed 7 September 2022](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780385548090)). The case: **[The Silk Road](/learn/the-silk-road-and-the-operator-who-leaked)**.

**The Perfect Weapon.** David Sanger, a Pulitzer-winning *New York Times* correspondent, surveyed state hacking from Stuxnet to the 2016 election (Crown, 2018); *Publishers Weekly* thought the investigation "perhaps overly worried" ([Publishers Weekly, The Perfect Weapon, reviewed 11 June 2018](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780451497895)).

**Cult of the Dead Cow.** Joseph Menn's history of the group that began among teenagers in Lubbock, Texas, in the mid-1980s and pushed "hacktivism" was published by PublicAffairs in 2019 ([Publishers Weekly, Cult of the Dead Cow, reviewed 24 January 2019](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9781541762381)). The group: **[L0pht, cDc, and the year the underground walked into the Senate](/learn/l0pht-cdc-and-the-road-to-the-industry)**; glossary: **[Cult of the Dead Cow](/glossary/cult-of-the-dead-cow)**.

**Sandworm.** Andy Greenberg's investigation (Doubleday, 5 November 2019) follows the unit of Russia's military intelligence agency, the GRU, named after references to the novel *Dune* found in its code, from the December 2015 attack on Ukraine's power grid to NotPetya in June 2017 ([Publishers Weekly, Sandworm, reviewed 13 September 2019](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780385544405); [Kirkus Reviews, Sandworm, 9 September 2019](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/andy-greenberg/sandworm/)). In January 2026 the Cybersecurity Canon named it among its Hall of Fame selections ([Cybersecurity Canon, CyberCanon 2026, 15 January 2026](https://cybercanon.substack.com/p/cybercanon-2026)). The outbreak: **[EternalBlue, WannaCry, NotPetya](/learn/eternalblue-wannacry-notpetya)**; glossary: **[NotPetya](/glossary/notpetya)**.

**This Is How They Tell Me the World Ends.** Nicole Perlroth's book on the market in zero-day exploits (Bloomsbury, February 2021) drew a mixed trade review: *Publishers Weekly* praised her account of American hubris in creating the market and of the 2016 Shadow Brokers leak, but found it left "many technical details about cyberweapons unexplained" ([Publishers Weekly, This Is How They Tell Me the World Ends, reviewed 9 November 2020](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9781635576054)). The bookseller Martins Fontes Paulista lists a Brazilian first edition of 2023 from Arquipélago, *É assim que me disseram que o mundo acaba*, at 528 pages ([Martins Fontes Paulista, catalogue entry](https://www.martinsfontespaulista.com.br/e-assim-que-me-disseram-que-o-mundo-acaba-1070651/p)); the publisher's own page could not be read. Glossary: **[zero-day](/glossary/zero-day)** and **[the Shadow Brokers](/glossary/shadow-brokers)**.

**Fancy Bear Goes Phishing.** Scott Shapiro, who directs the cybersecurity lab at Yale's Center for Law and Philosophy, retells five hacks, from the 1988 worm to the Democratic National Committee breach of 2016 (Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2023) ([Publishers Weekly, Fancy Bear Goes Phishing, reviewed 31 January 2023](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780374601171)). The first of the five is in **[The Morris Worm](/learn/the-morris-worm)**.

**Dark Wire.** Joseph Cox, a co-founder of 404 Media, tells how investigators broke the encrypted phone company Phantom Secure and how the FBI then ran its own phone company, Anom (PublicAffairs, 4 June 2024, 352 pages) ([Kirkus Reviews, Dark Wire, 20 April 2024](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/joseph-cox/dark-wire/); [Hachette Book Group, Dark Wire](https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/titles/joseph-cox/dark-wire/9781541702691/)).

**The Idealist and Exploding the Phone.** Two books go further back for their roots. Justin Peters set Aaron Swartz's life inside the longer history of copyright and free culture (Scribner, 2016) ([Publishers Weekly, The Idealist, reviewed 9 November 2015](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9781476767727)). Phil Lapsley's history of the phone phreaks (Grove, 2013) begins, in *Kirkus*'s summary, with David Condon, who in 1955 found that a Davy Crockett whistle produced the 2,600 hertz tone that the long-distance switching machines used to signal each other ([Kirkus Reviews, Exploding the Phone, 8 December 2012](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/phil-lapsley/exploding-the-phone/)), and *Publishers Weekly* noted that it draws on interviews with phreaks, FBI agents and former telephone company staff, with some names changed ([Publishers Weekly, Exploding the Phone, reviewed 22 October 2012](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780802120618)). The network and its tones: **[Phreaking: the network that whistled its own passwords](/learn/phone-phreaking-and-the-blue-box)**.

**WikiLeaks, in Portuguese.** The Guardian's investigations team, led by David Leigh with Luke Harding, wrote the book that Record published in Brazil on 23 February 2011 as *WikiLeaks: A guerra de Julian Assange contra os segredos de Estado*, translated by Ana Resende and Marcos Malvezzi Leal ([Record, WikiLeaks](https://www.record.com.br/products/wikileaks)). Glossary: **[Julian Assange](/glossary/julian-assange)**.

## Fiction: the novels that named things

Three words the field uses every day first appeared in fiction.

**The worm.** In John Brunner's *The Shockwave Rider* (Harper & Row, 1975), programs called tapeworms live inside computers and spread to other machines; when researchers at Xerox's Palo Alto Research Center (PARC) reported their own self-propagating programs in 1982, they cited Brunner as the inspiration for the name "worm", as Eugene Spafford recorded in his Purdue University report on the 1988 incident ([Spafford, The Internet Worm Incident, Purdue CSD-TR-933, 19 September 1991](https://www.cerias.purdue.edu/techreports-ssl/public/933.pdf)). *Kirkus* noted at the time that the novel acknowledged a debt to Alvin Toffler's *Future Shock* ([Kirkus Reviews, Shockwave Rider](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/john-brunner-4/shockwave-rider/)). Glossary: **[The Shockwave Rider](/glossary/the-shockwave-rider)**.

**True Names.** Vernor Vinge's novella appeared in *Binary Star* #5 in 1981 and was a Hugo and Nebula nominee; the Libertarian Futurist Society later added it to its Prometheus Hall of Fame ([Science Fiction Awards Database, Vernor Vinge](https://www.sfadb.com/Vernor_Vinge)). Tor reissued it in 2001 with essays by pioneers of virtual reality, cryptography and artificial intelligence, among them Marvin Minsky and Danny Hillis, and *Publishers Weekly* called it "the story that began SF's cyberpunk revolution" ([Publishers Weekly, True Names, reviewed 24 December 2001](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780312862077)).

**Cyberspace.** The word's first known use is 1982, in William Gibson's story "Burning Chrome" in the July issue of *Omni* ([Merriam-Webster, cyberspace](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/cyberspace); [Historical Dictionary of Science Fiction, cyberspace](https://sfdictionary.com/view/38/cyberspace)). *Neuromancer*, his first novel, published in 1984, was the first book to win the Hugo, Nebula and Philip K. Dick awards, a triple crown its Brazilian publisher Aleph still advertises ([Aleph, Neuromancer](https://editoraaleph.com.br/products/neuromancer); [Science Fiction Awards Database, William Gibson](https://www.sfadb.com/William_Gibson)). Aleph's current edition, translated by Fábio Fernandes, came out on 19 August 2016 ([Aleph, Neuromancer](https://editoraaleph.com.br/products/neuromancer)). Apple TV lists a series based on the novel for 22 January 2027, with Callum Turner as Case and Briana Middleton as Molly ([Apple TV Press, Neuromancer](https://www.apple.com/tv-pr/originals/neuromancer/)). Glossary: **[Neuromancer](/glossary/neuromancer)**.

**The metaverse.** Neal Stephenson coined the term in *Snow Crash*, his 1992 novel, Merriam-Webster notes ([Merriam-Webster, What is the metaverse?](https://www.merriam-webster.com/wordplay/meaning-of-metaverse)); *Kirkus*, reviewing the Bantam edition of 15 May 1992, was unimpressed by the characters but described the "computer-generated 'metaverse'" populated by hackers ([Kirkus Reviews, Snow Crash](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/neal-stephenson/snow-crash/)). Aleph, which first published it in Brazil in 2008, issued its current edition, translated by Fábio Fernandes, on 5 July 2022 ([Aleph, Snow Crash](https://editoraaleph.com.br/products/snow-crash)). Glossary: **[Snow Crash](/glossary/snow-crash)**.

**Cryptonomicon and a real cipher.** Stephenson's 1999 novel (Avon) runs a Second World War codebreaking story beside a present-day one about a data haven ([Publishers Weekly, Cryptonomicon, reviewed 4 January 1999](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780380973460); [Kirkus Reviews, Cryptonomicon](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/neal-stephenson/cryptonomicon/)). Its hand cipher is real: Bruce Schneier designed Solitaire, which appears in the novel under the code name Pontifex, so that field agents could communicate securely without electronics, and he warns on its page that "Solitaire is only as secure as the key" ([Bruce Schneier, The Solitaire Encryption Algorithm, version 1.2, 26 May 1999](https://www.schneier.com/academic/solitaire/)). It won the Locus Award for science fiction novel in 2000 and entered the Prometheus Hall of Fame in 2013 ([Science Fiction Awards Database, Neal Stephenson](https://www.sfadb.com/Neal_Stephenson)).

**The 1990s and 2000s thrillers.** Pat Cadigan's *Synners* won the Arthur C. Clarke Award in 1992 ([Science Fiction Awards Database, Pat Cadigan](https://www.sfadb.com/Pat_Cadigan)). Dan Brown's first novel, *Digital Fortress* (Dunne/St. Martin's, 1998), imagines an NSA supercomputer that breaks any encryption by brute force in an hour or two; *Kirkus* called it a technothriller "less improbable than some" ([Kirkus Reviews, Digital Fortress](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/dan-brown/digital-fortress/)); its Brazilian publisher's page could not be read. Stieg Larsson's *The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo*, published in English by Knopf in 2008 with a first printing of 100,000, introduced Lisbeth Salander, whom *Publishers Weekly* called a "feral but vulnerable superhacker" ([Publishers Weekly, The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo, reviewed 14 July 2008](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780307269751)); in Brazil it is *Os homens que não amavam as mulheres* from Companhia das Letras, translated by Paulo Neves ([Companhia das Letras, Os homens que não amavam as mulheres](https://www.companhiadasletras.com.br/livro/9788535926163/os-homens-que-nao-amavam-as-mulheres)), and the Swedish film adaptation is in the screen post. Charles Stross's *Halting State* (Ace, 2007) is a heist in a virtual world written in the second person of game instructions ([Publishers Weekly, Halting State, reviewed 13 August 2007](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780441014989)).

**Little Brother.** Cory Doctorow's novel for teenagers (Tor, 2008) has a high-school student fight a surveillance crackdown after a terrorist attack on San Francisco, with what *Publishers Weekly* called "detailed descriptions of how to counteract gait-recognition cameras, arphids (radio frequency ID tags)" and other devices ([Publishers Weekly, Little Brother, reviewed 14 April 2008](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780765319852)). It was a Hugo and Nebula nominee and won the Prometheus Award and, jointly, the John W. Campbell Memorial Award in 2009 ([Science Fiction Awards Database, Cory Doctorow](https://www.sfadb.com/Cory_Doctorow)), and the author offers it as a free download on his site ([Cory Doctorow, Little Brother](https://craphound.com/littlebrother/)). Record published it in Brazil as *Pequeno irmão* on 30 June 2011, translated by André Gordirro ([Record, Pequeno irmão](https://www.record.com.br/products/pequeno-irmao)).

**Novels by people who build systems.** Daniel Suarez's *Daemon*, in which a dead game designer's program takes over many of the world's computer systems, had been self-published before Dutton brought it out ([Publishers Weekly, Daemon, reviewed 6 October 2008](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780525951117)). Mark Russinovich, described by *Publishers Weekly* as a "Microsoft computer guru", published *Zero Day* with St. Martin's/Dunne in 2011, with a blurb from Bill Gates ([Publishers Weekly, Zero Day, reviewed 10 January 2011](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780312612467)); in 2016 an open internet vote run by the Cybersecurity Canon made it the "clear and popular winner" of the year's inductions ([Palo Alto Networks, 5 April 2016](https://www.paloaltonetworks.com/blog/2016/04/ignite-canon)). P. W. Singer and August Cole's *Ghost Fleet* (Houghton Mifflin Harcourt, 2015) carries endnotes documenting the real technologies behind its war ([Publishers Weekly, Ghost Fleet, reviewed 6 April 2015](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780544142848)). Marc Elsberg's German thriller *Blackout*, in which a computer scientist suspects a hacker attack behind the collapse of Europe's power grids, was chosen by the magazine bild der wissenschaft as science book of the year 2012 in the entertainment category, has sold more than two million copies in more than twenty languages by its author's count, and became a six-part series on Joyn and Sat.1, shown on free television in January 2023 ([Marc Elsberg, Blackout](https://marcelsberg.com/buecher?isbn=9783442380299); [Marc Elsberg, Verfilmung](https://marcelsberg.com/verfilmung)).

**Ghost in the Shell, in Portuguese.** Masamune Shirow's manga, whose Major Motoko hunts a hacker called the Puppet Master, has a Brazilian first volume from JBC dated 28 November 2025, at 352 pages ([Companhia das Letras, JBC imprint, The Ghost in the Shell vol. 1](https://www.companhiadasletras.com.br/livro/9788545702320/the-ghost-in-the-shell-vol-1)).

## The practitioner's shelf

These are handbooks and reference works. The label after each title applies the rule set out at the top.

**Practical UNIX Security** (Simson Garfinkel and Gene Spafford). O'Reilly's page says that when it was first published "more than a decade ago, it became an instant classic", that the second edition added internet security and doubled its size, and that the third edition, with Alan Schwartz, dates from February 2003 ([O'Reilly Media, Practical UNIX and Internet Security, 3rd edition](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/practical-unix-and/0596003234/)). *Last revised before 2016.*

**Firewalls and Internet Security: Repelling the Wily Hacker** (William Cheswick and Steven Bellovin). In the second edition, written with Aviel Rubin, the authors recall: "The first printing of the First Edition appeared at the Las Vegas Interop in May, 1994." ([Cheswick, Bellovin and Rubin, Preface to the Second Edition](https://www.wilyhacker.com/preface2e.pdf)); the publisher's page for that edition, of February 2003, says the first sold more than 68,000 copies and that the book is no longer in print ([InformIT, Firewalls and Internet Security, 2nd edition](https://www.informit.com/store/firewalls-and-internet-security-repelling-the-wily-hacker-9780201634662)). The authors now offer the full second edition and its LaTeX source under a Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial-NoDerivatives 4.0 licence, including the chapter "An Evening with Berferd" ([Firewalls and Internet Security, official site](https://www.wilyhacker.com/)). *Out of print; free from the authors.* The history of the device is in **[The Firewall: birth, generations, and what comes after the perimeter](/learn/firewall-family-history)**.

**TCP/IP Illustrated, Volume 1** (W. Richard Stevens). The second edition, by Kevin Fall, appeared on 15 November 2011 and builds, in its publisher's words, on "the late W. Richard Stevens' classic first edition" ([InformIT, TCP/IP Illustrated, Volume 1, 2nd edition](https://www.informit.com/store/tcp-ip-illustrated-volume-1-the-protocols-9780321336316)). *Last revised before 2016.*

**Applied Cryptography** (Bruce Schneier). The first edition was published by Wiley in 1994 (ISBN 0-471-59756-2), a second edition followed, and a 20th anniversary hardcover is listed ([Bruce Schneier, Applied Cryptography](https://www.schneier.com/books/applied-cryptography/); [Phil Karn, The Applied Cryptography Case](http://www.ka9q.net/export/)). One verdict on it is the author's own: in the preface to *Secrets and Lies* (2000), Schneier wrote that "The error of Applied Cryptography is that I didn't talk at all about the context" ([Bruce Schneier, Secrets and Lies, preface](https://www.schneier.com/books/secrets-and-lies/pref.html)). *Last revised before 2016.*

**"Smashing The Stack For Fun And Profit"** (Aleph One). Not a book but an article, file 14 of *Phrack* issue 49, dated 8 November 1996 ([Phrack 49, index](http://phrack.org/archives/issues/49/1.txt); [Phrack 49, file 14](http://phrack.org/archives/issues/49/14.txt)). Glossary: **[Aleph One](/glossary/aleph-one)** and **[buffer overflow](/glossary/buffer-overflow)**; the magazine: **[Phrack, 2600 and the underground press](/learn/phrack-2600-and-the-hacker-manifesto)**.

**Silence on the Wire** (Michal Zalewski). A field guide to passive reconnaissance and indirect attacks, April 2005 ([No Starch Press, Silence on the Wire](https://nostarch.com/silence.htm)). *Last revised before 2016.*

**The Art of Software Security Assessment** (Mark Dowd, John McDonald and Justin Schuh). Published on 20 November 2006 at 1,200 pages; its publisher's page now reads: "Sorry, this book is no longer in print." ([InformIT, The Art of Software Security Assessment](https://www.informit.com/store/art-of-software-security-assessment-identifying-and-9780321444424)). *Out of print.*

**Nmap Network Scanning** (Gordon "Fyodor" Lyon). ## When the book itself was the case

Some of these books ended up as evidence, exhibits or test cases.

- **A book could leave the country; the same code on a floppy disk could not.** In 1994 Phil Karn asked the State Department to rule on *Applied Cryptography* and on a disk holding the source code printed in it; the department found the book exportable and designated the disk a "defense article under category XIII(b)(1) of the United States Munitions List" ([Phil Karn, The Applied Cryptography Case](http://www.ka9q.net/export/)). Karn let his case be dismissed as moot after export rules changed on 14 January 2000 ([Phil Karn, 21 June 2000](http://www.ka9q.net/export/)). The wider fight, including the source code of PGP (Pretty Good Privacy) printed as a book, is in **[The Crypto Wars: when encryption was a munition](/learn/the-crypto-wars-and-the-cypherpunks)**.
- **A memoir's proceeds went to the government.** The Snowden judgment of September 2020 described above left *Permanent Record* in print and gave the United States its earnings ([US Department of Justice, 1 October 2020](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/united-states-obtains-final-judgment-and-permanent-injunction-against-edward-snowden)).
- **A book was given away as a protest.** *Hacking the Xbox* became a free ebook in honour of Aaron Swartz ([No Starch Press](https://nostarch.com/xboxfree)).
- **An author gave away his own electronic rights.** *The Hacker Crackdown* has been free text since 1 January 1994 ([Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/101)).

## Where to start

**If you are curious.** Start with *The Cuckoo's Egg*, which needs no technical background ([Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780385249461)), then one modern investigation: *Countdown to Zero Day* for Stuxnet or *Sandworm* for NotPetya ([Publishers Weekly, Countdown](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780770436179); [Publishers Weekly, Sandworm](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780385544405)). For fiction, *Neuromancer*, available in Portuguese from Aleph ([Aleph](https://editoraaleph.com.br/products/neuromancer)), and *Little Brother*, free from its author and in Portuguese from Record ([Cory Doctorow](https://craphound.com/littlebrother/); [Record](https://www.record.com.br/products/pequeno-irmao)). For Brazil, *Storm* ([Companhia das Letras](https://www.companhiadasletras.com.br/9788539007387-storm/p)).

**If you are a student.** Read the history first, *The Hacker Crackdown*, free online, and *Exploding the Phone* ([Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/101); [Kirkus](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/phil-lapsley/exploding-the-phone/)). Then the foundations: *TCP/IP Illustrated* for the protocols ([InformIT](https://www.informit.com/store/tcp-ip-illustrated-volume-1-the-protocols-9780321336316)), *Practical Packet Analysis* or Eriberto's *Análise de tráfego em redes TCP/IP* for packets ([No Starch](https://nostarch.com/packetanalysis3); [Novatec](https://s3.novatec.com.br/capitulos/capitulo-9788575223758.pdf)), *Hacking: The Art of Exploitation* for memory and exploits ([No Starch](https://nostarch.com/hacking2.htm)), and the free chapters of *Security Engineering* for how systems fail ([Ross Anderson](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/archive/rja14/book.html)).

**If you already work in the field.** The books the Cybersecurity Canon chose are a practitioner's list by design ([Palo Alto Networks, 5 April 2016](https://www.paloaltonetworks.com/blog/2016/04/ignite-canon)). For current technique, the handbooks labelled "kept current" above, and for web testing PortSwigger's free Academy, which PortSwigger built instead of a third edition of the handbook ([PortSwigger](https://portswigger.net/web-security/web-application-hackers-handbook)). For perspective, *Fancy Bear Goes Phishing* ([Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780374601171)) and *This Is How They Tell Me the World Ends* ([Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9781635576054)), with their reviewers' reservations in mind.
