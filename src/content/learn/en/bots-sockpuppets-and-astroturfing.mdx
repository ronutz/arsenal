A follower count, a trending topic, a row of five-star reviews and a crowd of replies all say the same thing to a reader: many people think this. Each can be manufactured. Software can run accounts on its own, one person can operate a dozen identities, and a campaign or a company can pay for activity that looks spontaneous. This part of the series opened by [the map from the small lie to the campaign](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign) sets out the words for these practices, what the platforms forbid, what courts and regulators have found, what the researchers who try to count bots have measured and why their counts clash, and what the Brazilian record shows. The tactics fit the behaviour component and the Establish Assets and Establish Legitimacy tactics described in [the anatomy of an influence operation](/learn/anatomy-of-an-influence-operation-abc-disarm-and-kill-chains); the reason they work at all is in [the human attack surface](/learn/the-human-attack-surface-why-repetition-works), where social proof and repetition do the persuading.

## Four words for manufactured numbers

**Social bots.** Emilio Ferrara, Onur Varol, Clayton Davis, Filippo Menczer and Alessandro Flammini, then at Indiana University, wrote that "A social bot is a computer algorithm that automatically produces content and interacts with humans on social media, trying to emulate and possibly alter their behavior" ([Ferrara et al., arXiv version of 6 March 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1407.5225)). They stress that "many bots are benign", that harmful ones can be designed for "persuading, smearing, or deceiving", and that the abundance of social bots "is uncertain". The novelty, in their account, is not falsehood itself but that bots "can give the false impression that some piece of information, regardless of its accuracy, is highly popular and endorsed by many".

**Cyborgs.** Between the program and the person sits the hybrid. Stefano Cresci, of Italy's National Research Council, records that the existence of accounts driven at once by algorithms and by people led researchers to define "cyborgs" as "either bot-assisted humans or human-assisted bots" ([Cresci, A Decade of Social Bot Detection, 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.03604)). Ferrara and colleagues had warned earlier that detecting accounts that mix human and bot features, or hacked accounts, "is currently impossible for feature-based systems".

**Sockpuppets.** Srijan Kumar, Justin Cheng, Jure Leskovec and V. S. Subrahmanian, in a study of nine online discussion communities presented at the World Wide Web (WWW) conference of 2017, "broadly define a sockpuppet as a user account that is controlled by an individual (or puppetmaster) who controls at least one other user account" ([Kumar et al., 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.07355)). Using traces that are hard to fake, such as Internet Protocol (IP) addresses and session data, they identified 3,656 sockpuppets run by 1,623 puppetmasters. The definition is deliberately broad: it does not require intent to deceive, which other authors build in. The New York Attorney General's office, describing a fake-follower business in 2019, used the plainer phrase "one person pretending to be many other people" ([New York Attorney General, 30 January 2019](https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2019/attorney-general-james-announces-groundbreaking-settlement-sellers-fake-followers)).

**Astroturfing.** The name comes from a brand of artificial grass: fake grassroots. Ferrara and colleagues note that campaigns using bots to support some candidates and smear others "are sometimes referred to as astroturf or Twitter bombs" ([Ferrara et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1407.5225)). The University of Illinois, presenting research by Franziska Keller, David Schoch, Sebastian Stier and JungHwan Yang, describes astroturfing as a campaign that "fakes the appearance of organic grassroots participation while being secretly orchestrated and funded" ([University of Illinois News Bureau, 2019](https://news.illinois.edu/evidence-of-humans-not-bots-key-to-uncovering-disinformation-campaigns/)). Astroturfing is a goal; bots, sockpuppets and paid people are the means. A campaign can be astroturf with no bot in it.

The four words describe different things: who operates an account (software, a person, a mix), how many identities one operator holds, and what the activity pretends to be. Reports often blur them, and the blur matters later, when counts of "bots" turn out to include people.

## What the platforms forbid

Platforms do not regulate opinions under these headings; they regulate behaviour, each in its own vocabulary.

Meta views coordinated inauthentic behaviour (CIB) "as coordinated efforts to manipulate public debate for a strategic goal, in which fake accounts are central to the operation", in which "people coordinate with one another and use fake accounts to mislead others about who they are and what they are doing"; it says it focuses "on behavior rather than content", "no matter who's behind them, what they post or whether they're foreign or domestic" ([Meta, Adversarial Threat Report, fourth quarter 2022](https://about.fb.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Meta-Quarterly-Adversarial-Threat-Report-Q42022.pdf)). The same report covers mass reporting, networks that "coordinate to abuse our reporting systems" to get accounts or content taken down.

X's platform manipulation and spam policy, in its March 2023 version, says "We define platform manipulation as using X to engage in bulk, aggressive, or deceptive activity" and lists among violations "inauthentic engagements, that attempt to make accounts or content appear more popular or active than they are" and "coordinated activity, that attempts to artificially influence conversations" ([X Help Center, read through a fetch tool](https://help.x.com/en/rules-and-policies/platform-manipulation)). It also lists what is not a violation: "coordinating with others to express ideas, viewpoints, support, or opposition towards a cause" and "operating multiple accounts with distinct identities, purposes, or use cases".

YouTube "doesn't allow anything that artificially increases the number of views, likes, comments, or other metrics either by using automatic systems or serving up videos to unsuspecting viewers", and bans content that promotes third-party services that inflate metrics ([YouTube Help, fake engagement policy](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/3399767?hl=en)). TikTok says it does not allow accounts that "mislead or try to manipulate our platform", nor "the trade of services that artificially boost engagement or trick the recommendation system", naming "covert influence operations, impersonation, spam, fake reviews"; multiple accounts are allowed "but not to deceive others" ([TikTok Community Guidelines, integrity and authenticity](https://www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines/en/integrity-authenticity)).

| Platform | Its term | What counts | What it says is allowed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meta | Coordinated inauthentic behaviour | Coordinated use of fake accounts to mislead about who is behind an effort to manipulate debate | Judged on behaviour, not on the content posted |
| X | Platform manipulation | Bulk, aggressive or deceptive activity; inauthentic engagement; coordinated activity through fake or automated accounts | Coordinating openly for a cause; several accounts with distinct identities; hobby bots |
| YouTube | Fake engagement | Artificially raised views, likes, comments or other metrics | Not stated in the policy text read |
| TikTok | Deceptive behaviours and fake engagement | Manipulating the platform; trading services that boost engagement; covert influence operations | Multiple accounts for fan content or creative expression |

These are terms of service, not law. Each company decides, applies and reports on its own rule, which is one reason, as earlier parts noted, why researchers depend on the figures platforms choose to release.

## A market for fake engagement

### Devumi: the follower factory

In January 2018 The New York Times reported that an American company, Devumi, drawing on "an estimated stock of at least 3.5 million automated accounts, each sold many times over", had provided customers "with more than 200 million Twitter followers", and that <Claim status="reported" by="by The New York Times">"At least 55,000 of the accounts use the names, profile pictures, hometowns and other personal details of real Twitter users, including minors"</Claim> ([The New York Times, The Follower Factory, 27 January 2018, as republished by the Anchorage Daily News](https://www.adn.com/nation-world/2018/01/27/the-follower-factory-inside-social-medias-black-market/)). Its founder, German Calas, <Claim status="denied" by="by Devumi's founder">denied that his company sold fake followers</Claim>: "The allegations are false, and we do not have knowledge of any such activity", he wrote to the newspaper. The Times also found that Devumi did not appear to make its own bots but bought them "from a thriving global market of fake social media accounts".

A year later the New York Attorney General, Letitia James, announced a settlement with Devumi and related companies. Her office called it "the first finding by a law enforcement agency that selling fake social media engagement and using stolen identities to engage in online activity is illegal", and found that <Claim status="attributed" by="by the New York Attorney General">Devumi "supplied the fraudulent activity using bot and sock-puppet accounts" and in some instances copied real people's profiles, name and picture included, without consent</Claim>; Devumi had ceased operations in mid-2018, after the investigation began and publicity cut its sales ([New York Attorney General, 30 January 2019](https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2019/attorney-general-james-announces-groundbreaking-settlement-sellers-fake-followers)). The office listed whom the practice deceived: other users choosing what to pay attention to, buyers, advertisers, and "policymakers, voters, and journalists" judging which people and policies have public support. Some customers, it said, believed they were buying authentic endorsements; "many other Devumi customers knew they were buying fake activity".

In October 2019 the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) settled charges that Devumi and Calas sold "fake indicators of social media influence, including fake followers, subscribers, views, and likes". The FTC alleged that <Claim status="alleged" by="by the FTC">"Devumi filled more than 58,000 orders for fake Twitter followers"</Claim> and made more than 4,000 sales of fake YouTube subscribers and over 32,000 of fake views; the stipulated order imposed a $2.5 million judgment on Calas, "upon payment of $250,000, the remainder of the judgment will be suspended", approved by the Commission 5-0 ([FTC, 21 October 2019, read through a fetch tool](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2019/10/devumi-owner-ceo-settle-ftc-charges-they-sold-fake-indicators-social-media-influence-cosmetics-firm)).

### Sunday Riley: astroturf in the reviews

The same FTC announcement settled a different kind of case. The agency alleged that managers at the cosmetics company Sunday Riley, including its chief executive, Sunday Riley, posted reviews on the Sephora site "using fake accounts created to hide their identity", that after Sephora removed reviews staff obtained a virtual private network (VPN) account "to . . . allow us to hide our IP address and location when we write reviews", and that a July 2016 email told employees to "create three accounts on Sephora.com, registered as . . . different identities" and to dislike negative reviews: "After enough dislikes, it is removed. This directly translates into sales!!" ([FTC, 21 October 2019](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2019/10/devumi-owner-ceo-settle-ftc-charges-they-sold-fake-indicators-social-media-influence-cosmetics-firm)). These are allegations settled by a consent order. The Commission approved it 3-2, with Commissioners Rohit Chopra and Rebecca Kelly Slaughter voting no; Chopra issued a separate statement, which the page read here does not reproduce. The case shows astroturfing without bots: a handful of employees, a VPN and a script.

### The rule that followed

In August 2024 the FTC announced a final rule on consumer reviews and testimonials that "prohibits anyone from selling or buying fake indicators of social media influence", limited to buyers who knew or should have known the indicators were fake and used them to misrepresent their influence for a commercial purpose; the Commission voted 5-0 ([FTC, 14 August 2024, read through a fetch tool](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials)). The codified rule defines fake indicators as those "generated by bots, purported individual accounts not associated with a real individual, accounts created with a real individual's personal information without their consent, or hijacked accounts", and makes selling, distributing, purchasing or procuring them "an unfair or deceptive act or practice" ([16 CFR Part 465, Electronic Code of Federal Regulations, sections 465.1 and 465.8](https://www.ecfr.gov/api/renderer/v1/content/enhanced/current/title-16?part=465)). The rule reaches commercial misrepresentation; it does not address political astroturfing.

### What €300 buys

The Strategic Communications Centre of Excellence (StratCom COE) in Riga, accredited by the North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO), tested the market directly. In 2019, it says, it spent €300 to buy "3 530 comments, 25 750 likes, 20 000 views, and 5 100 followers", which let it identify 18,739 accounts used for manipulation. In September and October 2020 it bought engagement on 39 posts on Facebook, Instagram, Twitter, YouTube and TikTok "using three high-quality Russian social media manipulation service providers": for €300 it received "1 150 comments, 9 690 likes, 323 202 views, and 3 726 shares", and identified 8,036 accounts ([NATO StratCom COE, Social Media Manipulation 2020, December 2020](https://stratcomcoe.org/cuploads/pfiles/social_media_manipulation_2020_stratcom_coe_21-12-2020_v2-1.pdf)).

The removal rates are the core result. <Claim status="reported" by="by NATO StratCom COE">"Of the 337 768 fake engagements purchased, more than 98 per cent remained online and active after four weeks, and even discounting fake views, more than 80 per cent of the 14 566 other fake engagements delivered remained active after a month"</Claim>. Of 499 accounts the researchers reported to the platforms as verified manipulators, 482 were still active five days later, and every reply they received said the user had not violated the terms of service. The centre ranked Twitter "the industry leader in combating manipulation", found Facebook improving and the only platform where the half-life of inauthentic accounts was rising, wrote that YouTube "seems to have given up", that TikTok "seems to be nearly defenceless", and that manipulating Instagram cost "roughly one tenth of the cost of targeting Facebook". With the consent of Senators Chuck Grassley, a Republican, and Chris Murphy, a Democrat, it also bought engagement on apolitical, dated posts on their verified accounts to test whether verified accounts were better protected. Its own ethics section says it bought only on its own fake accounts or with consent, chose trivial content and capped spending.

The 2020 report also records improvements: Facebook "managed to remove as much as 90 per cent of the fake views" in some cases before providers restored them, and Twitter and Facebook pushed sellers from automated comments to human labour. No reply by the platforms to the report's rankings was found in the sources read for this part.

## Coordinated campaigns on the record

### South Korea: a conviction and a dataset

In April 2018 South Korea's Supreme Court upheld a four-year prison term for Won Sei-hoon, director of the National Intelligence Service (NIS) from 2009 to 2013, <Claim status="established" by="by South Korea's Supreme Court">on his conviction for political interference and election meddling</Claim> in favour of Park Geun-hye before the 2012 presidential election; officials, The Korea Times reported, "hired hundreds of civilians and retirees of the agency to post Internet comments against her liberal rival Moon Jae-in" ([The Korea Times, 19 April 2018](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/amp/southkorea/law-crime/20180419/top-court-confirms-four-year-jail-term-for-ex-spy-chief-for-election-meddling)). The case had gone back and forth: acquitted on the election-law charge in 2014, convicted on appeal in 2015, sent back by the Supreme Court, convicted again in 2017; the 2018 ruling answered Won's own appeal.

Because a court had identified more than 1,000 Twitter accounts used in the campaign, Keller, Schoch, Stier and Yang could study a known astroturf operation instead of guessing at one. Their finding, as their university summarized it, was that the key to uncovering such accounts "lies not in finding automated 'bots' but in specific traces of human coordination", such as the same message posted on several accounts within a short time; only a small fraction of the accounts appeared to be bots; and <Claim status="reported" by="by the researchers">"even though they managed more than 1,000 accounts and tried to coordinate among themselves, they failed to get the response from the public"</Claim>, with retweet and mention counts closer to average users than to influencers ([University of Illinois News Bureau, 2019](https://news.illinois.edu/evidence-of-humans-not-bots-key-to-uncovering-disinformation-campaigns/)). The full paper appeared in Political Communication; the copies located for this part sat behind bot checks and were not read, so the figures here are the university's.

### China: 448 million posts a year

Gary King, Jennifer Pan and Margaret Roberts, using leaked emails from a local internet propaganda office, estimated that <Claim status="reported" by="by its authors">"the government fabricates and posts about 448 million social media comments a year"</Claim>, and found that the strategy was not to argue with critics but "to distract the public and change the subject", mostly by "cheerleading for China, the revolutionary history of the Communist Party, or other symbols of the regime" ([King, Pan and Roberts, American Political Science Review, 2017](https://gking.harvard.edu/files/gking/files/how_the_chinese_government_fabricates_social_media_posts_for_strategic_distraction_not_engaged_argument.pdf)). The posts, the so-called 50c party, were written largely by government employees under their own or pseudonymous accounts, not by bots, which places the operation under astroturfing rather than automation. No response from the Chinese government to the study was found in the sources read for this part.

### The platform tally

By December 2022 Meta said it had disrupted "more than 200 global networks" under its CIB policy since public reporting began in 2017, from 68 countries and in at least 42 languages, "with most targeting audiences in their home countries" ([Meta, 15 December 2022](https://about.fb.com/news/2022/12/metas-2022-coordinated-inauthentic-behavior-enforcements/)). Russia (34 networks), Iran (29) and Mexico (13) were the most prolific sources, "whether by state actors, political groups or commercial firms"; many Mexican networks were linked to public relations or marketing firms, "including instances where one network supported two rivals for the same electoral post", which Meta presented as the danger of "IO-for-hire" services. The planned part of this series on influence for hire takes up that market.

## Brazil

### 2018: pages removed before the election

On 25 July 2018 Facebook removed 196 Pages and 87 profiles in Brazil that, in its statement, "violavam nossas políticas de autenticidade", violated its authenticity policies, as part of "uma rede coordenada que se ocultava com o uso de contas falsas no Facebook, e escondia das pessoas a natureza e a origem de seu conteúdo com o propósito de gerar divisão e espalhar desinformação": a coordinated network that hid behind fake accounts and concealed the nature and origin of its content to sow division and spread disinformation ([Congresso em Foco, 25 July 2018, with the full statement](https://www.congressoemfoco.com.br/noticia/27467/facebook-desativa-quase-200-paginas-de-fake-news-ligadas-ao-mbl)). The statement named no group. The press reported that the pages were linked to the Movimento Brasil Livre (MBL), a group known for organizing demonstrations for the impeachment of President Dilma Rousseff. The MBL said coordinators of the group had their profiles "arbitrariamente" (arbitrarily) deleted, that in some cases the owners were identifiable so there could be no talk of fake accounts, and that Facebook "tem sido alvo de atenção internacional, por conta do viés político e ideológico da empresa", had drawn international attention for its political and ideological bias ([InfoMoney, 25 July 2018](https://www.infomoney.com.br/?p=60259)).

### 2020: a network linked to lawmakers' offices

In July 2020 Meta removed 33 Facebook accounts, 14 Pages, one Group and 37 Instagram accounts in a domestic Brazilian network that used duplicate and fake accounts "to evade enforcement, create fictitious personas posing as reporters, post content, and manage Pages masquerading as news outlets"; about 883,000 accounts followed the Pages and about 918,000 the Instagram accounts ([Meta, 8 July 2020, updated 21 July 2020](https://about.fb.com/news/2020/07/removing-political-coordinated-inauthentic-behavior/)). Meta said it found the activity while investigating suspected CIB "reported on by press and referenced in recent congressional testimony in Brazil", and that <Claim status="attributed" by="by Meta">its investigation "found links to individuals associated with the Social Liberal Party and some of the employees of the offices of Anderson Moraes, Alana Passos, Eduardo Bolsonaro, Flavio Bolsonaro and Jair Bolsonaro"</Claim>.

Meta's head of cybersecurity policy, Nathaniel Gleicher, drew the line of what the company could see: "We could see that there were employees of these offices engaged", but "we do not see evidence of the politicians … or whether there is systemic coordination and direction from the leadership of those offices or not" ([Associated Press, 8 July 2020, as republished by KSTP](https://kstp.com/kstp-news/international/facebook-removes-false-accounts-linked-to-brazils-bolsonaro/)). The Atlantic Council, which the Associated Press (AP) described as a research firm used by Facebook, said one operator was employed by the presidency; he did not reply to the AP. The Social Liberal Party (PSL) said the accounts belonged to lawmakers' aides, "sob responsabilidade direta de cada parlamentar", under each lawmaker's direct responsibility, with no relation to the party; Deputies Alana Passos and Anderson Moraes said their accounts were verified and they had not been notified of any irregularity ([Congresso em Foco, July 2020](https://www.congressoemfoco.com.br/noticia/31287/facebook-remove-contas-ligadas-ao-psl-e-a-familia-bolsonaro)). Senator Flávio Bolsonaro said that "it is impossible to evaluate what kind of profile was banned and if the platform crossed or not the limits of censorship"; Eduardo Bolsonaro did not comment, and the presidential office did not comment on Facebook's action (AP).

At the joint congressional committee of inquiry into fake news, the Comissão Parlamentar Mista de Inquérito (CPMI) treated in [the next part](/learn/brazil-mass-messaging-the-courts-and-the-cpmi), Deputy Joice Hasselmann, a former government leader in Congress, testified on 4 December 2019 that <Claim status="alleged" by="by Deputy Joice Hasselmann">"São quase dois milhões de robôs seguindo dois perfis, sendo 1,4 milhão no perfil de Jair Bolsonaro e 468 mil no perfil de Eduardo Bolsonaro"</Claim>, almost two million bots following the two profiles, and put the average price of a bot-boosted campaign at R$20,000 ([IstoÉ, 4 December 2019](https://istoe.com.br/em-cpmi-joice-acusa-carlos-e-eduardo-bolsonaro-de-controlar-milicias-virtuais/)). ### What the electoral rules say

The Superior Electoral Court, the Tribunal Superior Eleitoral (TSE), regulates online campaigning by resolution. Its consolidated Resolution 23.610/2019 bars the use of "ferramentas digitais não disponibilizadas pelo provedor da aplicação de internet" (digital tools not offered by the platform itself) "para alterar o teor ou a repercussão de propaganda eleitoral", to alter the content or reach of electoral propaganda (art. 28, § 3), and a 2026 amendment says providers may delete profiles "apenas nas hipóteses de perfis falsos, automatizados ou robô", only when they are fake, automated or bot profiles (art. 38-A, § 2, added by Resolution 23.755/2026) ([TSE, Resolution 23.610/2019, consolidated text, read through a fetch tool](https://www.tse.jus.br/legislacao/compilada/res/2019/resolucao-no-23-610-de-18-de-dezembro-de-2019)). The lawsuits over the 2018 campaign alleged, among other things, a pyramid structure "com emprego de robôs e números de telefone estrangeiros", using bots and foreign numbers; the court's unanimous dismissal of them, and what it did find, are set out in the next part ([TSE, judgment of 28 October 2021](https://sjur-servicos.tse.jus.br/sjur-servicos/rest/download/pdf/2673321)).

## Counting bots, and why the counts disagree

Estimates of how many accounts are bots vary by a factor of several, because they measure different things with different tools.

- **9% to 15%.** Varol, Ferrara, Davis, Menczer and Flammini, presenting the classifier that became Botometer in 2017, wrote that "Our estimates suggest that between 9% and 15% of active Twitter accounts are bots", calling even 15% "a conservative estimate" ([Varol et al., 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.03107)).
- **One fifth of a conversation.** Alessandro Bessi and Emilio Ferrara found in the 2016 United States (US) presidential election discussion "a large fraction of user population that may not be human, accounting for a significant portion of generated content (about one-fifth of the entire conversation)" ([Bessi and Ferrara, First Monday, 3 November 2016](https://firstmonday.org/ojs/index.php/fm/article/view/7090)).
- **Two thirds of links.** The Pew Research Center estimated in 2018 that 66% of tweeted links to popular websites were shared by accounts "with characteristics common among automated 'bots'"; it used Botometer, cited it as "about 86% accurate", ran its own validation and chose the term "suspected bots" "To acknowledge the possibility of misclassification" ([Pew Research Center, 9 April 2018](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2018/04/09/bots-in-the-twittersphere/)).
- **Fewer than 5%.** Twitter itself told investors that, from an internal review of a sample, false or spam accounts during the first quarter of 2022 "represented fewer than 5% of our mDAU", its monetizable daily active users, adding "we applied significant judgment" and "the actual number of false or spam accounts could be higher than we have estimated" ([Twitter, Form 10-Q for the first quarter of 2022, Securities and Exchange Commission](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1418091/000141809122000075/twtr-20220331.htm)).

The last figure became the centre of a lawsuit. When Elon Musk tried to walk away from his agreement to buy Twitter, his answer to the company's suit stated that his team had used Twitter's "firehose" of tweets and Botometer and that <Claim status="disputed" by="by Twitter">"false or spam accounts" made up 33% of visible accounts in the first week of July 2022 and about 10% of monetizable daily active users</Claim>, the Cable News Network (CNN) reported ([CNN Business, 9 August 2022, as republished by KVIA](https://kvia.com/news/business-technology/cnn-social-media-technology/2022/08/09/elon-musk-cited-this-tool-in-his-bot-dispute-with-twitter-its-creator-has-thoughts/)). Twitter replied that Botometer classifies accounts differently from the company and "earlier this year designated Musk himself as highly likely to be a bot"; CNN found that the tool had rated Musk's account at about one out of five on its bot scale since at least June. Kai-Cheng Yang, a Botometer developer at Indiana University's Observatory on Social Media (OSoMe), said the tool estimates how likely an account is to be automated, not whether it is fake or spam: "There's overlap of course, but they're not exactly the same thing". He added that the firehose shows only accounts that tweet, missing bots whose job is to inflate follower counts, and that private signals such as IP addresses would make estimates easier. The merger was completed on 27 October 2022 under the April agreement ([Twitter, Form 8-K, 28 October 2022](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1418091/000119312522272772/d411753d8k.htm)).

The disagreement is not only about numbers. "Bot", "automated", "spam", "fake" and "inauthentic" are different properties: a human can run a fake account, an automated account can be honest (a weather feed), and a coordinated network of real people can be inauthentic without any automation.

## Botometer and its critics

Botometer, the most widely used public bot detector for Twitter, scores accounts with a machine-learning model trained on labelled examples. Its developers, Yang, Ferrara and Menczer, explain in a 2022 tutorial that it analyses an account's 200 most recent tweets and tweets mentioning it, so "the results of Botometer change over time" and "a single bot score only reflects the status of the account at the moment when it is evaluated"; they recommend comparing score distributions across groups instead of judging single accounts, and validating any threshold on manually annotated accounts ([Yang, Ferrara and Menczer, Botometer 101, 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.01608)). The tool's own site warned, as CNN quoted it, that "It's tempting to set some arbitrary threshold score and consider everything above that number a bot and everything below a human, but we do not recommend this approach" ([CNN Business, 2022](https://kvia.com/news/business-technology/cnn-social-media-technology/2022/08/09/elon-musk-cited-this-tool-in-his-bot-dispute-with-twitter-its-creator-has-thoughts/)). The Botometer site itself refused automated download when this part was researched, so its current wording was not read.

Three lines of criticism followed.

**False positives that move.** Adrian Rauchfleisch and Jonas Kaiser collected Botometer scores for five datasets, three of verified bots and two of verified humans (4,134 accounts in English and German), over three months. <Claim status="reported" by="by its authors">"We show that the Botometer scores are imprecise when it comes to estimating bots; especially in a different language"</Claim>, they wrote, and thresholds, "even when used very conservatively, are prone to variance", producing both false negatives and false positives, so that "most studies in social science using the tool will unknowingly count a high number of human users as bots and vice versa" ([Rauchfleisch and Kaiser, PLOS ONE, 22 October 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0241045)).

**Studies of false positives.** Florian Gallwitz and Michael Kreil, in a 2022 paper posted on arXiv, report that when they tested Botometer in April 2018, "nearly half of U.S. Congress members present on Twitter were misclassified as bots (47%)" at the common threshold of 0.5; in May 2019 the rate fell to 0.4%, but they still found "10.5% of NASA-related accounts", "12% of Nobel Prize Laureates" and "17.7% of Reuters journalists" misclassified as bots ([Gallwitz and Kreil, 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.11474)). Inspecting hundreds of accounts counted as bots in peer-reviewed Botometer-based studies, they write, "We were unable to find a single social bot", and they conclude that <Claim status="disputed" by="by Botometer's developers">such studies "have, in reality, just investigated false positives and artifacts of this approach"</Claim>.

**Benchmarks that flatter.** Chris Hays, Zachary Schutzman, Erin Walk and Philipp Zimmer, at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), showed at the WWW 2023 conference that "simple decision rules" (in their words, "shallow decision trees trained on a small number of features") "achieve near-state-of-the-art performance on most available datasets", that datasets "do not generalize well to out-of-sample datasets", and that predictions "are highly dependent on each dataset's collection and labeling procedures rather than fundamental differences between bots and humans" ([Hays et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.07015)). They note why outsiders must use such tools at all: platforms' own "tools and data are not publicly available".

The developers' reply is on the record. Yang, Ferrara and Menczer accept that "Botometer is not perfect and may misclassify accounts", for reasons including conflicting training data, inactive accounts, languages other than English and novel behaviour, limitations "inevitable for all supervised machine learning algorithms". But "Some critics exploit these limitations to undermine the entire field of study devoted to social bots", for example by selecting small sets of accounts with high error rates "to argue that no bot detection tool is valid or that social bots do not exist at all", arguments that in their words "use fallacies such as cherry-picking and strawman in disingenuous ways" ([Botometer 101](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.01608)). They ask critics to contribute annotated datasets instead.

Cresci adds a structural reason why the problem will not settle: bots evolve. Newer bots, he writes, "are more similar to legitimate human-operated accounts than to other older bots", partly through hybridization with human operators, and they now use "the same technological weapons as their hunters", including generated texts and profile pictures ([Cresci, 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.03604)). A detector trained on yesterday's bots measures yesterday's bots. On generative tools in influence operations, see the planned part of this series on generative artificial intelligence (AI).

| Question | What the evidence read here supports |
|---|---|
| Can a public tool tell a bot from a human account by account? | Not reliably: its own developers advise against single-account judgments and fixed thresholds |
| Can it compare groups of accounts? | Its developers say distributions are more robust; critics say the errors bias the groups too |
| Do platforms know more? | Platforms hold private signals (IP addresses, device data) that outsiders lack, and publish only aggregates |
| Is coordination easier to see than automation? | The South Korea study found human coordination traces more telling than automation |

## What the evidence says about effects

Counting accounts is one question; whether they change anything is another, and the best-known studies point in different directions.

Soroush Vosoughi, Deb Roy and Sinan Aral studied about 126,000 stories that some 3 million people tweeted more than 4.5 million times between 2006 and 2017, each checked by six fact-checking organizations. They removed bots with a detection algorithm, then added them back, and found that none of their main conclusions changed: <Claim status="reported" by="by its authors">"robots accelerated the spread of true and false news at the same rate, implying that false news spreads more than the truth because humans, not robots, are more likely to spread it"</Claim>, results that held with a second, independent detector and varied thresholds ([Vosoughi, Roy and Aral, Science, 9 March 2018](https://politics.media.mit.edu/papers/Vosoughi_Science.pdf)). Their headline finding that falsehood spread further was later reanalysed by Jonas Juul and Johan Ugander, who found that, for true and false news, "the reported structural differences can almost entirely be explained by false-news cascades being larger" ([Juul and Ugander, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 8 November 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8609637/)); the planned part on how reach changed returns to that dispute.

Chengcheng Shao and colleagues at Indiana University analysed 14 million messages spreading 400,000 articles in 2016 and 2017 and found that <Claim status="reported" by="by its authors">"social bots played a disproportionate role in spreading articles from low-credibility sources"</Claim>, amplifying them "in the early spreading moments, before an article goes viral"; accounts labelled bots were 6% of a sample but posted 31% of the tweets linking to low-credibility content ([Shao et al., Nature Communications, 20 November 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-06930-7)). The labels came from Botometer at a 0.5 threshold, the practice the critics above question, so the two sets of findings rest partly on the measurement dispute.

On persuasion, the strongest data come from linking accounts to surveys. Gregory Eady, Tom Paskhalis, Jan Zilinsky, Richard Bonneau, Jonathan Nagler and Joshua Tucker matched US respondents to their Twitter feeds and found that exposure to the Russian Internet Research Agency (IRA) campaign in 2016 was "heavily concentrated: only 1% of users accounted for 70% of exposures", concentrated among strong Republican identifiers, eclipsed by domestic news and politicians, and that <Claim status="reported" by="by its authors">"we find no evidence of a meaningful relationship between exposure to the Russian foreign influence campaign and changes in attitudes, polarization, or voting behavior"</Claim> ([Eady et al., Nature Communications, 9 January 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-35576-9)). The IRA operated mostly through human-run personas, not bots, and the study measures one platform in one election.

Taken together, the sources read here support three narrower statements: inauthentic engagement is cheap and persistent (StratCom COE); some operations with many accounts reached few real people (South Korea, IRA exposure); and whether bots, as distinct from the people who reshare, drive what spreads depends on a detection method whose reliability is itself contested. None of these shows that inauthentic activity never matters: fake counts can still mislead buyers, advertisers and journalists, which is the harm the New York and FTC cases addressed.

## What defenders can do

- **Platforms** enforce on behaviour rather than content (Meta), and the South Korean study suggests looking for coordination traces, such as identical posts across accounts within seconds, more than for automation. StratCom COE's experiment found that user reports of verified fake accounts were rarely acted on within five days.
- **Researchers** who use public detectors can follow the tool makers' own advice: validate thresholds on hand-labelled samples from their own data, compare distributions, report uncertainty, run the analysis soon after collection, and avoid claims about single accounts ([Botometer 101](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.01608)). Hays and colleagues add that training data should match the population studied.
- **Regulators and courts** have treated the sale of fake engagement as consumer deception (New York, the FTC rule) and an intelligence service's comment campaign as a crime (South Korea); electoral rules in Brazil ban tools that alter the reach of propaganda.
- **Readers** can treat counts, trends and reviews as signals that can be bought, look at who the accounts are rather than how many, and use [lateral reading](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign), the subject of the planned part on verification.

## What to remember

- A social bot is software running an account; a cyborg mixes software and a person; a sockpuppet is one of several accounts run by one person; astroturfing is orchestrated activity posing as grassroots, with or without bots.
- Meta's coordinated inauthentic behaviour, X's platform manipulation, YouTube's fake engagement and TikTok's deceptive behaviours are company rules judged on behaviour; each draws the line differently.
- Devumi's sale of followers ended in a New York settlement and an FTC order; its founder had denied the allegations to The New York Times. The FTC's 2024 rule bans buying or selling fake indicators of influence for commercial purposes.
- NATO StratCom COE bought hundreds of thousands of fake engagements for €300 and found more than 98% still online after four weeks.
- A South Korean court convicted an intelligence chief over a comment campaign; researchers then found human coordination, not automation, the best marker of its accounts, and little public response to them.
- Bot-prevalence figures run from under 5% (Twitter's own estimate of spam in daily users) to a fifth of a conversation or two thirds of links (Botometer-based studies); they measure different things.
- Botometer's critics report large and shifting false-positive rates; its developers accept the limits and say critics cherry-pick.
- Studies disagree on whether bots drive what spreads; the best data on persuasion found no measurable effect of the Russian IRA campaign on attitudes or votes in 2016.
- In Brazil, Facebook removed a network in 2018 that the press tied to the MBL, which called it political bias, and Meta removed one in 2020 that it linked to staff of five politicians, who disputed or did not answer; a bot figure given to Congress was not independently verified.

<ReaderQuestions>

- If a public tool can mark nearly half of a parliament as bots one year and almost none the next, what should a count of "bots" in a news story be required to say about how it was made?
- When a platform holds the only data that can settle how many accounts are fake, and the platform's revenue depends on the count, who should check the figure, and with what access?
- Is a network of real people posting the same words on cue less of a problem than a network of bots, or more, because it is harder to see and to stop?
- Fake followers were treated as consumer fraud when sold to influencers and businesses; should the same sale to a political campaign be judged by the same rule, by electoral law, or not at all?
- If an operation with a thousand accounts reaches almost no one, what harm remains, and who bears it: the audience, the platform, or the trust everyone places in numbers?

</ReaderQuestions>
