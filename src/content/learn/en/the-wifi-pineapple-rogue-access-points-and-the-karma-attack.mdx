A phone that has once joined "Airport_Free_WiFi" will try to join it again, and for years many devices said so out loud. The WiFi Pineapple is the commercial product built on that habit: a small Linux computer that listens for the names of the networks nearby devices remember and answers to them. This part of the series opened by [the map from the blue box to the Flipper Zero](/learn/hacking-devices-from-the-blue-box-to-the-flipper-zero), after [the story of Hak5](/learn/hak5-from-a-video-show-to-a-hacking-gear-company), explains the mechanism from the research that found it, follows the device through its generations from Hak5's own pages, and walks through the frames of a deauthentication and an evil twin. After reading, you can explain how a client leaks its preferred networks, what the Pineapple can and cannot do, which defence closes which gap, and what the law says in four jurisdictions.

## The trust problem: networks a device remembers

The 802.11 standard says how a client station joins a network, not which network it should trust. Dino Dai Zovi and Shane Macaulay put it plainly in their 2005 paper: the standard says how a client joins a network, but how the network "is chosen is unspecified", and the choice "has been left up to implementation by hardware and operating system software vendors" ([Dai Zovi and Macaulay, Attacking Automatic Wireless Network Selection, 18 March 2005](https://theta44.org/karma/aawns.pdf)). A station (STA) finds networks in two ways, as Hak5's own Wi-Fi primer explains: passively, by listening for the beacon frames that access points (APs) broadcast "typically several times per second", or actively, by sending a probe request that is either general ("what access points are around") or directed at a particular service set identifier ([SSID](/glossary/ssid)) ([Hak5 Documentation, WiFi Pineapple, Management Frames](https://documentation.hak5.org/wifi-pineapple/wifi-basics/management-frames.md)).

Directed probes are the leak. Dai Zovi and Macaulay traced Windows XP Service Pack 2's Wireless Auto Configuration: it kept a Preferred Networks List (PNL) of networks joined before and, when none answered a broadcast scan, sent a directed probe for each one in order, so that a listener on one channel could "recreate the victim's Preferred Networks List" ([Dai Zovi and Macaulay, 2005](https://theta44.org/karma/aawns.pdf)). Their packet trace shows a laptop probing in turn for the networks "aye", "bee" and "sea", then parking its card on a random SSID for 60 seconds (same source). Mac OS X 10.3.8 revealed its trusted list the same way when a user logged in or the machine woke, and its older AirPort cards parked with the hard-coded 40-bit Wired Equivalent Privacy (WEP) key 0x0102030405, which let the researchers' access point lure them with no user interaction (same source).

The second leak is the address. A device's media access control ([MAC address](/glossary/mac-address)) travels in every probe request, so a fixed address lets an observer track a device from place to place, as the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) summarized in 2025 in Request for Comments (RFC) 9724: "While actively scanning for available networks, the MAC address is used in the Probe Request frames sent by the device" ([RFC 9724, State of Affairs for Randomized and Changing MAC Addresses, March 2025](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9724.txt)).

## Karma, 2004 to 2006

Dai Zovi's project page gives the name its expansion, "KARMA Attacks Radioed Machines Automatically", and describes it as tools that discover clients and their preferred networks "by passively listening for 802.11 Probe Request frames", then either create a rogue AP for one probed network or use "a custom driver that responds to probes and association requests for any SSID" ([Dino A. Dai Zovi, KARMA](https://theta44.org/karma/)). The page sums up the effect in one line: "So if a client looks for ‘linksys’, it is ‘linksys’ to them (even while it may be ‘tmobile’ to someone else)" (same source).

The dates come from Dai Zovi's own lists. Karma 0.1 was released at PacSec in Tokyo in November 2004, version 0.2 at the Immunity NYC Security Shindig in January 2005, version 0.3 at Microsoft BlueHat in March 2005 and version 0.4 at CanSecWest in Vancouver in May 2005 ([Dino A. Dai Zovi, Presentations](https://theta44.org/presentations.html); [KARMA](https://theta44.org/karma/)). The paper is dated 18 March 2005 and was published in the proceedings of the sixth annual Information Assurance Workshop of the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) ([KARMA, Publications](https://theta44.org/karma/)). The last snapshot is dated 24 January 2006, and the page says the tool "hasn’t been updated since 2006" but lives on in Karmetasploit, Jasager, Snoopy, Kali Linux, the WiFi Pineapple and Pwnie Express (same source).

Technically, the researchers patched the open-source MADWiFi driver for Atheros cards, "disabling SSID validation and rewriting the SSID field of incoming Probe Requests and Association Request frames with the configured SSID of the software access point", which let them "create a wireless network that masquerades as any network requested by a client within range" ([Dai Zovi and Macaulay, 2005](https://theta44.org/karma/aawns.pdf)). They also noted the companion trick that every later tool used: an attacker can force a connected client to search again "by spoofing 802.11 Disassociation frames from the base station", frames that "are always sent in the clear, even if the network is encrypted" (same source).

The vendors answered. Dai Zovi's page lists Microsoft Security Advisory 917021 and Apple's AirPort 4.2 update as the security updates that followed ([KARMA](https://theta44.org/karma/)). Microsoft's advisory of 17 October 2006 says update 917021 changed how Windows XP clients behave "in non-broadcast networks and in Ad Hoc networks" and changed the default "parking behavior", as "defense-in-depth changes" to keep systems from joining networks a user did not intend ([Microsoft Security Advisory 917021, 17 October 2006](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/security-updates/securityadvisories/2006/917021)).

## From Karma to Jasager to the Pineapple

Rob Fuller wrote in 2008 that he first heard Dai Zovi present Karma at ShmooCon 2006, put it on a small Fonera router in early 2007, and then took the idea to Darren Kitchen of Hak5, who brought in the developer Robin Wood ([Rob Fuller, Jasager: Past, Present and Future, 9 October 2008](https://malicious.link/posts/2008/2008-10-09-jasager-past-present-and-future/)). Wood's project page describes Jasager as "an implementation of Karma designed to run on OpenWrt on the Fon", with a web interface listing connected clients and the SSID each associated with, auto-run scripts and logging ([Robin Wood, Jasager](https://digi.ninja/jasager/)). Its change log dates the launch to 17 September 2008 and release 2.0 to 17 November 2008 ([Jasager, Todo and Change Log](https://digi.ninja/jasager/todo)). Security Management, the magazine of the security association ASIS, later glossed the name: Jasager is German for "The yes man" (summary of [Security Management, The Pineapple Express, 27 January 2012](https://www.asisonline.org/security-management-magazine/articles/2012/01/the-pineapple-express-hak5-builds-a-bigger-better-wifi-honey-pot/)).

Hak5's documentation for its sixth-generation devices calls the Pineapple "a versatile Linux-based wireless auditing platform in development since 2008", best known for its ability "to passively gather intelligence, target and track WiFi enabled devices and effectively deploy a rogue access point for man-in-the-middle attacks" ([Hak5 Documentation, About the WiFi Pineapple NANO and TETRA](https://documentation.hak5.org/wifi-pineapple-6th-gen-nano-tetra/getting-started/about-the-wifi-pineapple-nano-tetra.md)), the attack class described in [traffic diversion and the man in the middle](/learn/traffic-diversion-and-the-man-in-the-middle).

## PineAP, in Hak5's own words

PineAP is the Pineapple's engine. For the sixth generation Hak5 described it as "the intelligent sniffing and injection engine" that "performs recon, analyzes traffic, captures probes and broadcasts beacons" ([Hak5 Documentation, The PineAP Suite](https://documentation.hak5.org/wifi-pineapple-6th-gen-nano-tetra/getting-started/the-pineap-suite.md)). The same page traces its features back to the older names:

- **Allow Associations** lets a client "associate with the WiFi Pineapple through any requested SSID"; "Before the evolution of the PineAP suite with the 5th generation WiFi Pineapple, this feature was known as Karma."
- **Beacon Response** sends beacons only to the device that probed, "This prevents the beacon from being visible to other devices."
- **Capture SSIDs to Pool** saves the names seen in probe requests.
- **Broadcast SSID Pool** advertises the saved names as beacons, a feature "known to the WiFi Pineapple Mark V as “Dogma” as a compliment to “Karma”".
- **Target MAC** narrows the broadcasts to one client, which "enables precision device targeting".

For the Mark VII, Hak5 groups the engine into three modes, Passive, Active and Advanced, and five functions: filters, impersonation of saved networks, an Open AP that can "automatically impersonate *any* Open access point requested by a client", Evil WPA, which can copy an existing Wi-Fi Protected Access (WPA) network or "Capture partial handshakes", and Evil Enterprise, which serves a WPA-Enterprise network "with optional key exchange degradation" ([Hak5 Documentation, WiFi Pineapple Mark VII, PineAP](https://documentation.hak5.org/wifi-pineapple/ui-overview/pineap.md)). On the Pager the option is called "Mimic Open Networks", and Hak5's documentation names the underlying technique directly: "Using a Wi-Fi attack called `karma`, a Pineapple in mimicry mode will answer any request for a network name with a response - for that same name" ([Hak5 Documentation, Pager, Pineapple Open AP](https://documentation.hak5.org/wifi-pineapple-pager/pineapple-open-ap.md)).

Scope control is built in, at least by default. The Pager ships with both its client filter and its network filter in allow mode with empty lists, which "blocks all associations, which prevents a new Pineapple from interfering with nearby devices or networks unintentionally" (same source). The documented use is to allow only the network names in an engagement and to deny no clients, "useful with MAC address randomization" (same source).

## Worked example 1: a probe and a Karma answer, frame by frame

Every frame below is a management frame, type 0 in the 802.11 frame control field. The subtype values are those the Linux kernel's 802.11 header defines from the standard, shown as the kernel writes them, already shifted into the frame control field ([Linux kernel, include/linux/ieee80211.h](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/torvalds/linux/master/include/linux/ieee80211.h)). The sequence is the one Dai Zovi and Macaulay captured in 2005 when their patched access point answered a Windows XP laptop parked on a random SSID ([Dai Zovi and Macaulay, 2005, figure 3](https://theta44.org/karma/aawns.pdf)).

| # | Frame (subtype constant) | From → to | What it carries | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Probe Request (0x0040) | Client → broadcast address, BSSID ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff | The SSID the client wants (here the random parking name) and its supported rates | The client asks "is this network here?" |
| 2 | Probe Response (0x0050) | Rogue AP → client | The same SSID, copied from frame 1, and the AP's BSSID | The Karma driver says yes to a name it has never been configured with |
| 3 | Authentication (0x00B0), sequence 1 | Client → rogue AP | Open System algorithm | The client starts to join |
| 4 | Authentication (0x00B0), sequence 2 | Rogue AP → client | Status: successful | Open authentication proves nothing about either side |
| 5 | Association Request (0x0000) | Client → rogue AP | The SSID again, capability information | The client asks to associate |
| 6 | Association Response (0x0010) | Rogue AP → client | Status successful, association identifier 1 | The client is now on the attacker's network |

Two lessons sit in this table. Nothing in frames 1 to 6 authenticates the network; for an open network, as Bastille, a seller of wireless intrusion detection, puts it, "there is nothing to prove" ([Bastille, Wi-Fi Pineapple: Is It Still a Threat, 1 September 2026](https://bastille.net/research/wifi-pineapple/)). And the attack needs a client that names networks aloud or accepts an open one; once the client is associated, Hak5's own primer notes that "the usual first network interactions will occur", starting with an address from the Pineapple's DHCP server ([Hak5 Documentation, Association and State](https://documentation.hak5.org/wifi-pineapple/wifi-basics/association-and-state.md)).

## Worked example 2: a deauthentication and an evil twin

**The deauthentication frame.** Hak5's primer describes deauthentication and disassociation frames as the normal way a station ends a conversation, and adds: "When this unencrypted management frame is spoofed by a third party, the technique is often called a deauth attack" ([Hak5 Documentation, Management Frames](https://documentation.hak5.org/wifi-pineapple/wifi-basics/management-frames.md)). In the Linux kernel's structure for a management frame, a deauthentication is short ([Linux kernel, include/linux/ieee80211.h](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/torvalds/linux/master/include/linux/ieee80211.h)):

| Field | Size | In a legitimate frame | In a spoofed frame |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frame control | 2 bytes | Type 0, subtype Deauthentication (0x00C0); the Protected bit (0x4000) is set only when the frame is protected | Same type and subtype; on a network without protection the Protected bit is clear |
| Duration | 2 bytes | Radio timing | Copied or invented |
| Address 1, destination (da) | 6 bytes | The client, or the broadcast address | The target client, or ff:ff:ff:ff:ff:ff for every client |
| Address 2, source (sa) | 6 bytes | The AP | The AP's address, forged |
| Address 3, BSSID | 6 bytes | The AP's basic service set identifier ([BSSID](/glossary/bssid)) | The same, forged |
| Sequence control | 2 bytes | A counter | Any value |
| Reason code | 2 bytes | For example 3 (`WLAN_REASON_DEAUTH_LEAVING`) or 7 (`WLAN_REASON_CLASS3_FRAME_FROM_NONASSOC_STA`) | Any of the defined codes |

The reason codes come from the same header: 1 is unspecified, 2 means the previous authentication is no longer valid, 3 that the sender is leaving, 4 disassociation for inactivity, and 6 and 7 a class 2 or class 3 frame from a station that is not authenticated or not associated (same source). The whole frame body is 26 bytes before the checksum: a 24-byte header and the 2-byte reason code (same source). Without protection, a client has no way to tell this frame from one its own AP sent, because every field an attacker needs, the AP's address included, is "readily available from the Beacon frames the base station is required to continuously transmit", as the Karma paper put it in 2005 ([Dai Zovi and Macaulay, 2005](https://theta44.org/karma/aawns.pdf)).

**Why an attacker sends it.** Hak5's documentation gives two purposes for the Pager's deauthentication command: "to redirect clients to a Pineapple access point or to force a reassociation when harvesting WPA handshakes" ([Hak5 Documentation, Pager, DEAUTH_CLIENT](https://documentation.hak5.org/wifi-pineapple-pager/wifi-pineapple-commands/deauth_client.md)). The second matters for WPA with a pre-shared key (PSK): a client that rejoins performs the four-way handshake again, and Hak5 explains that collecting it allows an "offline dictionary attack against the secret key" on another computer ([Hak5 Documentation, Pager, Handshake Collection](https://documentation.hak5.org/wifi-pineapple-pager/handshake-collection.md)), the handshake whose design flaws are covered in [KRACK and the Wi-Fi handshake](/learn/krack-and-the-wifi-handshake).

**The evil twin.** An evil twin is a rogue AP that copies one specific network. The 2005 paper already cited the older idea that "a cloned base station (Evil Twin) can produce a stronger signal than the legitimate base station and divert unsuspecting clients" ([Dai Zovi and Macaulay, 2005](https://theta44.org/karma/aawns.pdf)). Bastille separates the three terms that are often confused: a rogue access point is any unauthorized AP; an evil twin impersonates "one specific known SSID"; Karma-style attacks respond "to whatever network names clients ask for" ([Bastille, 1 September 2026](https://bastille.net/research/wifi-pineapple/)). In principle the sequence is: the attacker's AP advertises the victim network's name, sometimes with the same BSSID; spoofed deauthentication frames push clients off the real AP; some clients reassociate with the stronger or nearer copy. What happens next depends on the network's security:

- On an **open network**, the copy needs nothing more, and a captive portal can ask for credentials. The Australian case below worked this way.
- On a **WPA2 network with a pre-shared key**, the copy can only complete the handshake if it knows the passphrase. Hak5's Evil WPA feature is described as able to "Serve a new WPA network, or copy an existing WPA network" and to capture partial handshakes when the key is unknown ([Hak5 Documentation, Mark VII, PineAP](https://documentation.hak5.org/wifi-pineapple/ui-overview/pineap.md)).
- On an **enterprise network using 802.1X**, the copy offers its own certificate. Hak5's documentation states the limit itself: "*Properly configured* WiFi Enterprise clients will reject unknown certificates, however many devices do not offer proper configuration and may either blindly accept new certificates, or prompt the user to accept the certificate"; a client using the Microsoft Challenge Handshake Authentication Protocol version 2 (MSCHAPv2) cannot fully connect, though its challenge is captured for offline work, while one using Generic Token Card (GTC) discloses the password (same source). How those Extensible Authentication Protocol (EAP) methods work is set out in [802.1X and the EAP methods](/learn/802-1x-and-eap-methods).

## What the Pineapple cannot do

Hak5's own documentation is specific about the limits:

- **Protected management frames stop forged disconnections.** "Networks which utilize Protected Management Frames (PMF) or the 802.11w standard will not be susceptible to injected disconnection packets. All networks utilizing WPA3 also enable Protected Management Frames" ([Hak5 Documentation, Pager, DEAUTH_CLIENT](https://documentation.hak5.org/wifi-pineapple-pager/wifi-pineapple-commands/deauth_client.md)).
- **6 GHz is closed to the old tricks.** The same page says "The WiFi standard requires that *all* networks on 6GHz utilize WPA3 features, such as Protected Management Frames", and that transmissions on 5 GHz Dynamic Frequency Selection (DFS) channels are prohibited by "stronger regulatory requirements" (same source).
- **WPA3 handshakes are not crackable offline.** Hak5 writes that handshakes "are not available on 6GHz networks" and that WPA3 networks "are not currently vulnerable to offline attacks that WPA1 and WPA2 networks are vulnerable to" ([Hak5 Documentation, Pager, Handshake Collection](https://documentation.hak5.org/wifi-pineapple-pager/handshake-collection.md)).
- **It does not crack passwords itself.** Hak5 describes offline attacks as "typically performed on a computer with a GPU", a graphics processing unit (GPU), with large password lists, and capture can take "up to hours, in a completely passive environment" (same source).
- **Some clients do not move.** "Some clients ignore disconnection attempts deliberately regardless of the network type or channel" ([Hak5 Documentation, Pager, DEAUTH_CLIENT](https://documentation.hak5.org/wifi-pineapple-pager/wifi-pineapple-commands/deauth_client.md)).

Bastille adds that HTTPS and HTTP Strict Transport Security (HSTS) "largely ended casual traffic interception", while credential capture at fake portals, device tracking and initial network access did not go away, and that modern phones broadcast far less about their network history, though directed probes still occur, "notably for hidden networks" ([Bastille, 1 September 2026](https://bastille.net/research/wifi-pineapple/)). That is the assessment of a vendor of defences.

## The defences, gap by gap

| Gap | Defence | Standard, owner and date | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Directed probes reveal remembered networks | Do not use hidden networks; forget open networks | Apple warns that a hidden network "might expose information that can be used to identify you and the hidden networks you use" ([Apple Support, Recommended settings for Wi-Fi routers](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102766)) | Older and embedded clients still probe |
| A fixed address makes devices trackable | Randomized MAC addresses | Android randomizes by default from Android 10 ([Android Open Source Project, MAC randomization behavior](https://source.android.com/docs/core/connect/wifi-mac-randomization-behavior)); Apple uses "a different MAC address for each Wi-Fi network" by default ([Apple Support, Use private Wi-Fi addresses](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102509)) | Defeats tracking by address, not association with a rogue AP |
| Forged deauthentication | Protected Management Frames, IEEE 802.11w | Amendment approved on 11 September 2009 ([IEEE 802.11 Working Group, timelines](https://www.ieee802.org/11/Reports/802.11_Timelines.htm)); the Wi-Fi Alliance says WPA3 networks "Require use of Protected Management Frames (PMF)" and that PMF "is required for all new certified devices" ([Wi-Fi Alliance, Security](https://www.wi-fi.org/discover-wi-fi/security)) | Mixed and legacy clients without PMF |
| Evil twin of a password network | WPA3-Personal, Simultaneous Authentication of Equals (SAE) | WPA3-Personal "Uses simultaneous authentication of equals (SAE)" ([Android Open Source Project, WPA3 and Wi-Fi Enhanced Open](https://source.android.com/docs/core/connect/wifi-wpa3-owe)) | Transition mode accepts WPA2 too; Apple describes it as "a mixed mode" ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102766)) |
| Eavesdropping on open networks | Opportunistic Wireless Encryption (OWE) | RFC 8110, March 2017 ([IETF, RFC 8110](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8110.txt)) | Encrypts but does not authenticate the network |
| Evil twin of an enterprise network | [802.1X](/glossary/802-1x) with the server certificate validated by managed configuration | Hak5's own note that properly configured clients "will reject unknown certificates" ([Hak5 Documentation, PineAP](https://documentation.hak5.org/wifi-pineapple/ui-overview/pineap.md)) | Clients that prompt users to accept a new certificate |
| Rogue devices in the building | Wireless intrusion detection and prevention | The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) Special Publication (SP) 800-153 of February 2012 recommends continuous monitoring and locating threats by triangulation ([National Institute of Standards and Technology, SP 800-153](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-153.pdf)) | Containment by deauthentication is regulated (see the law below) |

The NIST guideline lists what such monitoring should detect, including "rogue APs and unauthorized client devices", denial-of-service conditions, which it suggests spotting by counting events such as terminated sessions over time, and "Impersonation and man-in-the-middle attacks" ([NIST SP 800-153, section 3.3](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-153.pdf)). It also warns that passive attacks, which only listen, leave "nothing for an organization to monitor electronically" (same source). How one vendor's access points implement rogue and attack detection is described in [FortiAP monitoring, threats and troubleshooting](/learn/fortiap-monitoring-threats-and-troubleshooting).

Kitchen offered a personal canary in 2012: he adds a network called "Pineapple Alert" to his devices, so that if it ever appears nearby, "I’ll know there’s a WiFi Pineapple nearby" ([Computerworld, 2 February 2012](https://www.computerworld.com/article/2472217/sneak-pineapple-peak--hak5-creates-even-more-lethal-wi-fi-hot-spot-honeypot-hackin.html)). The trick only catches Karma-style responders, not an evil twin of one named network, and it adds one more remembered name to probe for. The Australian Federal Police (AFP) gave simpler advice to travellers: avoid any free network that asks for an email or social media login, use a virtual private network (VPN), "change your device settings to ‘forget network’" and switch Wi-Fi off in public places ([Australian Federal Police, 28 November 2025](https://afp.gov.au/news-centre/media-release/wa-man-jailed-stealing-intimate-material-and-using-evil-twin-wifi)).

## Cases on the record

**Perth, 2024 to 2025.** The AFP charged a Western Australian man in May 2024 after an airline's staff reported a suspicious network on a domestic flight; officers searched his luggage at Perth Airport on 19 April 2024 and seized "a portable wireless access device" ([Australian Federal Police, 28 June 2024](https://afp.gov.au/news-centre/media-release/man-charged-over-creation-evil-twin-free-wifi-networks-access-personal)). On 28 November 2025 the AFP announced his sentence of seven years and four months and described the method: the device, "sometimes known as a WiFi Pineapple", listened passively for probe requests and "instantly created a matching network with the same name"; the network led to a page asking for an email or social media login, which was saved on his device ([Australian Federal Police, 28 November 2025](https://afp.gov.au/news-centre/media-release/wa-man-jailed-stealing-intimate-material-and-using-evil-twin-wifi)). The release says investigators found data from fake pages at airports in Perth, Melbourne and Adelaide and on domestic flights (same source).

**The Hague, 2018.** A US indictment announced on 4 October 2018 alleges that officers of Russia's military intelligence service (GRU) ran "close access" operations against Wi-Fi used by anti-doping officials, and that equipment abandoned beside the Organisation for the Prohibition of Chemical Weapons (OPCW) in April 2018 included a "Wi-Fi Pineapple", with data showing use at hotels in Lausanne in 2016 and Kuala Lumpur in 2017 ([Indictment, United States v. Morenets et al., paragraphs 91 to 93, DocumentCloud copy](https://assets.documentcloud.org/documents/4954153/10-4-18-US-v-Morenets.pdf); [US Attorney's Office, Western District of Pennsylvania](https://www.justice.gov/usao-wdpa/vw/us-v-Aleksei-Sergeyevich-Morenets)). These are allegations in a charging document.

**Marriott, 2014.** The same deauthentication frame can be used by a network operator. The Federal Communications Commission (FCC) found in 2014 that Marriott International "deployed a Wi-Fi deauthentication protocol to deliberately block consumers who sought to connect to the Internet using their own personal Wi-Fi hot spots"; Marriott settled by "paying a civil penalty of $600,000" ([FCC Enforcement Advisory No. 2015-01, DA 15-113, 27 January 2015](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-15-113A1_Rcd.pdf)).

## The Brazilian angle

No Brazilian court decision or official report naming the WiFi Pineapple was found for this article. Brazil's experience with impersonated radio infrastructure comes from the cellular network instead. In January 2025 Anatel inspectors joined a São Paulo Civil Police operation that seized equipment simulating an Estação Rádio Base (ERB), a mobile base station, used to push fraudulent Short Message Service (SMS) texts; Mobile Time reported that Anatel located it with spectrum monitoring, drones and drive tests after a carrier reported interference, and that using such uncertified equipment is "atividade de telecomunicações clandestina", a crime under the Lei Geral de Telecomunicações (LGT) with a penalty of two to four years ([Mobile Time, 23 January 2025](https://www.mobiletime.com.br/noticias/23/01/2025/falsa-erb-golpe-sms/)). Teletime reported in May 2025 that this was the fifth seizure of such equipment in Greater São Paulo in under a year ([Teletime, 23 May 2025](https://teletime.com.br/23/05/2025/anarte-apreende-nova-erb-fake-em-sao-paulo/)). The "ERB fake" is the cellular cousin of the rogue AP: a transmitter that claims to be infrastructure the phone trusts.

For Wi-Fi gear itself, two Brazilian rules apply. Article 154-A of the Código Penal, quoted in the table above, punishes selling a device "com o intuito de permitir" an intrusion, so intent, not the object, is the test ([Lei 12.737/2012](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/588113/publicacao/15773241)). And radio transmitters fall under Anatel's certification and homologation, which the agency says exist for the "uso eficiente e racional do espectro radioelétrico" ([Anatel, Certificação de Produtos](https://www.gov.br/anatel/pt-br/regulado/certificacao-de-produtos)). Hak5's own Code of Ethics limits its products to a list of countries that does not include Brazil, as [the Hak5 part](/learn/hak5-from-a-video-show-to-a-hacking-gear-company) sets out; cheaper Pineapple-like tools built on ESP32 boards, including a Brazilian one, are the subject of [the part on the Flipper's alternatives](/learn/high-boy-esp32-marauder-and-the-flipper-alternatives), and the legal questions of owning such gear in Brazil a later part of this series.

## What to remember

- Clients find networks with probe requests, and directed probes for remembered names let a listener rebuild a device's preferred network list; Dai Zovi and Macaulay showed it in 2004 to 2005.
- Karma answers any requested name; Jasager put it on a Fonera router in 2008, and Hak5's PineAP turned it into "Allow Associations", Beacon Response and the SSID pool.
- The generations run from the 2008 Jasager Pineapple through the Mark IV (2012) and Mark V (2013), the NANO and TETRA (2016), the Mark VII (2020) and Enterprise (2022) to the Pager (2025).
- A deauthentication frame body is 26 bytes, addresses and a reason code that anyone can forge on an unprotected network; 802.11w (2009) and WPA3 stop it, and 6 GHz requires them.
- The strongest defences are protected management frames, WPA3, 802.1X with enforced certificate checks and continuous wireless monitoring; containment by deauthentication is itself regulated.
