A tone box is a small generator of sounds that a telephone network treated as orders. The people around the first of them, the blind boy who whistled 2600 Hz, Captain Crunch and his cereal whistle, and the two Steves who sold blue boxes in Berkeley dormitories, are the subject of [Phreaking: the network that whistled its own passwords](/learn/phone-phreaking-and-the-blue-box), and this part does not tell their story again. It reads the engineering instead: the papers the Bell System published about its own signalling, the 1980 operating manual that tabulated every tone, the patents written to stop payphone fraud, the period lists in which phone phreaks described their boxes, and the documents of the Brazilian payphone that went its own way. It is the second part of the series opened by [Hacking devices: from the blue box to the Flipper Zero](/learn/hacking-devices-from-the-blue-box-to-the-flipper-zero). After reading, you will be able to read the Bell System's signalling tables, say what each colour of box imitated and why it worked, explain how the telephone companies detected and then designed out the boxes, and follow how Brazil's orelhões moved from tokens to a card whose every credit is a fuse burned by induction.

Every system described here is gone or going: the in-band trunks of the long-distance network were replaced by common-channel signalling from 1976 onwards, and Brazil's last obligatory public telephones are due to be maintained only until the end of 2028. The tables below are the operators' own published documentation. Nothing here is a recipe; where period files gave build instructions, they are cited as historical sources and not repeated.

## Why the network signalled in the audio band

Early dial systems signalled with direct current, which only works over short distances and over lines that have a direct-current path; the long trunks and the carrier systems that multiplied after the war had none, so nationwide dialling needed signals in alternating current, as A. Weaver and N. A. Newell of Bell Telephone Laboratories explained in the *Bell System Technical Journal* in November 1954 ([Weaver and Newell, "In-Band Single-Frequency Signaling", Bell System Technical Journal, November 1954](https://archive.org/details/bstj33-6-1309)). Their scheme, single-frequency (SF) signalling, used a steady tone on the line for an idle trunk and no tone for a busy one: "when the circuit is idle, steady tone is transmitted over the line and holds relays operated at the receiving end", and signalling "is accomplished by removing and reapplying this tone" ([Weaver and Newell, 1954](https://archive.org/details/bstj33-6-1309)). Because the tone travelled in the same voice channel the customers used, the system needed no extra wire, no loss of bandwidth and no intermediate equipment, and the authors called the in-band method the Bell System's preferred way "to meet requirements for toll dialing on a national basis" ([Weaver and Newell, 1954](https://archive.org/details/bstj33-6-1309)).

The frequency was chosen against the human voice. The first in-band system used 1600 cycles per second, because wartime "emergency bank" carrier channels cut everything above about 1750 cycles; with the war over and that equipment removed, the Bell System raised the signalling frequency to 2600 cycles, where protection against speech was "considerably easier", C. Breen and C. A. Dahlbom wrote in a survey published in November 1960 ([Breen and Dahlbom, "Signaling Systems for Control of Telephone Switching", Bell System Technical Journal, November 1960](https://archive.org/details/bstj39-6-1381)). Weaver and Newell stated the rule plainly: signal imitation by speech "decreases rapidly as the signaling frequency is raised", so the highest frequency the line could carry reliably was used, 2600 cycles on four-wire lines, with 2400 cycles for the second direction on two-wire lines ([Weaver and Newell, 1954](https://archive.org/details/bstj33-6-1309)).

Speech still got through. In "observations on many thousands of calls", the authors found that vowel sounds caused most false signals, "with all except a few being formed by female speech", and at 2600 cycles "over 90 per cent were caused by the long e vowel sound" ([Weaver and Newell, 1954](https://archive.org/details/bstj33-6-1309)). The defence was "guard action": the receiver measured the energy at every other frequency in the voice band and refused to operate when that energy was high, so a sung or spoken 2600 component buried in a word would not drop a call ([Weaver and Newell, 1954](https://archive.org/details/bstj33-6-1309); [Breen and Dahlbom, 1960](https://archive.org/details/bstj39-6-1381)). The 1980 operating manual of the American Telephone and Telegraph Company (AT&T), *Notes on the Network*, described the same balance and its limit in one sentence: "It is obvious that pure tones near 2600 Hz will cause the far-end (receiving) unit to go on-hook" ([AT&T, Notes on the Network, 1980, section 5, paragraph 7.36](https://archive.org/details/bitsavers_westernEleesonTheNetwork1980_26249557)). A pure tone has no energy elsewhere in the band to trigger the guard. That is the whole engineering basis of the blue box.

The same manual explains a detail every phreak knew by ear: when an SF unit received 2600 Hz, a band-elimination filter was switched into the receive path so the tone did not reach the caller, while ringing and busy signals still did ([AT&T, Notes on the Network, 1980, paragraph 7.24](https://archive.org/details/bitsavers_westernEleesonTheNetwork1980_26249557)). The chirp heard when a 2600 Hz burst stopped was the trunk changing state at the far end.

## The address language: multifrequency pulsing

A trunk seized by SF still needed an address. For that the Bell System used multifrequency (MF) pulsing, in which "each digit is composed of a unique combination of two out of a possible five frequencies in the voice band", with combinations of a sixth frequency for control signals; the six frequencies are "spaced 200 cycles apart, from 700 to 1700 cycles inclusive", giving fifteen pairs: ten digits, a signal for the beginning of pulsing, one for the end, and three spare ([Breen and Dahlbom, 1960, section 5.3.5 and Table IV](https://archive.org/details/bstj39-6-1381)). The receiver checked each digit for parity and rejected anything with more or fewer than two frequencies ([Breen and Dahlbom, 1960](https://archive.org/details/bstj39-6-1381)). Phil Lapsley, the historian of phone phreaking, lists the Breen and Dahlbom paper as "often cited as the article that gave away the keys to the kingdom", and an earlier paper by Dahlbom, Horton and Moody in the 1949 *Transactions* of the American Institute of Electrical Engineers (AIEE) as the first public description of MF signalling he knew of ([Phil Lapsley, Exploding the Phone, "Some Historical Documents"](https://explodingthephone.com/docs.php)).

The operator's procedure was public too. The first signal sent was KP (key pulse), which "prepares the multifrequency receiver to accept the digits that are to follow"; then one button per digit; then ST (start), "to indicate the end of pulsing" ([Breen and Dahlbom, 1960](https://archive.org/details/bstj39-6-1381); [AT&T, Notes on the Network, 1980, paragraphs 7.46 and 7.47](https://archive.org/details/bitsavers_westernEleesonTheNetwork1980_26249557)). The 1980 manual adds that the receiver "does not respond to voice-frequency currents until it receives the KP signal", that a check circuit returns a reorder signal unless "two, and only two" channels operate for each digit, and that senders pulsed at 68 ± 7 milliseconds per digit and per gap, about seven digits a second, raised to ten a second on international calls over the International Telegraph and Telephone Consultative Committee's (CCITT, from its French name) Signalling System No. 5 ([AT&T, Notes on the Network, 1980, paragraphs 7.48 to 7.50](https://archive.org/details/bitsavers_westernEleesonTheNetwork1980_26249557)).

Three things in the table matter for what follows. The supervisory tone (2600 Hz) and the address tones (700 to 1700 Hz) shared the customer's voice channel, so anything that could put pure tones into a handset could speak to the trunk. The pairs were not secret: they were printed in a technical journal in 1960 and in an operating manual in 1980. And the same pairs meant different things in different contexts: the KP pair was also "Coin Return" between offices, and the ST pair was also "Coin Collect", which is where the green box of the period lists comes from.

## The blue box, read from the documents

In principle the blue box combined the two languages: a burst of 2600 Hz on a long-distance connection made the far end treat the trunk as released while the caller stayed connected to it, and the MF pairs then let the caller address the trunk as a sender or an operator would ([AT&T, Notes on the Network, 1980, paragraphs 7.36 and 7.47](https://archive.org/details/bitsavers_westernEleesonTheNetwork1980_26249557)). Ron Rosenbaum's 1971 article in *Esquire* put the tolerance in a phreak's words: "As long as you get the frequency within thirty cycles per second of the phone company's tones, the phone equipment thinks it hears its own voice talking to it" ([Ron Rosenbaum, "Secrets of the Little Blue Box", Esquire, October 1971, republished by Slate on 7 October 2011](https://www.slate.com/articles/technology/the_spectator/2011/10/the_article_that_inspired_steve_jobs_secrets_of_the_little_blue_.html)). The same article tells of a phreak, under the pseudonym Gilbertson, who in 1968 found "a technical journal with the phone tone frequencies" in an engineering library, built his first box "maybe twelve hours" later and went on to sell blue boxes "for $1,500 apiece" ([Rosenbaum, Esquire, 1971](https://www.slate.com/articles/technology/the_spectator/2011/10/the_article_that_inspired_steve_jobs_secrets_of_the_little_blue_.html)); that is the article's account, not a verified record.

What the box could not do follows from the same documents. It could only act on connections that crossed in-band trunks, because the SF units were on those trunks and not on the subscriber's own line; the MF receiver listened only after a KP; and the call that carried the caller to the trunk was still recorded by the billing equipment of the originating office. Rosenbaum's source explained how that last point caught users of one technique: a call placed to a directory-assistance number and then held for hours left "a charge for it on your tape", and the accounting computer "drops a trouble card which goes right into the hands of the security agent if they're looking for blue-box users" ([Rosenbaum, Esquire, 1971](https://www.slate.com/articles/technology/the_spectator/2011/10/the_article_that_inspired_steve_jobs_secrets_of_the_little_blue_.html)).

The article also spread the knowledge it described. A 1976 file of the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) field office in Los Angeles, released to Lapsley and published on the Internet Archive, records a subject who said he first became aware of phone phreaking and blue boxes when he read the October 1971 *Esquire* article, "The Secrets of the Little Blue Box" ([FBI, Phone Phreak Activities Control File, Los Angeles, 1976, released under the Freedom of Information Act, Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/PhonePhreakControlFile)). Reviewing Lapsley's history of the period in 2013, Bryan Alexander summarized the company's response as it escalated: working with the FBI, tracking users and "ultimately recording millions of phone calls" ([Bryan Alexander, "Battling Ma Bell", Reason, 27 May 2013](https://reason.com/2013/05/27/battling-ma-bell/printer/)).

### System No. 5 and System R2: the tones abroad

International traffic kept in-band signalling longer than the domestic network did. CCITT System No. 5, standardized from the Atlantic system after studies of 1960 to 1964, was "used in all of the undersea cables" and, being the only system that tolerated satellite delays, "the system used today for international satellite circuits", C. A. Dahlbom and J. S. Ryan wrote in February 1978; its line signals used "two frequencies, 2400 and 2600 Hz, separately or together in a fully compelled mode", and its address signals an MF code at ten digits a second ([Dahlbom and Ryan, "Common Channel Interoffice Signaling: History and Description of a New Signaling System", Bell System Technical Journal, February 1978](https://archive.org/details/bstj57-2-225)). The international codes of the table above (KP1, KP2, Code 11, Code 12) are System No. 5's.

The other international family, System R2, specified by the CCITT in its 1988 Blue Book, now published by the Telecommunication Standardization Sector of the International Telecommunication Union (ITU-T), for use "within international regions", split the two jobs differently ([ITU-T, Recommendations Q.400 to Q.490, summary, November 1988](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-t/rec/q/T-REC-Q.400-Q.490-198811-I!!SUM-HTM-E.htm)). Its analogue line signalling also used tone on idle, but on "a signalling channel outside the speech frequency band" ([ITU-T, Recommendation Q.411, Blue Book, 1988](https://www.itu.int/rec/dologin_pub.asp?lang=e&id=T-REC-Q.411-198811-I!!PDF-E&type=items)), while its interregister signals were in-band multifrequency pairs, forward on 1380, 1500, 1620, 1740, 1860 and 1980 Hz and backward on 1140, 1020, 900, 780, 660 and 540 Hz, in a compelled code in which each signal is acknowledged before the next ([ITU-T, Recommendation Q.441, Blue Book, 1988](https://www.itu.int/rec/dologin_pub.asp?lang=e&id=T-REC-Q.441-198811-I!!PDF-E&type=items)). An out-of-band line signal cannot be imitated by a tone played into a handset; a compelled register exchange can only be joined by something that answers in turn.

## The red box: how payphones announced coins

Payphones talked to operators about money with sound long before electronics. In the three-slot coin station, "the coins rolled and bounced against gongs", one *bing* for a nickel, *bing-bing* for a dime and a *bong* for a quarter, Bell Laboratories engineers wrote in 1979 ([G. T. Clark, K. Streisand and D. H. Larson, "Traffic Service Position System No. 1: Station Signaling and Announcement Subsystem: Hardware for Automated Coin Toll Service", Bell System Technical Journal, July to August 1979](https://archive.org/details/bstj58-6-1225)). A single-slot coin station introduced in 1966 tested each coin's dimensions, mass and conductivity and drove a pulsed oscillator that gave one beep for a nickel, two for a dime and five for a quarter ([Clark, Streisand and Larson, 1979](https://archive.org/details/bstj58-6-1225)).

When the Bell System set out to let a machine hear those beeps, it reasoned as it had for SF. A single frequency could in principle be recognized, but Bell System experience with MF and Touch-Tone signalling showed that "adequate protection against errors caused by speech or noise could be provided only by using dual-frequency" beeps, so a dual-frequency oscillator assembly went into coin stations from 1975, "deliberately well in advance" of the new service ([Clark, Streisand and Larson, 1979](https://archive.org/details/bstj58-6-1225)). That service was Automated Coin Toll Service (ACTS), which automated most coin-paid long-distance calls, quoted charges by synthesized voice and monitored deposits; it was first put into service in Phoenix, Arizona, on 26 November 1977 and made generally available to the Bell operating companies in mid-1978 ([M. Berger, J. C. Dalby Jr., V. L. Ransom and E. M. Prell, "Traffic Service Position System No. 1: Automated Coin Toll Service: Overall Description and Operational Characteristics", Bell System Technical Journal, July to August 1979](https://archive.org/details/bstj58-6-1207)). AT&T's 1980 manual gives the coin denomination tones:

| Coin station | Coin | Frequencies (Hz) | Pattern |
|---|---|---|---|
| Three-slot | 5 cents | 1050 to 1100 (bell) | one tap |
| Three-slot | 10 cents | 1050 to 1100 (bell) | two taps |
| Three-slot | 25 cents | 800 (gong) | one tap |
| Single-slot | 5 cents | 2200 + 1700 | one "beep" |
| Single-slot | 10 cents | 2200 + 1700 | two "beeps" |
| Single-slot | 25 cents | 2200 + 1700 | five "beeps" |

([AT&T, Notes on the Network, 1980, section 5, Table T, item 32](https://archive.org/details/bitsavers_westernEleesonTheNetwork1980_26249557))

The coin tone receiver was designed against speech, and its designers listed its four requirements: recognize coin tones from coin stations whose timing varied with temperature, line current and wear; identify stations outside requirements; "Operate in the presence of speech"; and "Reject coin simulations" ([Clark, Streisand and Larson, 1979](https://archive.org/details/bstj58-6-1225)). Both tones had to be present at once, "guard energy" at other frequencies blocked detection, the announcement was cut off the moment a coin tone began, and a four-wire terminating set isolated the receiver from speech travelling towards the payphone; the paper describes the trade-off between being "talked down" by real speech and accepting speech as coins as "a compromise but exacting choice" ([Clark, Streisand and Larson, 1979](https://archive.org/details/bstj58-6-1225)). The simulations it had in mind were accidental. A device that produced the right two frequencies with the right timing was, to that receiver, a coin.

That device was the red box. The phreaks' lists defined it in one line: "Make coin tones for payphones" in an underground bulletin board's list dated 20 August 1987, and "Make free phone calls from pay phones by generating quarter tones" in a later, undated list ([The Vandal, "The Underground Box List", 20 August 1987, textfiles.com](http://textfiles.com/phreak/BOXES/boxlist.txt); ["What do all the colored boxes do?", undated list, textfiles.com](http://textfiles.com/phreak/BOXES/allboxes.txt)). A United States (US) patent filed on 28 May 1991 described the same practice from the defender's side: the perpetrator uses "a small tone generator which generates tones emulating the tones used by telephone companies to signal the deposit of coins", and these tones "are usually entered via the transmitter of the coin telephone" ([H. Weaver Jordan, US Patent 5,150,403, "Coin fraud prevention system for coin telephones", filed 28 May 1991, granted 22 September 1992](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5150403A/en)). The red box therefore worked where three conditions met: a coin line served by ACTS, a payphone whose handset microphone reached the line while the tones were heard, and a receiver that could not tell the coin station's oscillator from a copy.

The defences moved into the payphone. The 1991 patent proposed sensing coin-tone frequencies at the handset transmitter, latching the dial so the call could not be placed, and dialling an alarm number to report the fraud ([Jordan, US Patent 5,150,403, 1992](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5150403A/en)). AT&T had already patented, in an application of 30 December 1983, a microcomputer-controlled coin telephone that "prevents coin call fraud and performs all of the coin discrimination functions" ([AT&T Bell Laboratories, US Patent 4,625,078, "Fraud prevention in an electronic coin telephone set", filed 30 December 1983, granted 25 November 1986](https://patents.google.com/patent/US4625078A/en)). A phreak's file of 24 January 1992 conceded that red boxes would not work from the "new digital pay phones" being installed in airports and hotels, nor from customer-owned coin-operated telephones (COCOTs), while claiming they still worked on most payphones in large cities ([Dial Tone, "The Art of Red Boxing", Circle-A Productions, 24 January 1992, textfiles.com](http://textfiles.com/phreak/BOXES/circa-01.txt)); that is a period claim, not a measurement. ACTS itself kept a quieter record: when a coin call ended and the caller walked away from an overtime charge, the system wrote a "walkaway record" so that patterns could be found "to reduce fraudulent use of coin phones" ([Berger, Dalby, Ransom and Prell, 1979](https://archive.org/details/bstj58-6-1207)).

## Green, black, silver and beige: the other colours

The box lists that circulated on bulletin boards in the 1980s and 1990s gave a colour to almost every trick, from the trunk tones to the dual-tone multifrequency (DTMF) keys of Touch-Tone telephones. The archive that preserves the largest collection of them warns that the function it lists for each box "has nothing to do with the plan's actual success at its goal or whether it even comes close" ([textfiles.com, "Phone Phreaking : Boxes and Box Plans", index](http://textfiles.com/phreak/BOXES/)). Read against the Bell System's documents, some colours correspond to a real signal and some do not.

| Box | What the period lists say it did | The legitimate signal or mechanism it imitated |
|---|---|---|
| Blue | "Emulate a true operator by seizing a trunk with a 2600hz tone" | SF supervision at 2600 Hz and MF pulsing ([Notes on the Network, 1980](https://archive.org/details/bitsavers_westernEleesonTheNetwork1980_26249557)) |
| Red | "Make coin tones for payphones" | ACTS coin denomination tones, 2200 + 1700 Hz ([Notes on the Network, Table T](https://archive.org/details/bitsavers_westernEleesonTheNetwork1980_26249557)) |
| Green | "Emulate the Coin Collect, Coin Return, and Ringback tones" | The expanded in-band MF signals between offices ([Notes on the Network, Figure 35](https://archive.org/details/bitsavers_westernEleesonTheNetwork1980_26249557)) |
| Black | "Allows the calling party to not be billed for the call placed" | Answer supervision, the off-hook signal from the called end that starts charging ([Notes on the Network, paragraph 7.35](https://archive.org/details/bitsavers_westernEleesonTheNetwork1980_26249557)) |
| Silver | "Create the DTMF tones for A, B, C and D" | The fourth column of the 16-signal Touch-Tone code ([Notes on the Network, paragraph 12.36](https://archive.org/details/bitsavers_westernEleesonTheNetwork1980_26249557)) |
| Beige | "Lineman's hand set" | A test handset clipped to someone else's line; not a signal at all |

(Quotations in the second column from ["What do all the colored boxes do?", textfiles.com](http://textfiles.com/phreak/BOXES/allboxes.txt), except the red box, from [The Vandal, 1987](http://textfiles.com/phreak/BOXES/boxlist.txt).)

The green box's signals are in the 1960 paper as well, which describes pulses similar to the MF digits used to send coin collect or coin return signals "from a master office to a satellite office not equipped with local coin supervisory equipment" ([Breen and Dahlbom, 1960, section 4.0.3](https://archive.org/details/bstj39-6-1381)). The colour, however, was not stable: the 1987 list defines the green box as a "Hold Button (Best one I know of)" ([The Vandal, 1987](http://textfiles.com/phreak/BOXES/boxlist.txt)). Readers of period files should expect the same name to mean different devices.

The black box worked on the other side of the call, the line being called. Charging began when the called end returned an off-hook signal, and the 1980 manual itself describes calls on which "the called end does not return an off-hook signal", such as business office or repair calls, as no-charge calls ([AT&T, Notes on the Network, 1980, paragraph 7.35](https://archive.org/details/bitsavers_westernEleesonTheNetwork1980_26249557)). A period file of 1992, written for the Greek network, describes the black box in exactly those terms: it makes the exchange believe the called phone is still ringing, so no charge is made ([Fabulist, "The TRUTH about Black Boxing", 1992, textfiles.com](http://textfiles.com/phreak/BOXES/black1.txt)). Rosenbaum's article mentions a self-styled inventor of the black box, an engineer using the pseudonym Alexander Graham Bell, who claimed to sell "black and blue boxes to gamblers and radical heavies" ([Rosenbaum, Esquire, 1971](https://www.slate.com/articles/technology/the_spectator/2011/10/the_article_that_inspired_steve_jobs_secrets_of_the_little_blue_.html)), and the 1992 red-box file already counted black boxes among "relics of the Step-By-Step and Crossbar eras" that had "lost their usefulness" ([Dial Tone, 1992](http://textfiles.com/phreak/BOXES/circa-01.txt)).

The silver box has the best-documented real target. The Touch-Tone code had 16 signals, each a pair drawn from a low group of 697, 770, 852 and 941 Hz and a high group of 1209, 1336, 1477 and 1633 Hz; the 1633 Hz column carried A, B, C and D, which the conventional ten-button pads of subscribers' sets did not have ([AT&T, Notes on the Network, 1980, paragraph 12.36](https://archive.org/details/bitsavers_westernEleesonTheNetwork1980_26249557)). The military Automatic Voice Network (AUTOVON) used those extra keys for call priority. An FBI file of April 1967 describes a conventional ten-button Touch-Tone pad, a Western Electric type 25B3, that "had been modified by inserting two microswitches into the circuit, which opened up the main frequency leads and substituted other frequency leads for them"; a laboratory examination found it able to produce the frequencies of a 16-button dial and so compatible with AUTOVON, up to top-priority Flash Override calls, and pencilled marks beside the keys read FO, F, I and P, showing that "whoever modified this set was aware of the Autovon priority designations"; on 20 April 1967 Bell Telephone Company of Pennsylvania briefed military investigators, noting about 200 AUTOVON circuits in the Philadelphia area and 14 multilevel priority units adaptable to such a device ([FBI, Phone Phreak Activities: AUTOVON Intrusions, 1967 to 1975, released under the Freedom of Information Act, Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/PhonePhreakAUTOVONIntrusions)).

The lists also contain things that never worked. The 1987 list adds, beside a "Blotto Box" said to blow up an area code, the comment "Doubt it works", and the undated list labels its "Rainbow" box "(joke)" ([The Vandal, 1987](http://textfiles.com/phreak/BOXES/boxlist.txt); ["What do all the colored boxes do?"](http://textfiles.com/phreak/BOXES/allboxes.txt)). The Jargon File, the hackers' own dictionary, kept the summary short: there were other colours, red, black and silver among them, "but the blue box was the original and archetype" ([The Jargon File, version 4.4.7, entry "blue box"](https://jargon-file.org/archive/jargon-4.4.7.dos.txt)).

## Why common-channel signalling ended the boxes

The fix was architectural, and the Bell System described it before the boxes were famous. Writing in February 1978, A. E. Ritchie and J. Z. Menard listed the limits of SF and MF signalling: it was slow (connection times of 10 to 20 seconds on multi-link calls, mostly spent signalling), carried few signals, cost a signalling unit at both ends of every voice channel, and, because it used voice frequencies "on the circuits which are used by customers", it was "vulnerable to interference and susceptible to fraud" ([A. E. Ritchie and J. Z. Menard, "Common Channel Interoffice Signaling: An Overview", Bell System Technical Journal, February 1978](https://archive.org/details/bstj57-2-221)). The answer was Common Channel Interoffice Signaling (CCIS), a separate data network between switching offices; it entered service in May 1976 between the last new No. 4A toll crossbar office in Madison, Wisconsin, and the first No. 4 ESS (Electronic Switching System) toll office in Chicago, and AT&T planned to give about 80 percent of intertoll trunks CCIS by 1985 ([Ritchie and Menard, 1978](https://archive.org/details/bstj57-2-221)).

Dahlbom and Ryan stated the consequence in two sentences: "An important aspect of CCIS is the complete separation of trunk control and communication channel functions. Fraudulent manipulation of the telephone network is eliminated in an all CCIS environment" ([Dahlbom and Ryan, 1978](https://archive.org/details/bstj57-2-225)). The same paper expected call set-up to fall from about ten seconds to one or two, and noted that "false disconnects or talk-offs resulting from customer speech characteristics are eliminated", the problem Weaver and Newell had measured in 1954 ([Dahlbom and Ryan, 1978](https://archive.org/details/bstj57-2-225)). Internationally, the CCITT approved the digital version of System No. 6, the common-channel system developed alongside CCIS, in October 1976 ([Dahlbom and Ryan, 1978](https://archive.org/details/bstj57-2-225)); its successor, Signalling System No. 7 (SS7), is introduced in ITU-T Recommendation Q.700, whose edition of March 1993 superseded that of November 1988 ([ITU-T, Recommendation Q.700, Introduction to CCITT Signalling System No. 7](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-Q.700/en)). The Jargon File dates the blue box to the time "before all-digital switches made it possible for the phone companies to move them out of band" ([The Jargon File, 4.4.7](https://jargon-file.org/archive/jargon-4.4.7.dos.txt)).

The separation that ended the boxes created a different trust problem. Common-channel networks trusted the operators connected to them rather than the callers, and in December 2014 Tobias Engel showed at the 31st Chaos Communication Congress (CCC) that companies were selling the ability to track a phone number "with a precision of up to 50 meters" through SS7 ([Tobias Engel, "SS7: Locate. Track. Manipulate.", 31st Chaos Communication Congress, 27 December 2014, media.ccc.de](https://media.ccc.de/v/31c3_-_6249_-_en_-_saal_1_-_201412271715_-_ss7_locate_track_manipulate_-_tobias_engel)). The European Union Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA) assessed the risk in interconnection signalling as "medium to high" in 2018 ([ENISA, "Signalling Security in Telecom SS7/Diameter/5G", March 2018](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/publications/signalling-security-in-telecom-ss7-diameter-5g)). The glossary entry [SS7](/glossary/ss7) and later parts of this series follow that thread; the point here is narrower. A tone a caller could make stopped meaning anything to the network once control moved off the voice path.

## Brazil: orelhões, tokens and the inductive card

Brazil's public telephones took a different road, and the documents show why the red box had no Brazilian equivalent of the same kind. The orelhão, the egg-shaped fibreglass hood that became the country's street telephone, was designed by the architect Chu Ming Silveira of the Companhia Telefônica Brasileira (CTB); the first was installed on Rua Sete de Abril in central São Paulo in 1971, according to an account published in January 2026 ([Metrópoles, article on the origin of the orelhão, 21 January 2026](https://www.metropoles.com/vida-e-estilo/historia-do-orelhao)). For two decades the phones ran on tokens, fichas, sold at newsstands, bakeries and grocers; one gave three minutes on a local call and about 30 seconds on a long-distance one, and the drop of the token into the box gave Portuguese the expression "cair a ficha", for the penny dropping ([Diário do Nordeste, "Fichas, coleção de cartões e filas: como o orelhão marcou gerações no Ceará", 26 January 2026](https://diariodonordeste.verdesmares.com.br/ceara/fichas-colecao-de-cartoes-e-filas-como-o-orelhao-marcou-geracoes-no-ceara-1.3735709)).

Tokens were expensive in their own way. Marcos Assano of the research centre of the state telephone holding Telebrás, the Centro de Pesquisa e Desenvolvimento em Telecomunicações (CPqD), wrote in June 1997 that Brazil had more than 250,000 token phones before the change; that collecting, washing, packing and redistributing tokens cost 50 percent of the cost of minting new ones; that vandalism and attempts to defraud the charging system cost Telebrás about 80 million US dollars a year in maintenance; and that in June 1991 about a third of the token phones were out of service each month ([Marcos Assano, CPqD and Telebrás, "Telefonia pública a cartão indutivo: uma solução adaptada à realidade brasileira", Revista da Sociedade Brasileira de Telecomunicações, vol. 12 no. 1, June 1997](https://jcis.sbrt.org.br/jcis/article/download/215/129/671)).

### How the inductive card works

CPqD studied magnetic cards, smart cards and memory cards and rejected them: magnetic cards needed local power or batteries in the phone, were more exposed to fraud and cost too much for cards with few credits, and chip cards were too expensive for Brazilian conditions, so Telebrás developed its own inductive technology ([Assano, 1997](https://jcis.sbrt.org.br/jcis/article/download/215/129/671)). Each credit unit on the card is a printed secondary winding with a fuse; the reader acts as the primary of a transformer, reads a unit by checking whether its fuse is intact, and collects it by inducing a current strong enough to melt it. "O rompimento do fusível F é um processo irreversível, impossibilitando a regravação do cartão", the engineer wrote: the fuse's rupture is irreversible, so the card cannot be rewritten ([Assano, 1997](https://jcis.sbrt.org.br/jcis/article/download/215/129/671)). Reading needs no galvanic contact and no moving parts; the manufacturing process is cheap at scale but "sensível a pequenos desvios, dificultando a fraude doméstica", sensitive to small deviations and so hard to copy at home; and cards were sold with 100, 90, 75, 50, 35 and 20 credits ([Assano, 1997](https://jcis.sbrt.org.br/jcis/article/download/215/129/671)). In 1997 the author could write that Brazil was "o único país a utilizar esta tecnologia em sistemas de telecomunicações", the only country using the technology in telecommunications ([Assano, 1997](https://jcis.sbrt.org.br/jcis/article/download/215/129/671)).

The architecture is the reverse of ACTS. In the American system the payphone told the network, by in-band tones, that money had been deposited. In the Brazilian card phone the network told the terminal to charge: its telephone interface includes "detetores de pulso de cobrança (por inversão de linha ou freqüência de 12 KHz)", charging-pulse detectors working by line-polarity reversal or by a 12 kHz tone, and the terminal burns credits on the card as the pulses arrive ([Assano, 1997](https://jcis.sbrt.org.br/jcis/article/download/215/129/671)). The terminal also reports to a supervision centre, the Centro de Supervisão Automatizada (CSA), over a 1200 bit/s modem, and event sensors send serious faults such as "a abertura não autorizada do terminal ou o rompimento do cordão", unauthorized opening of the terminal or a cut cord, in real time ([Assano, 1997](https://jcis.sbrt.org.br/jcis/article/download/215/129/671)).

Who invented the inductive card is disputed in court, and the sources do not settle it. Telebrás itself recognized the engineer Nelson Bardini as the card's inventor, according to a 2016 report: he began looking for a way to replace tokens in 1976 in Campinas, filed for a magnetic-card patent in July 1978 and won the Prêmio Landell de Moura in 1982, and the company Signal Card, which financed him, holds the inductive-card patent and has sued since July 1998 for royalties, while the card manufacturers have sought since 2000 to annul the patent ([Gazeta do Povo, "Inventor do cartão telefônico cobra royalties de operadoras em ação milionária", 13 March 2016](https://www.gazetadopovo.com.br/economia/inventor-do-cartao-telefonico-cobra-royalties-de-operadoras-em-acao-milionaria-8nluwosndyyp1phtcht1mkx0j)). The report dates Signal Card's filing to February 1991; the patent office's own 2002 opinion records the application, PI 9006534-4, as filed on 17 December 1990 by Nelson Guilherme Bardini and Geraldo Rospendowiski Junior, and a technical opinion in the file found one claim lacking inventive step against earlier patents and the 1990 Telebrás card ([Gazeta do Povo, 2016](https://www.gazetadopovo.com.br/economia/inventor-do-cartao-telefonico-cobra-royalties-de-operadoras-em-acao-milionaria-8nluwosndyyp1phtcht1mkx0j); [INPI, Nota and Parecer on PI 9006534-4, 20 and 21 May 2002](https://www.gov.br/inpi/pt-br/acesso-a-informacao/manifestacoes-juridicas-de-pi/arquivos/documentos/2002/nota-050-2002.pdf)). CPqD's 1997 paper, for its part, calls the inductive card a Telebrás development ([Assano, 1997](https://jcis.sbrt.org.br/jcis/article/download/215/129/671)).

### Fraud anyway

An irreversible fuse protects the card, not the terminal or the exchange. In 1995, a Jornal Nacional report recalled in 2025 described a fraud that cost the São Paulo operator Telesp more than 10 million reais: two men were arrested in the Praça da Sé making a long-distance call to Ceará, and a fault in the phones' memory let calls run indefinitely "sem registrar os impulsos do cartão magnético", without registering the card's charges ([Diário Online (DOL), "Relembre o truque dos cartões usados em orelhões nos anos 90", 6 February 2025](https://dol.com.br/tuedoide/curiosidades/893492/relembre-o-truque-dos-cartoes-usados-em-orelhoes-nos-anos-90)). In January 2004, a street seller arrested in Curitiba was selling adulterated cards for 6 reais that, he said, gave up to a thousand credits; the cards were made in São Paulo, the police charged him with estelionato (fraud), and the operator, Brasil Telecom, said it did not know of the scam and that its cards held at most 60 credits ([Tribuna do Paraná, "Cartões telefônicos levam vigarista à cadeia", 29 January 2004](https://www.tribunapr.com.br/painel-do-crime/cartoes-telefonicos-levam-vigarista-a-cadeia/amp/)). Neither report explains the technique, and none is given here.

### The end of the orelhão

In January 2026 the Ministério das Comunicações (MCom) reported that about 38,000 public telephones, technically Telefones de Uso Público, were still in the streets, 27,918 of them active in the state of São Paulo; the fixed-telephony concessions of the five companies responsible ended in December 2025 when the contracts were adapted to the authorization regime ([Ministério das Comunicações, "Símbolo urbano por décadas, orelhões entram em processo de desativação, mas seguem onde não há outro serviço de voz", 21 January 2026](https://www.gov.br/mcom/pt-br/noticias/2026/janeiro/simbolo-urbano-por-decadas-orelhoes-entram-em-processo-de-desativacao-mas-seguem-onde-nao-ha-outro-servico-de-voz)). According to the Agência Nacional de Telecomunicações (Anatel), as reported by the ministry, about 9,000 must be maintained until 31 December 2028 where mobile coverage is deficient, and where no card is available they must allow local and national calls to fixed lines free of charge, "porque o custo de produção dos antigos cartões usados para realizar ligações é maior do que o valor da própria chamada", because producing the old cards costs more than the call ([Ministério das Comunicações, 21 January 2026](https://www.gov.br/mcom/pt-br/noticias/2026/janeiro/simbolo-urbano-por-decadas-orelhoes-entram-em-processo-de-desativacao-mas-seguem-onde-nao-ha-outro-servico-de-voz)).

## What the law says

In the United States, the federal access-device statute, 18 U.S.C. 1029, was enacted on 12 October 1984; a 1994 amendment (Public Law 103-414) added the offence that, after renumbering in 1996, is paragraph (a)(7): to knowingly and "with intent to defraud" use, produce, traffic in, have control or custody of, or possess "a telecommunications instrument that has been modified or altered to obtain unauthorized use of telecommunications services" ([Legal Information Institute, 18 U.S. Code 1029, text and amendment notes, read 16 November 2026](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1029)). A first offence under that paragraph carries a fine or up to ten years' imprisonment, or both ([18 U.S. Code 1029(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1029)). The statute turns on intent: the same oscillator in a museum or a lecture is not an offence under it.

In Brazil the cases reported here were prosecuted under general fraud law, the Curitiba seller for estelionato according to the police ([Tribuna do Paraná, 29 January 2004](https://www.tribunapr.com.br/painel-do-crime/cartoes-telefonicos-levam-vigarista-a-cadeia/amp/)). The 2012 law on computer crimes, Lei 12.737, added to the Código Penal an offence of producing, offering, distributing, selling or spreading a "dispositivo ou programa de computador" with the intent of enabling the invasion of a computer device ([Senado Federal, Lei 12.737, 30 November 2012](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/588113/publicacao/15773241)); how that article, its 2021 amendment and Anatel's rules apply to modern hacking gear is the subject of a later part of this series on owning hacking gear legally. This summary reflects the texts as read on 16 November 2026 and is not legal advice.

## What to remember

- The long-distance network signalled in the voice band because its trunks had no direct-current path: a steady 2600 Hz meant idle, and the Bell System's own manual said pure tones near 2600 Hz would make the far end go on-hook.
- Digits travelled as two of six frequencies from 700 to 1700 Hz, with KP and ST around them; the pairs were published in 1960 and again in 1980, and the same pairs also carried coin and operator signals.
- ACTS, from 26 November 1977, listened for 2200 + 1700 Hz beeps (one for a nickel, two for a dime, five for a quarter); its receiver was built to reject speech, not a copy, which is the gap the red box used and the 1990s patents closed at the handset.
- The colour lists mix real targets (blue, red, green, silver) with tricks, misattributions and jokes; read them against the operators' documents.
- Common-channel signalling, from May 1976, ended the boxes by taking control off the voice path; SS7 then raised a different trust problem among operators.
- Brazil skipped coin tones: the exchange sent charging pulses and the card phone burned irreversible fuses on a Telebrás inductive card, which moved fraud to terminal faults and adulterated cards.

- The Bell System published its signalling frequencies in a technical journal in 1960. Was publication a mistake, or would the frequencies have been measured from the line anyway?

- Common-channel signalling ended tone fraud by trusting operators instead of callers, and SS7 abuse followed. Is every fix of this kind a move of trust rather than its removal?
