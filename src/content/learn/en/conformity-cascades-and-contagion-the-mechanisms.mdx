The [opening part of this series](/learn/mass-hysteria-how-groups-come-to-believe-anything) set out the words used for collective belief and a timeline of cases. This part examines the mechanisms that social psychology and economics have proposed to explain them: why people adopt a group's judgement when the evidence is ambiguous, and sometimes when it is not; why they obey; why they misread what others privately think; why it can be rational to follow the crowd; how moods and symptoms pass from person to person; and how expectation alone can make people ill. Each mechanism has a famous experiment, and most famous experiments have later replications, archival critiques or both. After reading, you can say what each mechanism predicts, which findings held and which were weakened, and how they combine in the episodes the rest of the series describes.

## Before the experiments: the replication question

Many of the studies below are textbook classics, and textbooks have not always kept up with their re-examination. In 2015 the Open Science Collaboration repeated 100 experimental and correlational studies from three psychology journals: "Ninety-seven percent of original studies had statistically significant results. Thirty-six percent of replications had statistically significant results", and replication effects "were half the magnitude of original effects" ([Open Science Collaboration, Science, 28 August 2015](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=26315443)). That project did not test the studies in this part, so its numbers say nothing about any one of them; they explain why each section below reports the replications and critiques alongside the original.

## Norms in an ambiguous world: Sherif's moving light

### The experiment

In the 1930s Muzafer Sherif looked for a situation with no objective standard, and found it in the autokinetic effect: "In complete darkness ... a single small light seems to move, and it may appear to move erratically in all directions", even when the viewer knows it is not moving ([Sherif, The Psychology of Social Norms, 1936](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.264611)). The light "was physically stationary during the entire time and was not moved at all during any of the experiments" (same). Male students at Columbia University and New York University judged how far it moved: 19 took part in the individual experiment and 40 in the group experiments, in eight groups of two and eight groups of three, half starting alone and half starting in groups (same). The full quantitative results appeared in his 1935 monograph, *A Study of Some Social Factors in Perception* ([Sherif, Archives of Psychology, 1935, Mead Project transcription](https://www.brocku.ca/MeadProject/Sherif/Sherif_1935a/Sherif_1935a_toc.html)).

### What it found

Alone, each person settled on "a range of extent and a point (a standard or norm) within that range which is peculiar to the individual" ([Sherif, 1936](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.264611)). Put together after working alone, people's ranges and norms "tend to converge. But the convergence is not so close as when they first work in the group situation" (same). And the group's norm stayed with the individual: "When a member of a group faces the same situation subsequently alone, after once the range and norm of his group have been established, he perceives the situation in terms of the range and norm that he brings from the group situation" (same). Most participants did not feel influenced: "the majority of the subjects reported not only that their minds were made up as to the judgment they were going to give before the others spoke, but that they were not influenced by the others in the group" (same).

Sherif drew the conclusion that "the psychological basis of the established social norms, such as stereotypes, fashions, conventions, customs and values, is the formation of common frames of reference as a product of the contact of individuals", while warning that his laboratory situation "does not represent a pressing social situation such as is found in the reality of everyday life" (same). This is the mechanism social psychologists call informational influence: when reality is ambiguous, other people's judgements become evidence. It is the closest laboratory model of the uncertain, threatening situations in which rumours and outbreaks begin.

## A clear line and a unanimous majority: Asch

### The experiments

Solomon Asch asked what happens when the answer is not ambiguous. A group of seven to nine college students compared the length of lines; all but one had been instructed to give unanimous wrong answers on 12 of 18 trials ([Asch, Opinions and Social Pressure, Scientific American, November 1955](https://archive.org/details/solomon-asch_confirmaty)). Of 123 subjects from three institutions, "Whereas in ordinary circumstances individuals matching the lines will make mistakes less than 1 per cent of the time, under group pressure the minority subjects swung to acceptance of the misleading majority's wrong judgments in 36.8 per cent of the selections" (same). About a quarter "were completely independent and never agreed with the erroneous judgments of the majority" (same). In the full report, a control group of 37 judging alone made almost no errors (35 were error-free), while among the experimental subjects one-fourth were errorless and 27% gave majority-determined answers eight to twelve times; the majority "succeeded in deflecting one-third of the minority estimates in its direction", yet "the preponderance of judgments was independent" ([Asch, Psychological Monographs 70(9), 1956](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0093718)).

### What changed the effect

Asch varied the conditions, and the variations are as instructive as the headline. With one opponent the subject erred 3.6% of the time; with two, 13.6%; with three, 31.8%; larger majorities added little (35.1% with four, 37.1% with seven, 31.2% with 15) ([Asch, 1955](https://archive.org/details/solomon-asch_confirmaty)). A single truthful partner reduced the majority's effect to "one fourth"; a dissenter who erred in the other direction still freed subjects, whose errors "dropped to only 9 per cent" (same). When the partner deserted to the majority, errors rose again to nearly the level of a unanimous majority (same). And "Even when the difference between the lines was seven inches, there were still some who yielded to the error of the majority" (same). The interviews showed different reasons: some yielding subjects concluded "I am wrong, they are right", others yielded "not to spoil your results", and "All the yielding subjects underestimated the frequency with which they conformed" (same).

### Was conforming irrational?

A reader of *Scientific American* argued at the time that, for the subject, "the closest approach to objective verification available to the subject was the near unanimous judgment of his equals", so deferring to it was what a scientist should do ([Eisemann, letter, with Asch's reply, January 1956](https://archive.org/details/solomon-asch_confirmaty)). Asch replied that some subjects "although convinced that the majority was judging correctly and that their own judgments were wrong, remained completely independent", and others "complied with the majority although convinced that it was wrong", and concluded: "agreement is not proof, that consensus is baseless unless it is independently confirmed in the experience and insights of each person" (same). The exchange anticipates the economists' informational cascades, below, in which following others is rational and still leads groups astray.

### The replications, across time and countries

Rod Bond and Peter Smith assembled 68 reports covering 133 experiments with 4,627 participants in 17 countries, 97 of them in the United States (US); they found that "conformity has declined since the 1950s" in the American studies and that "Collectivist countries tended to show higher levels of conformity than individualist countries" ([Bond and Smith, Psychological Bulletin 119(1), 1996](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.1.111)). They record the suggestion of several authors that the high conformity of the early 1950s was partly "a product of the McCarthy era", and Perrin and Spencer's description of the Asch effect as "a child of its time" after a British replication found far less of it (same). Brazil appears in that literature: three Brazilian studies were in the meta-analysis; earlier comparisons found greater conformity among Brazilians than among Americans (Sistrunk and Clement), others found levels comparable with Asch's (Whittaker and Meade), and a replication in Brazil by Rodrigues, published in 1982, "produced results similar to Asch" (same).

A preregistered replication in Bern with 210 participants, published in 2023, found "an error rate of 33% for the standard length-of-line experiment which replicates the original findings"; paying for correct answers lowered it to 25%, and with political statements instead of lines the conformity rate was 38% ([Franzen and Mader, PLOS ONE, 29 November 2023](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article/file?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0294325&type=printable)). Of the classic findings in this part, the Asch effect has been among the most consistently reproduced, with its size varying by period, country and incentive.

## Obedience: Milgram, his archives and his replications

### The 1963 report

Stanley Milgram's first report described "a procedure for the study of destructive obedience in the laboratory": a naive subject was ordered to give increasingly severe shocks to a learner, in fact a confederate, on a generator labelled from 15 to 450 volts ([Milgram, Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology 67(4), 1963](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0040525)). "26 Ss obeyed the experimental commands fully, and administered the highest shock on the generator. 14 Ss broke off the experiment at some point after the victim protested" (same). Before the results, 14 Yale seniors had predicted the behaviour of 100 hypothetical subjects: estimates of those who would go to the end "ranged from 0 to 3%", with a mean of 1.2% (same). The procedure "created extreme levels of nervous tension in some Ss", with sweating, trembling, nervous laughter and, in three subjects, "full-blown, uncontrollable seizures" (same). After the session, "A friendly reconciliation was arranged between the subject and the victim" (same).

### What the archives showed

The 65% figure (26 of 40) comes from one condition. Nick Haslam, Steve Loughnan and Gina Perry, working from Milgram's notes, report that he ran 23 conditions, of which only 18 were reported in his monograph; across the 21 conditions in which obedience meant going to 450 volts (740 participants) the overall rate was 43.6%, and eight features of the setting, including the experimenter's directiveness, legitimacy and consistency, group pressure to disobey and the closeness of teacher and learner, influenced it ([Haslam, Loughnan and Perry, PLOS ONE, 2014](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC3976349/fullTextXML)).

Perry, Augustine Brannigan, Richard Wanner and Henderikus Stam then analysed an unpublished study by Milgram's assistant Taketo Murata on whether subjects believed the shocks were real. In 18 of 23 variations, "the mean levels of shock for those who fully believed that they were inflicting pain were lower than for subjects who did not fully believe they were inflicting pain"; the authors conclude that "the perception of pain inflated subject defiance and that subject skepticism inflated their obedience", which "revises our perception of the classical interpretation of the experiment and its putative relevance to the explanation of state atrocities, such as the Holocaust" ([Perry et al., Social Psychology Quarterly, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/0190272519861952)).

### The replications

Ethics rules made a full replication impossible, so Jerry Burger stopped at the 150-volt point where the learner first protests, because "79% of Milgram's participants who went past this point continued to the end"; with 70 adults, screened by clinical psychologists and told they could withdraw and keep their $50, he found that 70% continued past 150 volts, against 82.5% in Milgram's comparable condition, a difference that was not statistically significant; and "participants who saw a confederate refuse the experimenter's instructions obeyed as often as those who saw no model" ([Burger, American Psychologist 64(1), 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0010932)). In Poland, a team at SWPS University used a version with ten buttons and lower "shock" levels with 80 participants and found that "90% of the people were willing to go to the highest level in the experiment" ([Society for Personality and Social Psychology, press release on Doliński et al., March 2017](https://www.spsp.org/news-center/press-releases/milgram-poland-obey)).

<Claim status="established" by="by the original studies and the partial replications">A majority of participants in Milgram-type procedures continue when instructed, well beyond what observers predict</Claim>; <Claim status="disputed" by="by the archival reanalyses">how much of that was obedience to authority, and how much reflected disbelief that anyone was being hurt</Claim> is the open question the archives raised.

## The Stanford prison experiment and Le Texier

### The original account

In August 1971 Philip Zimbardo and colleagues selected 24 male students judged "most stable (physically and mentally)" and randomly assigned half to be guards and half to be prisoners in a mock prison in a Stanford basement ([Haney, Banks and Zimbardo, Interpersonal Dynamics in a Simulated Prison, NCJRS copy](https://www.ncjrs.gov/pdffiles1/Photocopy/10301NCJRS.pdf)). Their report states that five prisoners "had to be released because of extreme emotional depression, crying, rage and acute anxiety", and that the experiment "was terminated prematurely after only six days" (same). Zimbardo later dated it to 14 to 19 August 1971 and explained its end by "the unexpectedly extremely negative reactions of many of the mock guards and prisoners" ([Zimbardo, response to recent criticisms, 2018](https://www.prisonexp.org/response)).

### The archival critique

Thibault Le Texier examined the archives at Stanford and interviewed 15 participants. He concluded that the data were collected in a "biased and incomplete" way, that the study drew on a prison experiment run by students in one of Zimbardo's classes three months earlier, that "the guards received precise instructions regarding the treatment of the prisoners", that the guards "were not told they were subjects", and that "participants were almost never completely immersed by the situation" ([Le Texier, American Psychologist 74(7), 2019](https://doi.org/10.1037/amp0000401)). Haslam, Reicher and Van Bavel, using the same archive, argued that "rather than guards conforming to role of their own accord, experimenters directly encouraged them to adopt roles and act tough in a manner consistent with tenets of identity leadership" ([Haslam, Reicher and Van Bavel, American Psychologist, October 2019](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=31380665)).

### The authors' response

Zimbardo published a reply in 2018. He acknowledged telling the guards they could create "a sense of powerlessness" but "We did not give any formal or detailed instructions about how to be an effective guard"; he argued that the range of guard behaviour, including "good guards", "undercuts any criticisms of the alleged demand characteristics", and that the experiment had been "a model of open science long before practices such as public archiving and data sharing were common" ([Zimbardo, 2018](https://www.prisonexp.org/response)). In 2020 Zimbardo and Craig Haney wrote in *American Psychologist* that the study "endures as a classic, dramatic demonstration" and that they value "intellectually engaged alternative perspectives" but "reject those that are ad hominem, misleading, inaccurate, and unscientific" ([Zimbardo and Haney, American Psychologist, April 2020](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=32250143)); Reicher, Van Bavel and Haslam answered by encouraging "students, teachers, and researchers to engage with this new material themselves" ([Reicher, Van Bavel and Haslam, American Psychologist, April 2020](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=32250145)).

For this series the dispute matters because it moves the explanation from a role that takes over by itself to a leader who presents cruelty as a duty. That is the same distinction the [opening part](/learn/mass-hysteria-how-groups-come-to-believe-anything) draws between episodes that someone steered and episodes that nobody steered.

## Pluralistic ignorance: everyone misreads everyone

### The campus studies

Pluralistic ignorance is the state in which most people privately reject a norm but believe that most others accept it. Christine Schroeder and Deborah Prentice describe students' beliefs about alcohol in those terms: "even though students acknowledge the liberal drinking norm and often conform to it in their outward behavior, they still harbor considerable misgivings", yet "They assume that their peers hold private views that are much more consistent with the drinking norm than are their own" ([Schroeder and Prentice, Journal of Applied Social Psychology, 1998](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/schroeder_prentice_1998_-_exposing_pluralistic_ignorance.pdf)). They summarize Prentice and Miller's study of 1993: students "rated themselves as less comfortable than the average student and as less comfortable than their friends with drinking on campus", overestimated both the support for the norm and its uniformity, and male students "modified their private attitudes over time in the direction of the position they mistakenly assumed to be held by the average student" (same). In their own experiment, first-year students who discussed pluralistic ignorance with peers reported drinking significantly less four to six months later than those in an individual-focused discussion (same).

### A field experiment in Saudi Arabia

Leonardo Bursztyn, Alessandra González and David Yanagizawa-Drott tested the idea at national scale. In their survey of young married Saudi men in early 2018 "only 4 percent had their wife currently working outside the home"; in their experiment 87% agreed that women should be allowed to work outside the home, yet about three-quarters underestimated how many of the other participants agreed ([Bursztyn, González and Yanagizawa-Drott, American Economic Review 110(10), 2020, authors' copy](https://home.uchicago.edu/bursztyn/Misperceived_Norms.pdf)). Telling men the true share raised sign-ups to a job-matching service for their wives from 23% by 9 percentage points; months later the wives of men whose beliefs were corrected were more likely to have applied for a job outside the home (up 10 points from 6%) and to have interviewed for one (up 5 points from 1%) (same).

### Why people enforce norms they do not believe

Robb Willer, Ko Kuwabara and Michael Macy proposed a darker loop. In laboratory studies with a wine tasting and an academic text, participants "who conformed to a norm due to social pressure then falsely enforced the norm by publicly criticizing a lone deviant", because enforcement "effectively signals the enforcer's genuine support for the norm"; they warn of "a vicious cycle in which perceived pressures to conform to and falsely enforce an unpopular norm reinforce one another" ([Willer, Kuwabara and Macy, American Journal of Sociology 115(2), September 2009](https://doi.org/10.1086/599250)). Their list of examples includes "Witch trials: affirming the credibility of one's confession by revealing the names of other religious or political heretics, thereby perpetuating the anxiety that fuels the accusations" (same).

Minjae Kim and Ezra Zuckerman Sivan tested when audiences see through this. Their experiments suggest that when enforcement requires the enforcer to interrupt the flow of interaction on their own initiative, "suspicions are aroused because the enforcers could have remained silent", so that "norms might frequently be underenforced" ([Kim and Zuckerman Sivan, Sociological Science, 25 October 2017](https://www.sociologicalscience.com/download/vol-4/october/SocSci_v4_580to610.pdf)). <Claim status="disputed" by="by Kim and Zuckerman Sivan's experiments">That false enforcement reliably makes the enforcer look sincere</Claim> depends on how the enforcement happens. The part on [witch hunts and moral panics](/learn/witch-hunts-and-moral-panics) returns to accusation as self-protection.

## Informational cascades: when following the crowd is rational

### The theory

Economists offered an account in which no one is irrational. Sushil Bikhchandani, David Hirshleifer and Ivo Welch defined it in 1992: "An informational cascade occurs when it is optimal for an individual, having observed the actions of those ahead of him, to follow the behavior of the preceding individual without regard to his own information" ([Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch, Journal of Political Economy 100(5), October 1992](https://doi.org/10.1086/261849)). Because people "rapidly converge on one action on the basis of some but very little information", the resulting consensus is fragile: "If even a little new information arrives ... the social equilibrium may radically shift", which they offer as an explanation of "rapid and short-lived fluctuations such as fads, fashions, booms, and crashes" (same). The glossary's [information cascade](/glossary/information-cascade) covers both this economists' sense and the reshare trees of network research.

### The laboratory

Lisa Anderson and Charles Holt tested the model with urns: subjects saw a private draw, announced in sequence which urn they thought was in use, and were paid for being right ([Anderson and Holt, American Economic Review 87(5), December 1997, record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:87:y:1997:i:5:p:847-62)). Where earlier announcements created an imbalance strong enough to outweigh a private signal, cascade behaviour "was observed in 41 of the 56 periods" (same). They also observed "reverse" cascades, in which early unrepresentative signals led everyone after them to the wrong urn, and found that about a quarter of decisions followed private information against the Bayesian prediction (26%) (same). Cascades form, then, but not every time, and people sometimes break them by trusting their own evidence.

### Cultural markets and ratings

Two field experiments measured herding in culture and opinion. In an artificial music market, 14,341 participants downloaded unknown songs with or without seeing others' downloads; "Increasing the strength of social influence increased both inequality and unpredictability of success", and "The best songs rarely did poorly, and the worst rarely did well, but any other result was possible" ([Salganik, Dodds and Watts, Science, 10 February 2006](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1121066)). On a social news site, a randomized up-vote "increased the likelihood of positive ratings by 32% and created accumulating positive herding that increased final ratings by 25% on average", whereas negative manipulation was corrected by users ([Muchnik, Aral and Taylor, Science, 9 August 2013](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=23929980)). Fake engagement exploits exactly this asymmetry, as the psyops part on [bots, sockpuppets and astroturfing](/learn/bots-sockpuppets-and-astroturfing) documents.

## Emotional contagion

### The concept

Hatfield and colleagues defined emotional contagion as "the tendency to automatically mimic and synchronize facial expressions, vocalizations, postures, and movements with those of another person and, consequently, to converge emotionally" ([Michalec et al., Frontiers in Psychology, 2025, quoting Hatfield et al.](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12164912/fullTextXML)). A review of the field relates it to arousal and empathy and to the facial, vocal and postural mimicry through which people "align with the emotional state of the other person" ([Herrando and Constantinides, Frontiers in Psychology, 2021](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8322226/fullTextXML)).

### The Facebook experiment of 2014

The best-known test online was run on Facebook's own users. Adam Kramer of Facebook, with Jamie Guillory and Jeffrey Hancock of Cornell University, reported "a massive (N = 689,003) experiment on Facebook" in which the amount of positive or negative content in users' News Feeds was reduced: "When positive expressions were reduced, people produced fewer positive posts and more negative posts; when negative expressions were reduced, the opposite pattern occurred" ([Kramer, Guillory and Hancock, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS), 17 June 2014, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=24889601)). The full paper gives the size of the effects. When positive posts were reduced, the share of positive words in people's own updates fell by 0.1 percentage points (Cohen's d = 0.02) and the share of negative words rose by 0.04 points (d = 0.001); when negative posts were reduced, negative words fell by 0.07 points (d = 0.02) and positive words rose by 0.06 points (d = 0.008). The experiment ran for one week, 11 to 18 January 2012, with about 155,000 participants per condition who posted at least one status update, and analysed over 3 million posts containing over 122 million words, counted with the Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count software (LIWC2007) ([Kramer, Guillory and Hancock, PNAS, 2014, full text](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1320040111)). The authors acknowledged that earlier network evidence for longer-lasting mood transfer was "controversial" (same). Kramer later wrote that the study had "very minimally" deprioritized content for "about 0.04% of users, or 1 in 2500" for "one week, in early 2012" ([Kramer, statement quoted by James Grimmelmann, 30 June 2014](https://laboratorium.net/archive/2014/06/30/parsing_the_facebook_studys_authorship_and_review)).

### The ethics dispute

The paper said the work "was consistent with Facebook's Data Use Policy, to which all users agree prior to creating an account on Facebook, constituting informed consent for this research" ([Retraction Watch, 3 July 2014, quoting the paper](https://retractionwatch.com/2014/07/03/rapid-mood-swing-pnas-issues-expression-of-concern-for-controversial-facebook-study/); [the paper](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1320040111)). On 3 July 2014 the journal's editor-in-chief, Inder Verma, published an Editorial Expression of Concern: as a private company Facebook "was under no obligation to conform to the provisions of the Common Rule", but "It is nevertheless a matter of concern that the collection of the data by Facebook may have involved practices that were not fully consistent with the principles of obtaining informed consent and allowing participants to opt out" ([Retraction Watch, 3 July 2014, quoting the Expression of Concern](https://retractionwatch.com/2014/07/03/rapid-mood-swing-pnas-issues-expression-of-concern-for-controversial-facebook-study/); [PNAS, Editorial Expression of Concern, 3 July 2014](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1412469111)). Cornell said its researchers "did not participate in data collection and did not have access to user data", so its institutional review board "concluded that he was not directly engaged in human research" ([Cornell University statement, quoted by Grimmelmann, 2014](https://laboratorium.net/archive/2014/06/30/parsing_the_facebook_studys_authorship_and_review)). In October 2014 Facebook's chief technology officer, Mike Schroepfer, wrote that "we were unprepared for the reaction the paper received", that "there are things we should have done differently", and announced an enhanced review process for research on "content that may be considered deeply personal (such as emotions)" ([Schroepfer, Research at Facebook, 2 October 2014](https://about.fb.com/news/2014/10/research-at-facebook/)).

A separate study by Facebook and academic researchers used weather as a natural experiment: rainfall changed the emotional content of status messages, and "For every one person affected directly, rainfall alters the emotional expression of about one to two other people" in other cities ([Coviello et al., PLOS ONE, 12 March 2014](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC3951248/fullTextXML)). Both studies measured words in posts, not felt emotion; what the posts mean for mood is part of the dispute, and the part on [social media and contagion](/learn/social-media-and-the-contagion-of-symptoms-and-beliefs) takes it further.

## Contagion in networks: what correlation can and cannot show

### Obesity, happiness and the critics

Nicholas Christakis and James Fowler analysed 12,067 people followed from 1971 to 2003 in the Framingham Heart Study and reported that "A person's chances of becoming obese increased by 57% ... if he or she had a friend who became obese", with clusters extending to "three degrees of separation" ([Christakis and Fowler, New England Journal of Medicine, 26 July 2007](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=17652652)). Statisticians challenged the inference. Russell Lyons argued that the conclusions of that series of papers "do not follow from Christakis and Fowler's statistical analyses" and that the studies "even provide some evidence against the existence of such transmission" ([Lyons, arXiv 1007.2876, version of May 2011](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1007.2876)). Cosma Shalizi and Andrew Thomas showed more generally, in a paper titled "Homophily and Contagion Are Generically Confounded in Observational Social Network Studies", that homophily (the tendency of similar people to form ties), contagion and individual causes are confounded, and that "Distinguishing them from one another requires strong assumptions" ([Shalizi and Thomas, arXiv 1004.4704](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1004.4704)). <Claim status="disputed" by="by Lyons and by Shalizi and Thomas">That observational network data show traits spreading through ties</Claim> remains contested.

### Complex contagion in an experiment

Centola avoided the confound by building the networks himself. In artificially structured online communities, "Individual adoption was much more likely when participants received social reinforcement from multiple neighbors", and a health behaviour "spread farther and faster across clustered-lattice networks than across corresponding random networks" ([Centola, Science, 3 September 2010](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=20813952)). Behaviours that need reinforcement, unlike a piece of news, travel best through dense clusters of mutual contacts: a school, a convent, a factory floor, a closed messaging group.

## Social proof and its boomerang

Robert Cialdini's principle of social proof, that "Especially when they are uncertain, people will look to the actions and behaviors of others to determine their own" ([Influence At Work, The Principles of Persuasion](https://www.influenceatwork.com/7-principles-of-persuasion/)), and the mixed replications of the hotel-towel experiments are set out in the psyops part on [the human attack surface](/learn/the-human-attack-surface-why-repetition-works). One further field experiment shows how a norm message can backfire. Households told their energy use compared with the neighbourhood average moved towards the average from both sides: a descriptive norm "produced either desirable energy savings or the undesirable boomerang effect, depending on whether households were already consuming at a low or high rate", and adding a message of approval or disapproval "eliminated the boomerang effect" ([Schultz et al., Psychological Science, May 2007](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=17576283)). A warning that "everyone" is falling ill, buying or panicking is itself a descriptive norm.

## The nocebo effect: expectation that makes people ill

### Definitions

Luana Colloca and Franklin Miller describe the nocebo effect as the "negative counterpart of the placebo phenomenon", produced by "anticipation of and information about negative outcomes", by "the prior experience of negative therapeutic outcomes" and by "observation of other patients' negative outcomes"; healthy subjects "given a sham radiofrequency stimulus and informed that an electrical current was passing through their heads experienced headache" ([Colloca and Miller, Psychosomatic Medicine, 2011](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=3167012&retmode=xml)). They distinguish "apparent" nocebo effects, adverse events reported in the placebo arm of a trial, from true ones, which can be identified only against a no-treatment group (same).

### Laboratory analogues of outbreaks

Two experiments reproduce the core of mass psychogenic illness. Students who inhaled an inert placebo described as a suspected environmental toxin reported more of the four specified symptoms, and "Changes in reported symptoms were significantly associated with changes in unobtrusively observed behavior"; watching a confederate display symptoms increased them among women ([Lorber, Mazzoni and Kirsch, Annals of Behavioral Medicine, February 2007](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=17291177)). In a follow-up, "observation of symptom displays increased reports of expected symptoms significantly" ([Mazzoni et al., Health Psychology, March 2010](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=20230091)).

### Trials, statins and vaccines

In the SAMSON trial, 60 patients who had stopped statins because of side effects took a statin, a placebo or nothing in randomly ordered months; average symptom intensity was 15.4 in placebo months, 16.3 in statin months and 8.0 in months without tablets, and after seeing their results half restarted statins ([British Heart Foundation, November 2020](https://www.bhf.org.uk/what-we-do/news-from-the-bhf/news-archive/2020/november/no-difference-in-side-effects-with-a-statin-or-dummy-pill); [Wood et al., New England Journal of Medicine, 26 November 2020, record](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=33196154)). A meta-analysis of 12 articles reporting adverse events for 45,380 participants in trials of vaccines against coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) found that "systemic AEs were experienced by 35% of placebo recipients after the first dose", and that responses in placebo arms "accounted for 76% of systemic AEs after the first COVID-19 vaccine dose and 52% after the second dose", while significantly more adverse events were reported in the vaccine groups ([Haas et al., JAMA Network Open, 18 January 2022](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8767431/fullTextXML)).

### Media, Wi-Fi and wind turbines

The nocebo link to media is measured too. A systematic review of 31 blind provocation experiments with 725 people who reported electromagnetic hypersensitivity found "no evidence of an improved ability to detect EMF" (electromagnetic fields), while noting that the symptoms "can be severe and are sometimes disabling" ([Rubin, Das Munshi and Wessely, Psychosomatic Medicine, March 2005](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=15784787)). In a later experiment, 82 of 147 participants (54%) reported symptoms they attributed to a sham Wi-Fi exposure, and a television report on the dangers of Wi-Fi increased worry and, among anxious participants, symptoms ([Witthöft and Rubin, Journal of Psychosomatic Research, March 2013](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=23438710)). In a double-blind study of infrasound, 54 volunteers shown material from the internet suggesting that infrasound from wind turbines causes symptoms reported more symptoms "during exposure to both infrasound and sham infrasound", while the low-expectancy group did not ([Crichton et al., Health Psychology, April 2014](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=23477573)). The symptoms in these studies are real; what the studies show is that their trigger was expectation.

## How the mechanisms combine

None of these mechanisms alone produces an outbreak, a panic or a mania; the episodes in the rest of the series show them in combination. An ambiguous threat, an odour or a rumour, is the situation in which Sherif found that others' judgements become evidence ([Sherif, 1936](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.264611)). Visible symptoms in a classmate supply the modelling that increased symptoms in the laboratory ([Mazzoni et al., 2010](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=20230091)), and an expectation about a toxin supplies the nocebo ([Lorber et al., 2007](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=17291177)). A sequence of people acting on what those before them did can lock a group into a choice its private information does not support, and the same model predicts that new public information can break it ([Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch, 1992](https://doi.org/10.1086/261849)). Private doubts kept quiet sustain a norm that few hold ([Schroeder and Prentice, 1998](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/schroeder_prentice_1998_-_exposing_pluralistic_ignorance.pdf)), and public enforcement by doubters can deepen it ([Willer, Kuwabara and Macy, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1086/599250)). And a leader who presents a harsh role as a duty, rather than the role itself, is what the archival critics found in the Stanford prison experiment ([Haslam, Reicher and Van Bavel, 2019](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=31380665)).

Two findings point to what interrupts the chain. In Asch's experiments a single dissenter removed most of the majority's effect ([Asch, 1955](https://archive.org/details/solomon-asch_confirmaty)); in the cascade model a little public information can overturn a consensus built on little information ([Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch, 1992](https://doi.org/10.1086/261849)); and in Saudi Arabia telling people what others privately thought changed what they did ([Bursztyn et al., 2020](https://home.uchicago.edu/bursztyn/Misperceived_Norms.pdf)). The part on [what ends an outbreak](/learn/what-ends-an-outbreak-and-what-helps) tests how far such findings carry outside the laboratory.

## What to remember

- Sherif showed that in an ambiguous situation people converge on a group norm and keep it when alone, mostly without feeling influenced.
- Asch's unanimous majorities drew about a third of judgements into error; one dissenting partner removed most of the effect; a meta-analysis of 133 experiments in 17 countries, including Brazil, replicated it, with lower conformity in the US after the 1950s and higher in collectivist countries.
- Milgram's 65% came from one of 23 conditions; the average across 21 was 43.6%; archival data suggest that disbelief in the shocks inflated obedience; partial replications in 2009 and 2017 still found most participants continuing.
- The Stanford prison experiment's guards were instructed and encouraged, according to archival critiques; its authors reject those critiques as misleading.
- Pluralistic ignorance sustains norms few privately hold; correcting it changed behaviour in Saudi Arabia; people may enforce such norms to look sincere, though audiences can grow suspicious.
- Informational cascades make following others rational and consensus fragile; cascades formed in most possible periods in the laboratory, including wrong ones.
- Emotional contagion was measured on 689,003 Facebook users without their specific consent; the journal published an expression of concern, and observational network evidence for contagion is disputed because of homophily.
- The nocebo effect turns expectation into real symptoms; laboratory analogues of mass psychogenic illness, trial placebo arms and media experiments all reproduce it.
