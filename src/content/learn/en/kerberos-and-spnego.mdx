## The promise: authentication with no prompt

A user signs in to a domain-joined workstation in the morning and, for the rest of the day, opens internal applications that simply know who they are - no login page, no password, no visible ceremony. That experience is Kerberos, and when it reaches a web application through the browser, the carrier is SPNEGO. Identity products expose it as an adapter or an "Integrated Windows Authentication" option; understanding the machinery underneath is what turns its configuration fields from folklore into engineering.

## Tickets: the KDC's two conversations

Kerberos is a ticket system operated by the **KDC** - the Key Distribution Center, which in an Active Directory world lives on the domain controllers. The KDC holds two logical services, and the whole protocol is two conversations with them.

At workstation login, the client talks to the **Authentication Service** and proves knowledge of the user's password - modern deployments wrap this initial proof in pre-authentication - receiving in return a **Ticket-Granting Ticket (TGT)**: a time-limited credential that says "this principal authenticated recently" without ever sending the password anywhere again.

When the user then needs a specific service, the client presents the TGT to the **Ticket-Granting Service** and asks for a **service ticket** for that service. The service ticket is encrypted with a key only the target service (and the KDC) knows - so when the client hands the ticket to the service, the service can decrypt and trust it **without contacting the KDC at all**. That offline verifiability is the elegance of the design: the KDC is consulted at login and at first use of each service, not on every request.

## SPNs and keytabs: naming and keying the service

Two artifacts make the service side work, and they are exactly the fields an adapter asks for.

The **SPN** - Service Principal Name - is the service's identity in the ticket system, conventionally shaped like `HTTP/sso.example.com@REALM`. When the browser wants a ticket "for that website," the SPN is how the KDC knows which service key to encrypt it with. A missing or duplicated SPN registration is the single most common reason silent SSO (single sign-on) silently is not.

The **keytab** is a file holding the service's long-term key - the material that lets a non-Windows service (an identity server on Linux, say) decrypt the tickets clients bring, without the service itself joining the domain interactively. It is generated against the service account that owns the SPN, it is a credential in file form, and it is protected and rotated like one. Realm, KDC address, service account, keytab: every Kerberos adapter's configuration page is this paragraph as a form.

## SPNEGO: the ticket meets HTTP

Browsers do not speak raw Kerberos to websites; they speak **SPNEGO** - the Simple and Protected GSS-API Negotiation Mechanism - inside HTTP. The dance is short: the server challenges with `WWW-Authenticate: Negotiate`; a browser that trusts the site for integrated authentication obtains a service ticket for the site's SPN and replays the request with the ticket wrapped in the `Authorization: Negotiate` header; the server validates it with the keytab and the user is authenticated - zero prompts, one round trip of ceremony.

The conditional in the middle is the operational catch: **browser trust is configuration**. Browsers only attempt Negotiate against sites an administrator has marked as intranet/trusted for integrated authentication; against everything else they quietly fall back. Which is why real deployments pair the silent path with a fallback path - try Kerberos, fall back to the login form - the exact pattern [authentication policy trees](/learn/pingfederate-authentication-policies) express in one branch.

## The short list of ways it breaks

Kerberos failures cluster tightly, and the list is worth memorizing. **Clock skew**: tickets are time-stamped and tolerance is minutes - unsynchronized clocks are failure mode number one, and time service health is Kerberos health. **SPN problems**: unregistered or duplicate SPNs mean the KDC issues tickets for the wrong key or none at all. **Keytab drift**: the service account's key changed (password reset) and the keytab still holds the old one. **Browser scope**: the site is not in the trusted zone, so the browser never tries. Four causes cover most incidents - and every one of them is checkable before a packet capture.

## The three exchanges, key by key

The protocol is [RFC 4120](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4120) (July 2005, a Proposed Standard), updated since by thirteen RFCs, among them RFC 6113 (a framework for pre-authentication), RFC 6649 and RFC 8429 (retiring weak encryption types) and RFC 8062 (anonymity) ([RFC Editor, RFC 4120](https://www.rfc-editor.org/info/rfc4120)); the steps below are RFC 4120's own. The KDC keeps the secret key of every principal, users and servers alike ([section 1.1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4120.html#section-1.1)), and each message part is sealed in a stated key ([section 7.5.1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4120.html#section-7.5.1)). In the **AS exchange** the client may send PA-ENC-TIMESTAMP, its current time encrypted in its own long-term key ([section 5.2.7.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4120.html#section-5.2.7.2)); the reply carries the TGT, encrypted in the TGS's long-term key, and a part encrypted in the client's key that holds the TGS session key, the nonce and the ticket's times ([sections 3.1.3](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4120.html#section-3.1.3) and [5.4.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4120.html#section-5.4.2)). In the **TGS exchange** the client sends the TGT with an authenticator, its name and its time, encrypted in the TGS session key; the reply carries the service ticket, encrypted in the service's long-term key, and the service session key in a part encrypted in the TGS session key ([sections 3.3](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4120.html#section-3.3) and [5.5.1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4120.html#section-5.5.1)). In the **AP exchange** the client presents that ticket with a fresh authenticator in the service session key, and the AP-REP, when mutual authentication is asked for, comes back in the same key ([sections 3.2.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4120.html#section-3.2.2) and [3.2.4](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4120.html#section-3.2.4)). A ticket holds the session key, the client's name and the ticket's times, encrypted ([section 5.3](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4120.html#section-5.3)).

The failures have names. A KDC that requires pre-authentication answers a request without it with KDC_ERR_PREAUTH_REQUIRED and a METHOD-DATA listing the mechanisms it accepts ([section 3.1.3](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4120.html#section-3.1.3)); Microsoft notes that most MIT-Kerberos clients then send the pre-authentication, "in which case the error can be ignored" ([Microsoft Learn, event 4769](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/previous-versions/windows/it-pro/windows-10/security/threat-protection/auditing/event-4769)). The TGS accepts no expired ticket ([section 3.3](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4120.html#section-3.3)). KDC_ERR_S_PRINCIPAL_UNKNOWN means "Server not found in Kerberos database" ([section 7.5.9](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4120.html#section-7.5.9)), which Microsoft explains as a domain controller that "can't find the server's name in Active Directory" ([event 4769](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/previous-versions/windows/it-pro/windows-10/security/threat-protection/auditing/event-4769)). The service decrypts the ticket first and answers KRB_AP_ERR_BAD_INTEGRITY when that fails ("chances are good that different keys were used to encrypt and decrypt"), as when the SPN is registered to another account; then compares the authenticator's time with its own and answers KRB_AP_ERR_SKEW when the two "differ by more than the allowable clock skew (e.g., 5 minutes)"; then answers KRB_AP_ERR_TKT_EXPIRED for a ticket past its end time by more than that skew ([section 3.2.3](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4120.html#section-3.2.3)). The RFC puts the skew "typically on the order of 5 minutes" ([section 1.6](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4120.html#section-1.6)); the actual defaults are vendor settings: 5 minutes for Active Directory's "Maximum tolerance for computer clock synchronization", in the Default Domain Policy and as the domain controller's effective default ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/previous-versions/windows/it-pro/windows-10/security/threat-protection/security-policy-settings/maximum-tolerance-for-computer-clock-synchronization)), and 300 seconds for MIT Kerberos's clockskew ([MIT Kerberos 1.22.2, krb5.conf](https://web.mit.edu/kerberos/krb5-latest/doc/admin/conf_files/krb5_conf.html)). RFC 4120 does not say what a KDC does with an encrypted timestamp outside the skew; Microsoft documents KRB_AP_ERR_SKEW for a TGT request whose timestamp differs from the server's by more than that tolerance ([Microsoft Learn, event 4768](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/previous-versions/windows/it-pro/windows-10/security/threat-protection/auditing/event-4768)). MIT clients correct for the difference between their clock and the KDC's by default (kdc_timesync, [krb5.conf](https://web.mit.edu/kerberos/krb5-latest/doc/admin/conf_files/krb5_conf.html)), an offset RFC 4120 lets a client keep ([section 3.1.5](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4120.html#section-3.1.5)).

## Where this lands in an identity product

Map the machinery onto the [Kerberos adapter](/learn/pingfederate-authentication-adapters) and the configuration explains itself: the realm and KDC locate the ticket authority, the service account and keytab let the server decrypt what browsers bring, and the surrounding policy tree supplies the fallback for every client outside the silent path's reach. The prompt-free morning is not magic - it is tickets, names, keys, and clocks, each of which you can now name.
