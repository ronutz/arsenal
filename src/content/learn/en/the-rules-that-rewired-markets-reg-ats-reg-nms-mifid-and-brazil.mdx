In January 2005 the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) executed about 79.1% of the share volume in the stocks it listed; by October 2009 the figure was 25.1%, according to the United States (US) Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) ([SEC, Concept Release on Equity Market Structure, 21 January 2010](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-01-21/html/2010-1045.htm)). Technology made that possible, as [The screens arrive](/learn/the-screens-arrive-electronic-exchanges-from-nasdaq-to-globex) showed. This part of the series opened by [Electronic trading: from the pit to the microsecond](/learn/electronic-trading-from-the-pit-to-the-microsecond) reads the rules that governed the change: the US Order Handling Rules (1996) and Regulation ATS (1998), decimal prices (2001), Regulation NMS (2005), MiFID (2007) and MiFID II (2018) in the European Union (EU), and Brazil's path from the merger that created BM&FBOVESPA (Bolsa de Valores, Mercadorias e Futuros) in 2008 to B3 (Brasil, Bolsa, Balcão) in 2017, under the rules of the Comissão de Valores Mobiliários (CVM). After reading, you can say what each rule required and when, follow a trade-through on a worked example, read the European clock table, explain why Brazil still has one exchange under rules written for several, and weigh the evidence for and against fragmentation.

## The tension every rule tries to settle

US law has asked the SEC since 1975 to build a "national market system". In its 2005 rule the SEC described the task as a balance between two kinds of competition: competition among markets, which pushes venues to innovate and cut fees, and competition among orders, which needs buyers and sellers to meet in one place ([SEC, Regulation NMS, 29 June 2005](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2005-06-29/pdf/05-11802.pdf)). It wanted to avoid two extremes: "Isolated markets that trade an NMS stock without regard to trading in other markets and thereby fragment the competition among buyers and sellers in that stock", and "a totally centralized system that loses the benefits of vigorous competition and innovation among individual markets".

The SEC put the cost of getting it wrong plainly in 2010: when many trading centres compete for orders in the same stock, "such competition can lead to the fragmentation of order flow in that stock. Fragmentation can inhibit the interaction of investor orders and thereby impair certain efficiencies and the best execution of investors' orders" ([SEC, 21 January 2010](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-01-21/html/2010-1045.htm)). Every rule in this part chooses a point between those poles, and Europe and Brazil chose differently from the United States.

## The Order Handling Rules (1996): quotes made public

The first push came from a finding about dealers. The SEC Historical Society recounts that in 1994 the economists William Christie and Paul Schultz asked why Nasdaq market makers avoided odd-eighth quotes, and that market makers had been quoting some 70 to 100 heavily traded stocks in quarters, widening spreads ([SEC Historical Society, Increments and ECNs](https://sechistorical.org/exhibition/transformation-regulation-equities-market-structure-1934-to-2018-2/rulemaking-and-linkages-1975-2005/increments-and-ecns/)).

In 1996 the SEC adopted a Display Rule requiring dealers and specialists to show customer limit orders priced better than their own quotes, and amended its Quote Rule so that a market maker had to make public any better price it quoted privately on certain electronic communications networks (ECNs) ([SEC, Order Execution Obligations, 12 September 1996](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1996-09-12/html/96-23210.htm)). The rules took effect on 10 January 1997. The release recorded the scale of private trading: in 1993 all proprietary trading systems together carried 13% of volume in Nasdaq National Market securities, and by January 1996 Instinet alone carried about 15% of Nasdaq volume. The SEC Historical Society credits these Order Handling Rules with the rapid creation of new ECNs such as Island and Archipelago ([SEC Historical Society, Increments and ECNs](https://sechistorical.org/exhibition/transformation-regulation-equities-market-structure-1934-to-2018-2/rulemaking-and-linkages-1975-2005/increments-and-ecns/)).

## Regulation ATS (1998): what an exchange is

By 1998 the new systems were too large to leave outside the national market. The SEC wrote that alternative trading systems "now handle more than twenty percent of the orders in securities listed on The Nasdaq Stock Market", and almost four percent of orders in exchange-listed securities; that Nasdaq market makers had used Instinet as a private market where they "were able to quote prices better than those made available to public investors"; and that these systems were "private, available only to chosen subscribers" yet regulated as broker-dealers rather than as exchanges ([SEC, Regulation of Exchanges and Alternative Trading Systems, 22 December 1998](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1998-12-22/pdf/98-33299.pdf)).

Regulation ATS, adopted in Release 34-40760 and effective on 21 April 1999, gave each alternative trading system (ATS) a choice: register as a national securities exchange, or register as a broker-dealer and comply with Regulation ATS "depending on their activities and trading volume" ([SEC, 22 December 1998](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1998-12-22/pdf/98-33299.pdf)). The obligations rose with market share:

| Share of trading volume | What Regulation ATS required |
|---|---|
| Under 5% in all securities traded | File a notice of operation and quarterly reports, keep records including an audit trail, avoid the words "exchange" or "stock market" in the name |
| 5% or more in a national market system security | Link to a registered market and send the best-priced orders displayed in the system into the public quote stream, under the same execution priority rules as that market's members |
| 20% or more in any single security | Grant or deny access on objective, non-discriminatory standards, and keep adequate systems capacity, integrity and contingency planning |

The same release redefined an exchange: under the new Rule 3b-16, a system is covered when it "brings together the orders of multiple buyers and sellers" using established, non-discretionary methods ([SEC, 22 December 1998](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1998-12-22/pdf/98-33299.pdf)). It also aimed to "accommodate the registration of proprietary alternative trading systems as exchanges, and provide an opportunity for registered exchanges to better compete". The SEC Historical Society notes that Island ECN applied to become an exchange in 1999 and that other ECNs followed in the early 2000s ([SEC Historical Society, Reg ATS](https://sechistorical.org/exhibition/transformation-regulation-equities-market-structure-1934-to-2018-2/rulemaking-and-linkages-1975-2005/reg-ats/)).

The rule kept evolving. In 2018 the SEC adopted Form ATS-N, which requires ATSs that trade national market system stocks, "including so called 'dark pools'", to disclose how they operate and what their broker-dealer operator does, with public posting of the filings; it took effect on 9 October 2018 ([SEC, Regulation of NMS Stock Alternative Trading Systems, 7 August 2018](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2018-08-07/pdf/2018-15896.pdf)). A 2022 proposal to extend the definition of an exchange to systems that use "non-firm trading interest and communication protocols to bring together buyers and sellers" was among the proposals the SEC formally withdrew on 17 June 2025 ([SEC, Withdrawal of Proposed Regulatory Actions, 17 June 2025](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-06-17/html/2025-11110.htm)).

## Decimalization (2001): from eighths to pennies

American shares were priced in fractions of a dollar until 2001. The SEC Historical Society traces the eighth to the Spanish dollar and its "pieces of eight", and records that Congress introduced a Common Cents Stock Pricing Act in 1997 before the SEC took up the matter itself; in the same year a market maker's move to quoting in sixteenths was copied by the other markets within days ([SEC Historical Society, Increments and ECNs](https://sechistorical.org/exhibition/transformation-regulation-equities-market-structure-1934-to-2018-2/rulemaking-and-linkages-1975-2005/increments-and-ecns/)). Toronto had moved first: TMX Group records that in 1996 the Toronto Stock Exchange "became the first exchange in North America to introduce decimal trading" ([TMX Group, History at a Glance](https://www.courthouselibrary.ca/sites/default/files/inline-files/TMX_GroupHistoryAtAGlance.pdf)).

An SEC order of 8 June 2000 directed the exchanges and Nasdaq to phase in decimal prices from September 2000, ending "no later than April 9, 2001", and they finished on time ([SEC staff, Report to Congress on Decimalization, July 2012](https://www.sec.gov/files/decimalization-072012.pdf)). Asked by Congress to review the result, SEC staff reported in 2012 that "Numerous studies have found that, on average, both quoted and effective spreads declined with the advent of decimalization", that the effect on institutional trading costs "is mixed", that for small and mid-sized companies the effect on spreads "appears to be relatively minor", and that no academic paper directly examined decimalization and the number of initial public offerings. The staff recommended that "The Commission should not proceed with the specific rulemaking to increase tick sizes".

The penny itself became the constraint. In September 2024 the SEC amended Regulation NMS to allow a half-penny increment ($0.005) for quotes and orders priced at $1.00 or more in stocks whose time weighted average quoted spread (TWAQS) is $0.015 or less, and to lower the caps on exchange access fees; the amendments took effect on 9 December 2024 ([SEC, Regulation NMS: Minimum Pricing Increments, Access Fees, and Transparency of Better Priced Orders, 8 October 2024](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-10-08/pdf/2024-21867.pdf)). Compliance was then deferred twice: an order of 31 October 2025 extended relief to the first business day of November 2026, and an order of 11 June 2026 extended it again, for the new tick size and the $0.001 access fee cap, "until the first business day of November 2027" ([SEC, Release 34-105656, 11 June 2026](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/exorders/2026/34-105656.pdf)).

## Regulation NMS (2005): protecting the best displayed price

The SEC voted on Regulation NMS at an open meeting on 6 April 2005, dated the release 9 June 2005, and published it on 29 June; it took effect on 29 August 2005, with compliance phased in later ([SEC, Regulation NMS, 29 June 2005](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2005-06-29/pdf/05-11802.pdf); [SEC, Dissent of Commissioners Glassman and Atkins, 2005](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/final/34-51808-dissent.pdf)). It had four parts:

| Rule | What it does |
|---|---|
| Order Protection Rule (Rule 611) | Trading centres must keep policies "reasonably designed to prevent the execution of trades at prices inferior to protected quotations displayed by other trading centers", with exceptions; to be protected, a quotation must be "immediately and automatically accessible" |
| Access Rule (Rule 610) | Fair and non-discriminatory access to quotations, a cap on access fees of $0.003 a share, and rules against displaying quotes that lock or cross automated quotations |
| Sub-Penny Rule (Rule 612) | No accepting, ranking or displaying orders in increments below a penny, except for prices under $1.00 |
| Market Data Rules | New formulas for consolidating and distributing market data and for sharing its revenue |

### Why the SEC wanted it

The SEC's staff had measured trade-throughs, trades executed at a worse price than one displayed elsewhere. The release reported that "an estimated 1 of every 40 trades in both Nasdaq and NYSE stocks represents a significant trade-through of a displayed quotation", and that for many active Nasdaq stocks about 1 of every 11 shares traded was one ([SEC, 29 June 2005](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2005-06-29/pdf/05-11802.pdf)). For NYSE stocks, the older trade-through provisions of the Intermarket Trading System (ITS) left out block trades and 100-share quotes; removing those gaps took the measured rate from 7.2% to about 2.3% of share volume, which the SEC took to show that the gaps held most of the problem.

Only automated quotes were protected. The release explains that the ITS rules made routers wait for slow manual markets, and that "The Order Protection Rule eliminates this potential inefficiency by protecting only automated quotations" ([SEC, 29 June 2005](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2005-06-29/pdf/05-11802.pdf)). A trading centre could reasonably bypass another that "had repeatedly failed to respond to orders within a one-second time frame". The protection covered only each market's best bid and offer (BBO), the "Market BBO Alternative", not the depth of book behind it. The SEC Historical Society sums up the consequence: the quotes of slower, floor-based markets would not be protected, and faster electronic markets could ignore their prices ([SEC Historical Society, Reg NMS](https://sechistorical.org/exhibition/transformation-regulation-equities-market-structure-1934-to-2018-2/rulemaking-and-linkages-1975-2005/reg-nms/)).

### A worked example

The quotes below are invented. Three trading centres display offers to sell the same stock at the moment a broker receives a customer's order to buy 600 shares at the market.

| Trading centre | Best offer (US$) | Size | Automated? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exchange X | 20.00 | 300 | Yes |
| Exchange Y | 20.01 | 1,000 | Yes |
| Floor market Z | 19.99 | 100 | No, manual |

If the broker sends the whole order to Exchange Y and it fills there at US$20.01, the 300 shares on offer at US$20.00 on Exchange X have been traded through: the customer paid a cent more on those shares than a price displayed and immediately accessible elsewhere. Under the Order Protection Rule, Exchange Y must have procedures to prevent that, typically by routing 300 shares to X first. One of the rule's exceptions is the intermarket sweep order: a limit order marked as such, sent while the broker simultaneously routes other limit orders to take the full displayed size of any better-priced protected quotation ([SEC, 29 June 2005](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2005-06-29/pdf/05-11802.pdf)). The offer at US$19.99 on floor market Z is not protected, because it is not an automated quotation: it can be bypassed. And the rule protects only the top of each book: if X had a further 400 shares offered at US$20.01 behind its best offer, Rule 611 would not protect them, because only each venue's best bid and best offer are protected quotations.

### The dissent

The rule split the Commission three to two ([SEC Historical Society, Reg NMS](https://sechistorical.org/exhibition/transformation-regulation-equities-market-structure-1934-to-2018-2/rulemaking-and-linkages-1975-2005/reg-nms/)). Commissioners Cynthia Glassman and Paul Atkins wrote that <Claim status="attributed" by="by Commissioners Glassman and Atkins">"The majority has failed to establish that current trade-through rates indicate a significant investor protection problem"</Claim>, that "the rule does not actually protect limit orders at each market's BBO", and that "the trade-through rule limits competition among markets" ([SEC, Dissent, 2005](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/final/34-51808-dissent.pdf)). The majority answered the dissent at length in section XII of the release ([SEC, 29 June 2005](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2005-06-29/pdf/05-11802.pdf)).

### What followed

The market that Regulation NMS described changed fast. The NYSE began fully automated access to its displayed quotes in October 2006, and its share of trading in its own listed stocks fell from 79.1% in January 2005 to 25.1% in October 2009 ([SEC, 21 January 2010](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-01-21/html/2010-1045.htm)). In 2010 registered exchanges together executed about 63.8% of share volume in national market system stocks, with no single exchange above 19.4%, and some exchanges published average response times below 1 millisecond. The SEC listed 29 national securities exchanges on a page last updated on 7 January 2026 ([SEC, National Securities Exchanges](https://www.sec.gov/about/divisions-offices/division-trading-markets/national-securities-exchanges)).

Whether the rule caused the race for speed is argued. The SEC Historical Society writes that Reg NMS, by letting markets ignore venues that could not respond within one second, <Claim status="attributed" by="by the SEC Historical Society">"arguably encouraged greater automation and a greater focus on speed"</Claim> ([SEC Historical Society, Consequences of NMS](https://sechistorical.org/exhibition/transformation-regulation-equities-market-structure-1934-to-2018-2/emergence-of-the-national-market-2003-2018/consequences-of-nms/)). The dispute over high-frequency trading has [a part of its own](/learn/high-frequency-trading-what-it-is-and-the-dispute).

## MiFID (2007): Europe opens the field

The European Union's first Markets in Financial Instruments Directive (MiFID), Directive 2004/39/EC of 21 April 2004, recognized "the emergence of a new generation of organised trading systems alongside regulated markets" ([Directive 2004/39/EC, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32004L0039)). It defined a multilateral trading facility (MTF) as a multilateral system, run by an investment firm or a market operator, "which brings together multiple third-party buying and selling interests in financial instruments - in the system and in accordance with non-discretionary rules"; defined a systematic internalizer as a firm that "on an organised, frequent and systematic basis, deals on own account by executing client orders outside a regulated market or an MTF"; and set a "best execution" obligation to execute client orders "on terms that are most favourable to the client". After a postponement, its provisions applied from 1 November 2007 ([Directive 2006/31/EC, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32006L0031)).

The European Commission later summarized the effect: MiFID "abolished the possibility for Member States to require all trading in financial instruments to take place on traditional exchanges and enabled Europewide competition between those exchanges and alternative venues" ([European Commission, MiFID II proposal, COM(2011) 656, 21 October 2011](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-15939-2011-INIT/en/pdf)). After three and a half years, it said, there was more competition between venues, "Overall, transaction costs have decreased and integration has increased", but the benefits "have not always been passed on to the end investors", and "The market fragmentation implied by competition has also made the trading environment more complex, especially in terms of collection of trade data." Among the new venues, Instinet says it launched Chi-X Europe in 2006 and that within five years it became the largest pan-European trading platform ([Instinet, history](https://instinet.com/history)); that is the company's own account.

## MiFID II and MiFIR (2018): rules for algorithms and clocks

The second package, Directive 2014/65/EU (MiFID II) and Regulation (EU) No 600/2014, the Markets in Financial Instruments Regulation (MiFIR), was adopted on 15 May 2014 and applied, after a one-year deferral, from 3 January 2018 ([Directive (EU) 2016/1034, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32016L1034)). Its main market-structure provisions:

- **A third venue type.** An organized trading facility (OTF) is a multilateral system, not a regulated market or an MTF, for bonds, structured finance products, emission allowances or derivatives ([Directive 2014/65/EU, art. 4(1)(23)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32014L0065)).
- **Algorithms defined.** Algorithmic trading is trading where "a computer algorithm automatically determines individual parameters of orders"; a high-frequency algorithmic trading technique adds infrastructure intended to minimize latency, "including at least one of the following facilities for algorithmic order entry: co-location, proximity hosting or high-speed direct electronic access", system-determined orders without human intervention, and "high message intraday rates" (art. 4(1)(39) and (40)).
- **Controls on firms and venues.** A firm engaged in algorithmic trading must have effective systems and risk controls, trading thresholds and limits, and safeguards against erroneous orders and disorderly markets (art. 17); a regulated market must ensure that "its rules on co-location services are transparent, fair and non-discriminatory" (art. 48(8)) and adopt tick-size regimes calibrated to each instrument's liquidity (art. 49).
- **Limits on dark trading.** MiFIR capped trading under two pre-trade transparency waivers at 4% of an instrument's volume on any one venue and 8% across the Union over twelve months, and required shares to trade on a regulated market, MTF or systematic internalizer unless the trades are non-systematic, ad hoc, irregular and infrequent, or are carried out between eligible or professional counterparties and do not contribute to price discovery ([Regulation (EU) No 600/2014, arts. 5 and 23](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32014R0600)).
- **Synchronized clocks.** Article 50 requires all trading venues and their members "synchronise the business clocks they use to record the date and time of any reportable event" ([Directive 2014/65/EU, art. 50](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32014L0065)).

### RTS 25: the clock table

The regulatory technical standard (RTS) for article 50, known as RTS 25, is Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2017/574 ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32017R0574)). Venues and their members must synchronize to Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) as issued by the timing centres listed by the Bureau international des poids et mesures, or to UTC disseminated by satellite provided any offset is removed; they must be able to show "traceability to UTC" and review it at least once a year. The required accuracy depends on speed:

| Who | Activity or system | Maximum divergence from UTC | Timestamp granularity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trading venue | Gateway-to-gateway latency above 1 millisecond | 1 millisecond | 1 millisecond or better |
| Trading venue | Gateway-to-gateway latency of 1 millisecond or less | 100 microseconds | 1 microsecond or better |
| Member or participant | High-frequency algorithmic trading technique | 100 microseconds | 1 microsecond or better |
| Member or participant | Voice trading, request-for-quote with human response, negotiated transactions | 1 second | 1 second or better |
| Member or participant | Any other trading activity | 1 millisecond | 1 millisecond or better |

Gateway-to-gateway latency is measured from the moment a message reaches the venue's outer gateway, through the order submission protocol and the matching engine, until an acknowledgement leaves the gateway ([Delegated Regulation (EU) 2017/574, art. 2](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32017R0574)). How firms meet these numbers, with protocols such as the Precision Time Protocol, is covered in [Colocation, microwaves and the race for latency](/learn/colocation-microwaves-and-the-race-for-latency); why accurate time is hard in the first place is told in [Time: The Dependency Nobody Lists](/learn/ntp-family-history).

### The 2024 review and the consolidated tape

Regulation (EU) 2024/791 of 28 February 2024 amended MiFIR to remove "obstacles to the emergence of consolidated tapes", simplify the trading obligations and prohibit payment for order flow ([Regulation (EU) 2024/791, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R0791)). It replaced the double volume cap with a single Union-wide cap "set at 7 %" applying only to trading under the reference price waiver, and it bars investment firms acting for retail or certain professional clients from receiving any fee, commission or non-monetary benefit for routing their orders to a particular venue. The European Securities and Markets Authority (ESMA) selected Ediphy (fairCT) as the first consolidated tape provider for bonds in July 2025 ([ESMA, 3 July 2025](https://www.esma.europa.eu/node/220717)) and, on 19 December 2025, EuroCTP, a joint venture whose shareholders are 15 European exchange groups, as the first for shares and exchange-traded funds ([ESMA, 19 December 2025](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-12/ESMA74-276584410-11222_ESMA_selects_equity_CTP.pdf)).

## Brazil: one exchange, rules written for several

### The mergers

Brazil moved the other way. B3's regulatory filing records that in 2000 Bovespa led the integration of the eight other Brazilian stock exchanges, "com o objetivo de concentrar toda a negociação de ações do Brasil em uma só bolsa"; that BM&F and Bovespa Holding were integrated into BM&FBOVESPA on 8 May 2008; that the CVM approved BM&FBOVESPA's registration as a market administrator on 19 May 2009; and that the combination with Cetip, operator of organized over-the-counter markets, was consummated on 29 March 2017, creating B3 ([B3, Formulário de Referência 2024, item 1.1](https://api.mziq.com/mzfilemanager/v2/d/5fd7b7d8-54a1-472d-8426-eb896ad8a3c4/8d8a1c1b-991e-904f-e699-a9cb7cc6d9e5?origin=1)). B3's investor-relations history adds that the 2017 combination was approved by the Banco Central do Brasil, the CVM and the Conselho Administrativo de Defesa Econômica (CADE), the competition authority ([B3, Histórico](https://ri.b3.com.br/pt-br/b3/historico/)).

In the same filing B3 states that "Atualmente, a B3 é a única bolsa de valores em operação no Brasil", that the media had reported a new exchange backed by foreign investors, and that there was as yet no information that it had been authorized by the CVM and the Banco Central ([B3, Formulário de Referência 2024, item 1.4](https://api.mziq.com/mzfilemanager/v2/d/5fd7b7d8-54a1-472d-8426-eb896ad8a3c4/8d8a1c1b-991e-904f-e699-a9cb7cc6d9e5?origin=1)).

### The CVM's rules for a market with competitors

The CVM rewrote its market-structure rules in 2022 with competition in mind. Its announcement of 10 June 2022 was headed "Normas adaptam a regulamentação a um eventual ambiente de concorrência entre mercados" (rules adapt regulation to a possible environment of competition between markets) ([CVM, 10 June 2022](https://www.gov.br/cvm/pt-br/assuntos/noticias/2022/cvm-moderniza-regras-sobre-mercados-organizados-e-melhor-execucao-de-ordens-de-clientes)). The pieces:

- **Organized markets.** Resolução CVM 135 of 10 June 2022, in force from 1 September 2022, replaced Instrução CVM 461 of 23 October 2007 ([Resolução CVM 135, consolidated text](https://conteudo.cvm.gov.br/export/sites/cvm/legislacao/resolucoes/anexos/100/resol135consolid.pdf)). It defines an exchange market as one that operates regularly as a centralized, multilateral trading system, or that executes trades against a market maker (formador de mercado) committed to firm quotes (art. 116), and requires price formation with precedence for the best price and then the chronological order of entry (art. 119, § 1º). A 2024 amendment, Resolução CVM 220 of 11 November 2024, requires admission rules to respect equal access, competition and "não discriminação de participantes em razão dos relacionamentos comerciais ou societários mantidos com a entidade administradora" (art. 83).
- **Ownership.** The rule requires prior CVM authorization to acquire 15% or more of the voting capital of a market administrator, a limit the CVM put to public consultation in 2023 with the stated aim of easing restrictions on such acquisitions ([CVM, Edital de Consulta Pública SDM 06/23](https://conteudo.cvm.gov.br/export/sites/cvm/audiencias_publicas/ap_sdm/anexos/2023/sdm0623_Edital.pdf)).
- **Best execution.** Resolução CVM 134 of 10 June 2022 rewrote article 20 of Resolução CVM 35: the intermediary must take "todas as medidas suficientes para obter o melhor resultado possível para o cliente", weighing price, cost, speed, likelihood of execution and settlement, size and nature, and for non-qualified investors the total outlay is the preponderant factor ([Resolução CVM 134, 10 June 2022](https://conteudo.cvm.gov.br/cvm_institucional/export/sites/cvm/legislacao/resolucoes/anexos/100/resol134.pdf)). The CVM said it kept a model "alinhado com o europeu, em que o dever de melhor execução recai sobre o intermediário" ([CVM, 10 June 2022](https://www.gov.br/cvm/pt-br/assuntos/noticias/2022/cvm-moderniza-regras-sobre-mercados-organizados-e-melhor-execucao-de-ordens-de-clientes)).

### Direct market access in the CVM's rules

Resolução CVM 35 of 26 May 2021, in force from 1 July 2021 and replacing Instrução CVM 505 of 2011, governs intermediation ([Resolução CVM 35](https://conteudo.cvm.gov.br/cvm_institucional/export/sites/cvm/legislacao/resolucoes/anexos/001/resol035.pdf)). It lists three ways a client order can reach an intermediary: by telephone or other voice systems, in writing, or "por sistemas eletrônicos de negociação de acesso direto ao mercado", through electronic direct market access (DMA) trading systems, a term the resolution also gives in English (art. 12). An intermediary that receives DMA orders must try to identify their origin and trace their issuer, and must keep "sistema de controle de gerenciamento de riscos pré-operacionais", with operating limits and parameters to catch erroneous orders (art. 16). Foreign intermediaries and portfolio managers may use DMA terminals only if their home regulator has a cooperation agreement with the CVM or has signed the multilateral memorandum of the International Organization of Securities Commissions (IOSCO), and if they are registered at home (art. 17). Each exchange must adopt DMA rules approved by the CVM (art. 18), and DMA trades are supervised by the exchange and by the self-regulator (art. 19).

The exchange's own access models came earlier. In 2009 BM&FBOVESPA announced co-location from 15 June for derivatives and said that, by the end of that year, investors would be offered "three differentiated levels of direct access to the market (DMA)" including co-location ([BM&FBOVESPA, 21 May 2009](https://mondovisione.com/media-and-resources/news/bmandfbovespa-authorizes-derivative-trading-via-colocation-new-direct-market-acc-2011124/); [BM&FBOVESPA, 30 June 2009](https://mondovisione.com/news/trading-in-the-bmandf-segment-at-bmandfbovespa-to-become-fully-electronic-as-of--2011124)). B3's current access models, sponsored access and colocation are covered in [Brazil: BM&F, Bovespa, B3 and electronic access](/learn/brazil-bmf-bovespa-b3-and-electronic-access).

### New entrants, so far

The rules have begun to be tested, though not yet by a second stock exchange. At its meeting of 25 November 2025 the CVM's board considered a request by CERC S.A. to operate an organized over-the-counter market for the registration of securities and of previously executed trades under Resolução CVM 135; the technical staff found the requirements met, two members voted to grant the authorization on conditions, and the interim chairman asked to review the case before a decision ([CVM, Collegiate decisions, 25 November 2025](https://conteudo.cvm.gov.br/decisoes/2025/20251125_R1.html)). The final decision was not found in the sources read. In July 2026 the Rio de Janeiro newspaper Diário do Rio reported that Base Exchange, the planned Rio exchange backed by Mubadala, had a new chief executive and expected to start operating in the second half of 2027 after completing approvals at the Banco Central and the CVM, where it had passed technical tests and awaited a board decision ([Diário do Rio, 10 July 2026](https://diariodorio.com/nova-bolsa-do-rio-muda-de-comando-e-prepara-operacao-para-2027)). A5X, founded by former partners of the broker XP, says it plans a derivatives exchange and credits Resolução CVM 135 with encouraging new entrants ([A5X](https://a5x.com.br/en/?p=1612)). Neither had begun trading in the sources read on 15 November 2026.

## Fragmentation and its critics

The evidence on fragmentation is mixed, and the people who weighed it disagreed.

**The case that competition helped.** Maureen O'Hara and Mao Ye, using US trade-reporting data, found that "more fragmented stocks have lower transactions costs and faster execution speeds", with higher short-term volatility but prices closer to a random walk, and concluded that <Claim status="reported" by="by O'Hara and Ye (2011)">fragmentation "does not appear to harm market quality"</Claim>, consistent with US markets being "a single virtual market with multiple points of entry" ([O'Hara and Ye, Journal of Financial Economics, 2011](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:100:y:2011:i:3:p:459-474)). The European Commission found that transaction costs had decreased after MiFID ([European Commission, 21 October 2011](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-15939-2011-INIT/en/pdf)).

**The case for caution.** An SEC staff review of the literature in 2013 found that visible fragmentation among lit venues generally improved market quality but that "there may be a point at which too much visible fragmentation leads to deteriorating market quality"; that one study found displayed depth in small stocks hurt beyond a threshold; and that most papers on dark fragmentation concluded it "can detract from market quality, both in the form of higher transaction costs" and less efficient price discovery, one finding that informational efficiency deteriorates when sub-block dark trading exceeds 10% of volume ([SEC staff, Equity Market Structure Literature Review, Part I: Market Fragmentation, 7 October 2013](https://www.sec.gov/marketstructure/research/fragmentation-lit-review-100713.pdf)). The review also cautioned that the O'Hara and Ye measure mixed lit and dark volume, which makes it hard to interpret. The Commission noted that the benefits of competition had not flowed equally to all participants and that fragmentation made trade data harder to collect, the problem the 2024 consolidated tape is meant to solve.

**The case against the rule itself.** Glassman and Atkins argued in 2005 that the order protection rule was anticompetitive and unnecessary, and that "the hoped-for benefits of the trade-through rule will not materialize" ([SEC, Dissent, 2005](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/final/34-51808-dissent.pdf)). The SEC's 2010 concept release asked again about fragmentation, dark pools, high-frequency trading and whether protection should extend beyond the top of the book ([SEC, 21 January 2010](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-01-21/html/2010-1045.htm); [SEC Historical Society, Reevaluating the national market system](https://sechistorical.org/exhibition/transformation-regulation-equities-market-structure-1934-to-2018-2/emergence-of-the-national-market-2003-2018/reevaluating-the-national-market-system/)). No later rule repealed Rule 611 in the sources read.

## What to remember

- The rules balance competition among venues against the meeting of orders in one place; the SEC named both extremes in 2005, and fragmentation is the cost on one side.
- The 1996 Order Handling Rules made ECN quotes public; Regulation ATS (effective 21 April 1999) let a trading system choose between exchange registration and broker-dealer rules, with duties at 5% and 20% of volume.
- Decimal prices arrived between September 2000 and April 2001; SEC staff found narrower spreads on average; a half-penny tick for tick-constrained stocks was adopted in 2024 and deferred to November 2027.
- Regulation NMS protects only automated top-of-book quotes; it rested on a staff finding of 1 in 40 trades as significant trade-throughs, and two Commissioners dissented; the NYSE's share of its own stocks fell from 79.1% to 25.1% between 2005 and 2009.
- MiFID (1 November 2007) opened Europe to competing venues; MiFID II (3 January 2018) added OTFs, algorithm controls, volume caps and RTS 25, which sets 100 microseconds from UTC for high-frequency traders; the 2024 review created consolidated tapes.
- Brazil concentrated trading in one exchange (2000, 2008, 2017) and then wrote rules for competition: Resolução CVM 135 (organized markets), 134 (best execution) and 35 (DMA controls); B3 remains the only stock exchange in operation, with entrants still seeking approval.

<ReaderQuestions>

- Should a market protect only the best displayed price on each venue, or the whole book, and who would gain and lose from each choice?
- If fragmentation helps up to a point and harms beyond it, how should a regulator find that point, and should the answer differ for large and small companies?
- The United States protects prices venue by venue, while Europe and Brazil put the best-execution duty on the broker; which model gives an ordinary investor better results, and how could that be measured?
- Brazil merged its exchanges into one and then wrote rules for competition; is a single, vertically integrated exchange a safer design for a market of Brazil's size, or a barrier to lower costs?
- RTS 25 asks high-frequency firms to stay within 100 microseconds of UTC; what does a regulator gain from timestamps that precise, and what does the requirement cost smaller firms?
