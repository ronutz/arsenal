A Capture the Flag (CTF) competition hands its players a set of deliberately vulnerable programs, files and networks, and pays points for proof that a weakness was found and used: a short secret string called a flag. The European Union Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA) defines a CTF as "a competitive computer security event where participants compete in security-themed challenges for the purpose of obtaining the highest score" ([ENISA, CTF Events, May 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf)). This article explains how the competitions are built, scored and played, where they came from, how to start without crossing a legal line, and what employers and researchers have found about their value. The scene as it stands in 2026, its memorable moments and a verified list of Brazilian competitions and teams are in the companion post, **[Flags, finals and the Brazilian list](/blog/flags-finals-and-the-brazilian-list)**.

## What a CTF is

ENISA's report traces the name to the outdoor game in which players capture a physical flag, and to the competition's likeness to early "wargaming" and "king-of-the-hill" security exercises; it notes that a CTF has run at the DEF CON hacker conference since 1996 and that events now take place online and in person throughout the year ([ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf)). CTFtime.org, the community calendar, puts it in one line: "Capture the Flag (CTF) is a special kind of information security competitions" ([CTFtime, What is CTF](https://ctftime.org/ctf-wtf/)).

The flag is the unit of proof. In the description of the International Capture The Flag (iCTF) run at the University of California, Santa Barbara (UCSB), a flag "is just a string of characters", presented to the organizers as proof of compromise ([iCTF, history and background](https://ictf.cs.ucsb.edu/)). ENISA explains why most events standardize the format, often as `[CTF Name]{[Flag Text]}`: players then know when a challenge is solved and the whole process can be checked automatically, without judges ([ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf)). The DEF CON qualifier of May 2026 is a typical case: its rules gave the format as `bbb{...}` unless a challenge said otherwise ([Benevolent Bureau of Birds, DEF CON CTF Quals 2026 Rules](https://bbbirds.org/static/rules/defcon-2026-quals-rules.pdf)).

## The formats

CTFtime names "three common types of CTFs: Jeopardy, Attack-Defence and mixed" ([CTFtime, What is CTF](https://ctftime.org/ctf-wtf/)). DEF CON's finals add a fourth, King of the Hill, and a live head-to-head side contest.

### Jeopardy

In a jeopardy CTF, ENISA explains, players face custom-made challenges, each a standalone problem whose solution yields a flag worth a number of points; challenges are usually independent and can be solved in any order, and when a challenge runs on a remote server, no team can affect that server's performance for the others ([ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf)). The report's assessment of the format has two sides. Because teams never block each other, even the lowest-ranked can keep learning, organizers can cater for every level, and players can drop in and out across time zones; but jeopardy events are "more gamified and less representative of realistic security skillsets", and team members often work alone on separate categories with little interaction ([ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf)).

ENISA lists three variants: the Hack Quest, a guided sequence of challenges for beginners, of which it cites Google CTF's Beginner's Quest; vulnerable virtual machines, still jeopardy because nobody has to defend them; and quiz-style events that ask questions about the challenges ([ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf)). CTFtime adds that in some events "The next task in chain can be opened only after some team solve previous task" ([CTFtime, What is CTF](https://ctftime.org/ctf-wtf/)).

### Attack-defence

In an attack-defence CTF, every team receives a copy of the same vulnerable services. The iCTF describes the classic design: each team must keep its services running and unbroken while trying to compromise the other teams' copies; because all copies are identical, a team that finds a flaw in its own copy can patch it and also use it against everyone else; stolen flags earn attack points, intact services earn defence points ([iCTF, history and background](https://ictf.cs.ucsb.edu/)). ENISA calls it the earliest CTF format, grown from wargame activities in military and hacker communities, and notes that rules usually demand services stay up, so that a team cannot score by simply switching a vulnerable service off ([ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf)).

A player's account of the DEF CON 33 finals in 2025 shows the mechanics: at regular intervals called ticks, the organizers' infrastructure checks the stolen flags submitted and plants new ones; teams monitor incoming traffic and often replay other teams' exploits against everyone else; and because at DEF CON every team's patches are public, some teams have inserted backdoors into their patches to catch opponents who copy them ([SRLabs, Competing at the DEF CON CTF Finals 2025](https://srlabs.de/blog/competing-at-the-def-con-ctf-finals-2025)). ENISA's assessment: the format models real scenarios closely and suits spectators, but it is daunting for newcomers, and when skill levels are unequal a strong team can lock everyone else out early ([ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf)).

### King of the Hill

The Order of the Overflow, which ran DEF CON CTF from 2018 to 2021, described the difference in its 2019 finals notes: in attack-defence "you exploit other teams' services to steal their flags, and protect your own", while "King of the Hill is different: you compete against other teams for the best solution, which depends on the service in question" ([Order of the Overflow, DEF CON CTF 2019 Finals](https://oooverflow.io/dc-ctf-2019-finals/)). Each tick, the teams tied for first place earned 10 points, second 6, third 3, fourth 2 and fifth 1, and the three kinds of points were normalized to make up 40% (attack), 40% (defence) and 20% (King of the Hill) of the total ([Order of the Overflow, 2019](https://oooverflow.io/dc-ctf-2019-finals/)). The 2025 account gives an example of such a round: writing the shortest possible shellcode to read a flag, with more bytes banned in each new round ([SRLabs, 2025](https://srlabs.de/blog/competing-at-the-def-con-ctf-finals-2025)). DEF CON's results page says the 2026 organizers "brought a broad gamut of attack-defense and King of the Hill challenges" ([DEF CON 34 contest results](https://defcon.org/html/defcon-34/dc-34-contest-results.html)).

### Mixed formats, live duels and defence-only games

CTFtime says only that "Mixed competitions may vary possible formats" ([CTFtime, What is CTF](https://ctftime.org/ctf-wtf/)); ENISA describes events combining jeopardy challenges and attack-defence targets as a middle ground ([ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf)). The European Cybersecurity Challenge of October 2026 ran jeopardy and attack-defence rounds and added an offline race against the clock called CTF Unplugged ([ENISA, 16 October 2026](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/news/sweden-sweeps-the-winning-title-of-the-european-cybersecurity-challenge)). DEF CON's finals also hold LiveCTF, introduced at DEF CON 30 in 2022: one-against-one matches between a player from each team, streamed with commentary, on challenges meant to take top players under an hour ([SRLabs, 2025](https://srlabs.de/blog/competing-at-the-def-con-ctf-finals-2025)). At the other end, ENISA records the National Collegiate Cyber Defense Competition in the United States as defence-only, with students as a blue team against a red team of volunteer professionals, and it treats defender-focused wargames as a related but different kind of event ([ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf)).

### How common each format is

ENISA analysed 879 events recorded on CTFtime between 1 January 2015 and 6 December 2020: more than 87% used the jeopardy format, more than 73% ran online, and attack-defence accounted for nearly 25% of in-person events but only about 5% of online ones ([ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf)). A separate count by researchers at Masaryk University, in the Czech Republic, found 969 events on CTFtime from 2012 to 9 October 2020, 684 of them (71%) remote ([Švábenský et al., Computers & Security, 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.01421)). ENISA suggests that the higher cost of setting up attack-defence infrastructure, and the difficulty of scaling it, may explain why it is rare online ([ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf)).

## The categories

Jeopardy challenges are sorted into categories, and the names are stable across events. The table follows ENISA's list, with the additions other organizers document.

| Category | What the player does | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| pwn (binary exploitation) | Attack a remote service, usually a custom binary, for example by overflowing a buffer until it reveals the flag | [ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf) |
| re (reverse engineering) | Extract the flag from a downloaded program by understanding, and sometimes patching, compiled code | [ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf) |
| web | Exploit a web application, with SQL (Structured Query Language) injection and PHP flaws as common themes; shell access is rare | [ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf) |
| crypto | Break encrypted messages or a flawed implementation of a cryptographic protocol | [ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf) |
| forensics | Work out the format of unknown data (corrupted files, odd disk images, captured traffic) and read the flag out of it | [ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf) |
| misc | Everything else, often the gentlest entry point for beginners | [ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf) |
| programming (PPC) | Write code against the clock; ENISA calls it less favoured, the Brazilian community CTF-BR lists it as PPC (Professional Programming and Coding) | [ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf); [CTF-BR, Sobre](https://ctf-br.org/sobre/) |
| physical and hardware | Wiretap Ethernet, intercept Wi-Fi, pick locks; at DEF CON 34, probe a hidden universal asynchronous receiver-transmitter (UART) with a logic analyser | [ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf); [DEF CON 34 results](https://defcon.org/html/defcon-34/dc-34-contest-results.html) |
| radio frequency (RF) | Decode signals: FM (frequency modulation) radio data, fax-teleprinter tones, telephone tones, mesh-network packets; or hunt transmitters | [DEF CON 34 results](https://defcon.org/html/defcon-34/dc-34-contest-results.html) |
| OSINT (open-source intelligence) | Find the answer in public information; at DEF CON 34 one contest made players "OSINT their virtual text based world" | [DEF CON 34 results](https://defcon.org/html/defcon-34/dc-34-contest-results.html) |

ENISA notes that harder events concentrate on the categories closest to real exploitation (pwn, web and re), while beginner events lean on gamified misc challenges ([ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf)). Defender-focused competitions exist too: the Blue Team Village CTF at DEF CON 34 was built around container and malware forensics, cloud attack investigation and incident reconstruction, with 194 teams and 298 problems ([DEF CON 34 results](https://defcon.org/html/defcon-34/dc-34-contest-results.html)).

## Scoring

ENISA lists what can set a jeopardy challenge's value: a base score chosen by the organizer; the time taken, with challenges losing value as the event goes on; a bonus for the first team to solve ("first blood"); and the number of teams that solve it, either as solves happen or at the end, so that "the number of successful solves of a challenge acts as a proxy for the difficulty of the challenge" ([ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf)).

The last method is dynamic scoring. CTFd, a widely used open-source CTF platform, defines a dynamic value challenge as "a challenge whose point value decreases after each solve", which also lowers the score of every team that solved it earlier; it uses a parabolic curve so that high-value challenges lose value slowly at first ([CTFd documentation, Dynamic Value](https://docs.ctfd.io/docs/custom-challenges/dynamic-value/)):

```
value = (((minimum - initial) / (decay ** 2)) * (solve_count ** 2)) + initial
value = math.ceil(value)   # never below minimum
```

With an initial value of 500, a minimum of 100 and a decay of 50 solves, the formula gives 400 points after 25 solves and reaches the floor of 100 at 50 solves. A team that solves a hard challenge early keeps gaining relative to a team that solves an easy one, because the easy one keeps getting cheaper for everybody.

Attack-defence scoring works on time. ENISA describes points awarded at regular intervals for each host a team controls, conditional on its services staying available ([ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf)); CTF-BR's summary of the format ends "SLA conta!" (the service level agreement counts), meaning that a patch which breaks the service costs points ([CTF-BR, Sobre](https://ctf-br.org/sobre/)). The 2019 DEF CON finals were an exception: the organizers wrote that there was no service level agreement or uptime component at all ([Order of the Overflow, 2019](https://oooverflow.io/dc-ctf-2019-finals/)).

Across events, teams are ranked by CTFtime. Its rating "is counted per-year" from each team's place and points in each event, multiplied by an event weight that "depends on tasks and organization level, participated teams or public voting"; since 2017, "only 10 best results count in yearly rating" ([CTFtime, rating formula](https://ctftime.org/rating-formula/)). CTFtime's FAQ shows the weight carries a format factor, "X = 2 for attack-defence events and 1.5 for the rest" ([CTFtime, FAQ](https://ctftime.org/faq/)).

## Teams and infrastructure

ENISA observes that in jeopardy events members often split the categories among themselves ([ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf)). At the top, teams are large: the 2025 account describes 30 or more people on one team, a chat channel for each challenge, a shared note tool built for CTFs, traffic-analysis software built for attack-defence, and scripts that fire every exploit at every team each tick ([SRLabs, 2025](https://srlabs.de/blog/competing-at-the-def-con-ctf-finals-2025)). The 2026 DEF CON qualifier set no limit on team size but required one account per team ([BBB, Quals 2026 Rules](https://bbbirds.org/static/rules/defcon-2026-quals-rules.pdf)). Teams also merge: Carnegie Mellon University's Plaid Parliament of Pwning (PPP) won DEF CON in 2024 as the Maple Mallard Magistrates (MMM), together with the University of British Columbia's Maple Bacon and players from the start-up Theori ([Carnegie Mellon University, 12 August 2024](https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2024/august/carnegie-mellons-hacking-team-wins-third-straight-record-eighth-overall-def-con-capture-the-flag)).

Behind each event is a platform and a network. ENISA reports that major CTFs mostly run custom platforms, probably built on popular open-source ones ([ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf)); the UCSB team released the iCTF framework that runs its competition as open source in 2017 ([iCTF](https://ictf.cs.ucsb.edu/)). For attack-defence, players usually connect through a virtual private network (VPN) that isolates the malicious traffic, and ENISA stresses that they "must still take care to target their exploits onto strictly in-scope targets" ([ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf)). Sponsors and universities carry part of the bill: for the Brazilian Pwn2Win CTF of 2021, Google provided cloud data centres to host the challenges and the Federal University of São Carlos (UFSCar) supplied infrastructure and specific hardware ([Canaltech, 13 May 2021](https://canaltech.com.br/hacker/pwn2win-torneio-de-hacking-abre-portas-do-mercado-e-incentiva-especializacao-184873/)).

## A short history

### DEF CON, from 1996

DEF CON dates its CTF to DEF CON 4 in 1996, building "on the chaos of a wide open network at DC 3" ([DEF CON, CTF Archive](https://defcon.org/html/links/dc-ctf.html)). A history written by a member of the organizing group DDTek says DEF CON 4 was the first time CTF was formalized into a contest, with judges deciding when points were awarded; at DEF CON 5 and 6 players could either supply a target or attack the provided ones, which led to chaos; since DEF CON 10 in 2002 the game has been about custom services, attacking the others' and patching one's own; and a qualification round was later added, "similar to the Olympics" ([DEF CON, CTF History](https://defcon.org/html/links/dc-ctf-history.html)).

DEF CON's archive lists the organizers ([DEF CON, CTF Archive](https://defcon.org/html/links/dc-ctf.html)):

| Years | Organizers |
| --- | --- |
| 1996–2001 | "The People" (the history table lists DEF CON's goons as hosts) |
| 2002–2004 | Ghetto Hackers |
| 2005–2008 | Kenshoto |
| 2009–2012 | DDTek |
| 2013–2017 | Legitimate Business Syndicate |
| 2018–2021 | Order of the Overflow |
| 2022–2025 | Nautilus Institute |
| From 2026 | Benevolent Bureau of Birds |

The pattern is that winners became organizers: the history records that the Ghetto Hackers ran the contest for three years after dominating DEF CON 7 to 9, and that DDTek, a subgroup of the team Sk3wl0fr00t that had won twice, took over for DEF CON 17 in 2009 ([DEF CON, CTF History](https://defcon.org/html/links/dc-ctf-history.html)). The contest has kept growing: Carnegie Mellon reported that the 12 finalists of 2024 qualified from a field of 1,742 teams ([Carnegie Mellon University, 2024](https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2024/august/carnegie-mellons-hacking-team-wins-third-straight-record-eighth-overall-def-con-capture-the-flag)), and DEF CON reported more than 600 teams in the 2026 qualifier and more than 30,000 flags captured in the three-day final, won by Blue Water ([DEF CON 34 results](https://defcon.org/html/defcon-34/dc-34-contest-results.html)).

### The iCTF at UC Santa Barbara

The iCTF grew out of local "live exercises" run by Professor Giovanni Vigna at UCSB in 2001 and 2002; its first wide-area edition, in December 2003, put fourteen United States teams against each other, and in 2004 it became international, with teams from Austria, Germany, Italy and Norway ([iCTF](https://ictf.cs.ucsb.edu/)). For years it was the world's largest educational security competition, and it kept changing shape: a separate network for each team in 2008, attacks on simulated web users in 2009, exploits with risk attached in 2011, and in 2015 a design in which each team had to contribute a vulnerable service; in 2017 it opened to anyone and drew more than 280 teams, and in 2019 almost 400 ([iCTF](https://ictf.cs.ucsb.edu/)). Its organizers drew a framework from the first decade in a 2014 paper for the USENIX Summit on Gaming, Games, and Gamification in Security Education (3GSE) ([Vigna et al., USENIX 3GSE 2014](https://www.usenix.org/conference/3gse14/summit-program/presentation/vigna)). In 2023 the iCTF moved from attack-defence to a week-long challenge-based format with separate high school and undergraduate tracks ([iCTF](https://ictf.cs.ucsb.edu/)); the 2024 edition ran from 16 to 22 November ([ACTION Institute, 22 November 2024](https://action.ucsb.edu/news/2024-ictf-wraps-after-six-days-competition)), and the organizers announced for March 2026 the first edition with agent-based challenges, in which participants upload AI (artificial intelligence) agents that must solve challenges autonomously ([iCTF 2026 slides](https://ictf.cs.ucsb.edu/media/iCTF_2026_Slides.pdf)).

### CTFtime.org

CTFtime is a community-run site that, since its foundation in 2012, has collected past and future events, team rankings and challenge write-ups ([Švábenský et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.01421)). ENISA calls it "a de facto publication platform for CTF events", free for readers and organizers and populated voluntarily by the community, and used it as the dataset for its own analysis ([ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf)). By October 2020 it held more than 969 events, 12,952 challenges and 23,517 write-ups ([Švábenský et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.01421)).

### Google CTF

Google's first CTF ran online from 29 April to 1 May 2016 and was won by the team pasten ([CTFtime, Google CTF 2016](https://ctftime.org/event/303)). Announcing the second edition, Google wrote that the winners had competed against 2,400 teams, 900 of which solved at least one challenge; that the top ten teams of 2017 would play a final on site for a prize pool of over US$31,337; and that it would reward the best write-ups from the qualifying round ([Google Security Blog, 2 June 2017](https://security.googleblog.com/2017/06/announcing-google-capture-flag-2017.html)). The 2025 edition ran from 27 to 29 June, with top teams invited to a later on-site event ([CTFtime, Google CTF 2025](https://ctftime.org/event/2718)).

### picoCTF

Carnegie Mellon announced the first picoCTF for 26 April to 6 May 2013: a story-driven game, free and open nationwide to students in grades 6 to 12, designed by PPP and by Team Osiris from the university's Entertainment Technology Center ([Carnegie Mellon University, 13 March 2013](https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2013/march/march13_computercontest.html)). The university later reported about 6,600 students and teachers in the first year; demand for practice outside the competition produced picoGym, a non-competitive platform, and by 2024 the site had over 600,000 users, 100,000 of them undergraduates ([Carnegie Mellon University, 28 June 2024](https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2024/june/cmus-picoctf-seeks-to-make-cybersecurity-education-more-accessible)).

### The European Cybersecurity Challenge and the International Cybersecurity Challenge

The European Cybersecurity Challenge (ECSC), an initiative of ENISA, brings national teams of ten players aged 14 to 25, chosen through national competitions ([ECSC, home](https://ecsc.eu/); [ECSC, About](https://ecsc.eu/about)). Its first edition was held in Fürstenfeld, Austria, from 3 to 6 November 2014 ([ECSC, 2014](https://ecsc.eu/past-editions/2014)); the twelfth, in Bochum, Germany, in October 2026, gathered 35 European teams and 7 guest teams and was won by Sweden, ahead of Finland and Austria ([ENISA, 16 October 2026](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/news/sweden-sweeps-the-winning-title-of-the-european-cybersecurity-challenge)).

The International Cybersecurity Challenge (ICC) describes itself as "the first global Capture the Flag event" with a focus on younger generations, organized by ENISA with regional organizations representing more than 80 countries ([ICC, home](https://icc.ecsc.eu/)). At the first edition, in Athens in June 2022, Team Europe won, Team Asia was second and Team USA third; Europe scored most in the jeopardy challenges and Asia most in attack-defence ([ENISA, 20 June 2022](https://enisa.europa.eu/news/enisa-news/hats-off-to-team-europe-winners-of-the-1st-international-cybersecurity-challenge)). The ICC has since been held in San Diego (2023), Santiago, Chile (2024), Tokyo (2025) and Brisbane, Australia (May 2026), and Team Europe won every edition ([ICC, 2023](https://icc.ecsc.eu/past-editions/2023); [ICC, 2024](https://icc.ecsc.eu/past-editions/2024); [ICC, 2025](https://icc.ecsc.eu/past-editions/2025); [ENISA, 20 May 2026](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/news/5th-consecutive-victory-for-team-europe-the-still-undefeated-eu-champions-of-the-international-cybersecurity-challenge)). Brazil competes inside the CyberLATAM team, with Bolivia, Chile, Colombia, Costa Rica, El Salvador, Mexico and Peru ([ICC, Teams](https://icc.ecsc.eu/teams)). The 2027 edition is planned for Ireland in October ([ENISA, 20 May 2026](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/news/5th-consecutive-victory-for-team-europe-the-still-undefeated-eu-champions-of-the-international-cybersecurity-challenge)).

### Governments and machines

Governments adopted the format as a talent filter. Executive Order 13870 of 2 May 2019 ordered a plan for an annual President's Cup Cybersecurity Competition for United States federal civilian and military employees, to "identify, challenge, and reward the United States Government's best cybersecurity practitioners and teams" ([Executive Order 13870, Federal Register, 9 May 2019, GovInfo copy](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2019-05-09/html/2019-09750.htm)). The Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) used it to test automation: on 4 August 2016 its Cyber Grand Challenge had seven computer systems play a game of Capture the Flag in Las Vegas, finding and patching flaws without humans ([DARPA, Cyber Grand Challenge](https://www.darpa.mil/research/programs/cyber-grand-challenge)), and the winning system, Mayhem, was invited to that year's DEF CON CTF ([DARPA, 4 August 2016](https://www.darpa.mil/news/2016/mayhem-winner-cyber-grand-challenge)), where the organizers congratulated it as "the first autonomous computer system to play DEF CON CTF" ([Legitimate Business Syndicate, 2016 DEF CON CTF Final Scores](https://blog.legitbs.net/2016/09/2016-def-con-ctf-final-scores.html)).

## How to start

**Find events.** CTFtime is the calendar; ENISA notes that most events listed there set no entry criteria, while a handful of renowned ones use a qualifier ([ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf)). Beginner formats exist on purpose: ENISA cites guided Hack Quests ([ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf)), and picoCTF's picoGym stays open between competitions ([Carnegie Mellon University, 2024](https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2024/june/cmus-picoctf-seeks-to-make-cybersecurity-education-more-accessible)).

**Practise on old challenges.** Organizers publish them: the iCTF points new teams to its 2023 and 2024 challenges on GitHub ([iCTF](https://ictf.cs.ucsb.edu/)), and DEF CON's archive collects files, packet captures and write-ups of past finals ([DEF CON, CTF Archive](https://defcon.org/html/links/dc-ctf.html)). CTF-BR distinguishes CTFs from pentest-style practice platforms such as Hack The Box and TryHackMe ([CTF-BR, Sobre](https://ctf-br.org/sobre/)).

**Read and write write-ups.** A write-up is a step-by-step solution published after an event. CTFtime asks players to post a copy on its site because "We noticed that most CTF teams websites do not live long" ([CTFtime, FAQ](https://ctftime.org/faq/)); DEF CON's archive introduces its collection with "Please read them, learn from them, and share them" ([DEF CON, CTF Archive](https://defcon.org/html/links/dc-ctf.html)). Some events make them mandatory: the 2026 DEF CON qualifier required write-ups within a week to qualify, "primarily written by humans", with any prompts used for a language model disclosed ([BBB, Quals 2026 Rules](https://bbbirds.org/static/rules/defcon-2026-quals-rules.pdf)).

**Join a team.** University groups are the usual door. Ganesh, the security group of the Institute of Mathematical and Computer Sciences of the University of São Paulo (ICMC-USP), admits members through a yearly process of classes and weekly CTF challenges that needs no previous knowledge, followed by a signed term of ethical guidelines ([Ganesh, About](https://ganesh.icmc.usp.br/en/about)). CTF-BR exists to bring Brazilian players together and to take CTFs to universities ([CTF-BR, Sobre](https://ctf-br.org/sobre/)).

**Stay inside the lines.** A CTF's permission covers the targets the organizers provide, and nothing else. The 2026 DEF CON rules say it plainly: "Attacking contest/organizer infrastructure is prohibited", bugs in the infrastructure must be reported, and sharing flags, solutions or ideas with other teams leads to disqualification ([BBB, Quals 2026 Rules](https://bbbirds.org/static/rules/defcon-2026-quals-rules.pdf)). Outside the game, the law applies. In Brazil, article 154-A of the Penal Code, created by Law 12.737 of 30 November 2012, punishes invading another person's computing device to obtain, alter or destroy data without the holder's express or tacit authorization ([Senado Federal, Lei 12.737/2012](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/588113/publicacao/15773241)); Law 14.155 of 27 May 2021 raised the basic penalty to one to four years of imprisonment and a fine ([Senado Federal, Lei 14.155/2021](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/34016289/publicacao/34016562)). In the United States, the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) punishes whoever "intentionally accesses a computer without authorization or exceeds authorized access" and thereby obtains information ([18 U.S.C. § 1030, Legal Information Institute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1030)). Practising a technique learned in a CTF on a system nobody authorized is not a CTF.

**Know the event's AI rules.** Policies on language models now differ by event. The 2026 DEF CON qualifier disallowed "Fully/primarily autonomous teams", let humans use any tools including LLM-based ones (large language models), and offered a self-reported human-only scoreboard ([BBB, Quals 2026 Rules](https://bbbirds.org/static/rules/defcon-2026-quals-rules.pdf)).

## What employers and researchers say

### The employers' and organizers' case

Companies that run or sponsor CTFs explain why. Google gave three reasons for its competition in 2017: the community helps it protect users, as its Vulnerability Reward Program shows; it wants to reach as many people as possible; and it wants to grow the community, adding that "Our internal security team employs several people who actively compete in CTF competitions in their spare time" ([Google Security Blog, 2017](https://security.googleblog.com/2017/06/announcing-google-capture-flag-2017.html)). Trail of Bits wrote in its CTF field guide that "These competitions distill major disciplines of professional computer security work into short, objectively measurable exercises" ([Trail of Bits, CTF Field Guide](https://trailofbits.github.io/ctf/)). Governments use the same argument: at the 2022 ICC, European Commission Vice-President Margaritis Schinas presented the event as a response to the cybersecurity skills shortage ([ENISA, 2022](https://enisa.europa.eu/news/enisa-news/hats-off-to-team-europe-winners-of-the-1st-international-cybersecurity-challenge)).

Survey evidence on employers is thinner. A 2016 report by Intel Security and the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS), covering eight countries, found that only 23% of respondents thought education programmes prepared students for the industry, and suggested that "hands-on training, gaming and technology exercises and hackathons, may be a more effective way to acquire and grow cybersecurity skills" ([Security Magazine, 27 July 2016](https://www.securitymagazine.com/articles/87290-businesses-and-countries-vulnerable-due-to-shortage-of-cybersecurity-talent)). That is a statement of what respondents believe, not a measurement of outcomes. In Brazil, a graduate who played for Ganesh told the press in 2021 that the competitions had helped him get a job, while Professor Paulo Matias of UFSCar said that what is lacking is people prepared for real-world problems, adding "Acreditamos nos CTFs como um dos caminhos" (we believe in CTFs as one of the paths) to train them ([Canaltech, 13 May 2021](https://canaltech.com.br/hacker/pwn2win-torneio-de-hacking-abre-portas-do-mercado-e-incentiva-especializacao-184873/)).

### What peer-reviewed studies found, and their limits

**Flags measure basic skill better than deep understanding.** In a paper for the 2015 USENIX 3GSE summit, Tom Chothia and Chris Novakovic of the University of Birmingham compared the flags students captured in an offline CTF-style virtual machine with the marks for their written answers across three runs of a course. The overall correlation was high (0.92, 0.84 and 0.93), but among students with the top grade it fell to 0.37, 0.22 and 0.68; the authors concluded that capturing flags is an excellent indicator of basic knowledge and technical ability but "does not accurately assess whether students have a deeper understanding of the underlying cybersecurity issues", and that CTF challenges should complement written assessment, not replace it ([Chothia and Novakovic, USENIX 3GSE 2015](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/3gse15/3gse15-chothia.pdf)). Limits: one university, one course design, correlations rather than an experiment.

**In a course, flag sharing is the weak point.** Jan Vykopal, Valdemar Švábenský and Ee-Chien Chang of Masaryk University and the National University of Singapore used jeopardy CTFs as homework in an introductory undergraduate course and reported, at the symposium of the Association for Computing Machinery's Special Interest Group on Computer Science Education (SIGCSE), four design issues: scoring, scaffolding (hints), plagiarism and learning analytics ([Vykopal, Švábenský and Chang, ACM SIGCSE 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.11556)). Only 37 of the 120 enrolled students agreed to take part; the static hints proved of little use for medium and hard challenges; three students confessed to using flags shared by peers; and the authors warned that weeks-long CTFs "are much more vulnerable to flag sharing between students than invigilated intensive CTFs" ([Vykopal et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.11556)). Limits: a small, self-selected sample in one course.

**CTFs teach the technical half of the curriculum.** Švábenský and colleagues at Masaryk University mapped 15,963 write-ups from CTFtime to the curricular guidelines of the Association for Computing Machinery (ACM) and the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE): data security (cryptography, forensics, authentication) accounted for more than 27% of keyword matches, followed by connection and system security, while societal security reached only 3%, and "human aspects, such as social engineering and cybersecurity awareness, are neglected" ([Švábenský et al., Computers & Security, 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.01421)). Limits stated by the authors: they could download only 15,963 of the 23,517 write-ups, only 54% of those contained a keyword, and keyword matching can produce false positives ([Švábenský et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.01421)).

**Organizers know the failure modes.** Kevin Chung and Julian Cohen of New York University's Polytechnic School of Engineering, drawing on the large CTF they organized there, listed difficulties that "counteract these benefits": issues of participation, quality assurance and confusing challenges ([Chung and Cohen, USENIX 3GSE 2014](https://www.usenix.org/conference/3gse14/summit-program/presentation/chung)). ENISA adds that attack-defence has a higher barrier to entry and that jeopardy is less representative of real work ([ENISA, 2021](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20CTF%20Events.pdf)).

**CTFs became a yardstick for machines.** Researchers now use CTF challenges to measure AI. Cybench, presented at the International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR) 2025, packaged 40 professional-level tasks from four competitions; without guidance, the best agents solved tasks that had taken human teams up to 11 minutes, while the hardest task had taken humans 24 hours and 54 minutes ([Zhang et al., Cybench, 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.08926)). How that changes the competitions themselves is the subject of the companion post.

None of these studies tests whether CTF players become better professionals over a career; the evidence is about assessment, curriculum coverage and course design.

## What to remember

- A CTF pays points for flags, strings that prove a challenge was solved; jeopardy offers standalone challenges, attack-defence gives every team the same vulnerable services to break and patch, King of the Hill ranks the best solution round by round.
- Jeopardy dominates: more than 87% of 879 CTFtime events from 2015 to 2020, while attack-defence is mostly an in-person, final-round format.
- Dynamic scoring lowers a challenge's value with each solve; CTFtime ranks teams by their ten best weighted results of the year.
- DEF CON has run a CTF since 1996 under eight organizer groups; the iCTF began at UCSB in 2003; Google CTF in 2016; picoCTF in 2013; the ECSC in 2014; the ICC in 2022.
- Permission ends at the organizers' infrastructure: attacking it is prohibited by the rules, and attacking anything else is a matter for article 154-A in Brazil or the CFAA in the United States.
- Studies find flags track basic skill well and deep understanding poorly, that long CTFs invite flag sharing, and that CTFs neglect the human side of security.
