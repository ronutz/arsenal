Some outbreaks have a villain that nobody ever sees. A town hears that a prowler is spraying gas into bedrooms; a city notices tiny pits in its windshields; men in a district fear that their genitals are being stolen or are shrinking; a capital panics about a creature that leaps from rooftops; villagers say beams of light from the sky leave burns and drain their blood; diplomats and spies report a sound, a pressure in the head and symptoms that do not go away. In each case people were frightened and some were ill, police or the military were called, and the press carried the story. What investigators found differs from case to case, and in one of them, Havana syndrome, the official findings changed more than once and are still disputed in 2026. This part of the series on [mass hysteria and collective belief](/learn/mass-hysteria-how-groups-come-to-believe-anything) sets these cases side by side. After reading, you can say what was found in each, how strong each explanation is, who disputes it, and where nobody steered the panic and where someone may have.

## The pattern: an attacker no one can catch

Researchers have long grouped these episodes. Robert Bartholomew and Robert Baloh gave a chapter of their 2020 book on Havana syndrome the title "Phantom Assailants: Mad Gassers, Phantom Slashers, and Other Believed-in Imaginings" ([Baloh and Bartholomew, Havana Syndrome, Springer, 2020, chapter record](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-40746-9_8)); the sociologist Norman Jacobs studied "The Phantom Slasher of Taipei" as early as 1965 ([Jacobs, Social Problems 12(3), January 1965, record](https://doi.org/10.2307/798937)); only the bibliographic records of these two works could be read for this part. For the koro waves, Bartholomew proposed in 1994 to reclassify them "as a collective misperception rather than a culture-bound syndrome", arguing that they involve "conformity dynamics, perceptual fallibility and the local acceptance of koro-associated folk realities" ([Bartholomew, International Journal of Social Psychiatry 40(1), 1994, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=8005778)).

The cases below share four features in their records: an agent that is never caught or seen clearly, real fear and real symptoms, newspapers and broadcasters that carry each new report, and an end that comes when the authorities, the press or both declare that there was little or nothing to find. They differ in what remains afterwards. In Mattoon and Seattle investigators found no attacker. In the koro waves psychiatrists found anxiety and folk belief. In Delhi doctors found injuries of an accidental kind. In Colares the military's own officers said they saw objects, and the records are incomplete. In the Havana case scientists and intelligence agencies disagreed among themselves for a decade. Respect for the people involved runs through the scholarship: Bartholomew and Baloh, who argue that Havana syndrome was psychogenic, write that the intelligence agencies believed the victims "sincerely and honestly reported their experiences" ([Bartholomew and Baloh, International Journal of Social Psychiatry 70(2), March 2024, full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC10913303/fullTextXML)). Psychogenic illness is illness, not pretence; the part on [mass psychogenic illness](/learn/mass-psychogenic-illness-when-symptoms-spread) sets out how it is diagnosed.

## Mattoon, Illinois, 1944: the gasser nobody caught

### What the town read

On the evening of Saturday 2 September 1944, the Mattoon Daily Journal-Gazette ran the headline "'Anesthetic Prowler' on Loose" and reported that "A prowler who used some kind of anesthetic or gas to knock out his intended victims was on the loose in Mattoon Friday night"; a woman and her three-year-old daughter had been made ill in their home, and the woman said her mouth and throat felt parched and her lips burned ([Mattoon Daily Journal-Gazette, 2 September 1944, front page, Eastern Illinois University copy](https://www.eiu.edu/localite/9-2-44.JPG)). The bibliography compiled by Eastern Illinois University dates this first report 3 September ([Eastern Illinois University, The Mattoon Gasser: bibliography](https://www.eiu.edu/localite/gasserbiblio.php)); the front page itself is dated Saturday 2 September. The university's list of reported attacks, compiled from the newspapers, runs from a case placed "a few months ago" through 31 August and 1 September to 10 September, with a dozen addresses in a single week ([Eastern Illinois University, victims and locations](https://www.eiu.edu/localite/gasservictims.php)).

The press spread the story beyond the town. On 9 September the neighbouring Charleston Daily Courier carried a United Press dispatch headlined "Victims of Gas Prowler Now 25" ([Charleston Daily Courier, 9 September 1944, front page, Eastern Illinois University copy](https://www.eiu.edu/localite/9-9-44.JPG)); the headlines the university collected run from "'Mad Anesthetist' Strikes Again!" to "Mass Hysteria Is Spreading" and "Mattoon Police Expose 'Madman'" ([Eastern Illinois University, newspaper headlines](https://www.eiu.edu/localite/gasserheadlines.php)). The context mattered: a reporter writing in 2015 notes that a United Press story of 30 August had suggested Germany could be preparing poison gas, and quotes Bartholomew on fears of chemical warfare that were "based in reality" for families whose sons had come home from the First World War ([Belt Magazine, 4 June 2015](https://beltmag.com/the-mattoon-mad-gasser-looking-back-at-a-textbook-case-of-mass-hysteria)).

### How the town stopped

By Monday 11 September the same newspaper's front page read "Many Prowler Reports; Few Real" and "City Calmer After Wild Week-End". It reported that "bedlam prevailed in the city both Saturday and Sunday nights when police received dozens of calls from persons claiming they had smelled the mystery gas", and that a physician who examined two women "could find no evidence of a poison gas or other chemical and that in his opinion both suffered from extreme nervous tension". On the same page Commissioner Thomas V. Wright and the chief of police asked residents to stay off the streets, and wrote: "Put away the guns now in the hands of individuals, because some innocent person may get killed" ([Mattoon Daily Journal-Gazette, 11 September 1944, front page, Eastern Illinois University copy](https://www.eiu.edu/localite/9-11-44.JPG)). A United Press story the same day quoted Wright: "There is more than one mad man in Mattoon, there's 15,000 of them. What we've got here is mass hysteria" ([Belt Magazine, 2015](https://beltmag.com/the-mattoon-mad-gasser-looking-back-at-a-textbook-case-of-mass-hysteria)). Armed residents patrolling at night are the point at which a phantom becomes dangerous to real neighbours, the step that the part on [rumour, panic and lynching](/learn/rumour-panic-and-lynching) follows further.

### What the investigators concluded, and who disagrees

Donald M. Johnson, then a psychology student at the University of Illinois according to a 2015 account ([Belt Magazine, 2015](https://beltmag.com/the-mattoon-mad-gasser-looking-back-at-a-textbook-case-of-mass-hysteria)), published a field study in 1945 under the title "The 'phantom anesthetist' of Mattoon: a field study of mass hysteria" ([Johnson, Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology 40(2), April 1945, 175-186, record](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0062339)); the paper itself could not be read for this part. In a 1994 article in Skeptic, Willy Smith summarized Johnson's figures: "there were a grand total of 25 cases in 13 days", "the majority of the victims were women of poor education and modest economic level", and "No attacks were reported in the two high-income areas of Mattoon"; Johnson's conclusion, as Smith quotes it, was that "the hypothesis of hysteria fits all of the evidence, without remainder" ([Willy Smith, Skeptic 3(1), 1994, reprinted in eSkeptic, 15 September 2010](https://old.skeptic.com/eskeptic/10-09-15/)). The 2015 account gives a different total, "nearly 30 police reports" ([Belt Magazine, 2015](https://beltmag.com/the-mattoon-mad-gasser-looking-back-at-a-textbook-case-of-mass-hysteria)); the two counts may measure different things, cases and reports, and the original paper would settle it.

Smith disputed Johnson's verdict. He argued that "the first three cases (August 31 and September 1) definitely were real incidents, each one with two witnesses", favoured "a flesh and blood gasser who was most likely a prankster whose motivations we will never know", and wrote that "If mass hysteria means what I think it means, and if there is such a phenomenon, the case of Mattoon is not a good example" ([Smith, 1994](https://old.skeptic.com/eskeptic/10-09-15/)). A local author has gone further and named a specific local man, long dead and never charged, as the culprit; Bartholomew, interviewed in 2015, replied that "There's absolutely no link that it was an actual person" ([Belt Magazine, 2015](https://beltmag.com/the-mattoon-mad-gasser-looking-back-at-a-textbook-case-of-mass-hysteria)). Bartholomew and Jeffrey Victor reexamined the case in 2004 as a "collective anxiety attack" ([Bartholomew and Victor, The Sociological Quarterly 45(2), 2004, record](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1533-8525.2004.tb00011.x)), and Rita James Simon and James Lumpp revisited the town for Journalism Quarterly in 1967 ([Journalism Quarterly 44(4), December 1967, record](https://doi.org/10.1177/107769906704400417)); only the records of these papers could be read. <Claim status="disputed" by="by Johnson (1945) and Bartholomew, against Smith (1994)">Whether Mattoon was pure mass hysteria or a real prankster magnified by fear</Claim> is not settled by the material available, but no gasser was ever identified by the police.

## Seattle, 1954: the windshields everyone suddenly saw

According to HistoryLink, the encyclopedia of Washington State history, tiny pits were first noticed in car windshields in Bellingham in late March 1954; by 13 April roadblocks were being set up around Anacortes and marines searched the Whidbey Island naval air station, and by the end of that day more than 2,000 cars from Bellingham to Oak Harbor were reported damaged ([Alan J. Stein, HistoryLink essay 5136, 27 January 2003](https://www.historylink.org/File/5136)). On the night of 14 April reports reached Seattle; by 15 April close to 3,000 windshields had been reported, and Mayor Allan Pomeroy wired the governor and President Dwight D. Eisenhower that "What appeared to be a localized outbreak of vandalism ... has now spread throughout the Puget Sound area" and urged that federal agencies "cooperate with local authorities on emergency basis" ([HistoryLink, 2003](https://www.historylink.org/File/5136)).

Explanations multiplied: radioactivity from hydrogen-bomb tests, a new navy radio transmitter, cosmic rays, sand-flea eggs. A University of Washington chemist, D. M. Ritter, said: "Tommyrot! There isn't anything I know of that could be causing any unusual breaks in windshields. These people must be dreaming." A committee of university scientists surveyed 84 cars and found the damage "overly emphasized" and most likely "the result of normal driving conditions in which small objects strike the windshields of cars"; the King County sheriff disagreed after his deputies examined more than 15,000 cars and found more than 3,000 damaged. Seattle police found that older cars were pitted and new ones on the same lots were not, and Sergeant Max Allison of the police crime laboratory declared the reports "5 per cent hoodlum-ism, and 95 per cent public hysteria"; the incidents "abruptly ceased" by 17 April ([HistoryLink, 2003](https://www.historylink.org/File/5136)).

The reports did not stop at the state line. An International News Service dispatch of 19 April counted pitted windshields in at least nine states and Canada, and in Canton, Ohio, some 1,000 residents told police their windshields had been "blemished in a mysterious manner" ([NPR, 28 May 2015](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-history-dept/2015/05/28/410085713/the-windshield-pitting-mystery-of-1954)). The sociologist David Rohall, interviewed by NPR, put the arithmetic plainly: "There are millions of cars in Washington state but thousands of cases of pitting ... it represents less than 1 percent of cars. If everyone is looking for and reporting it, it would appear to be a conspiracy of some sort." The classic study, by Nahum Medalia and Otto Larsen in 1958, is titled "Diffusion and Belief in a Collective Delusion: The Seattle Windshield Pitting Epidemic" ([Medalia and Larsen, American Sociological Review 23(2), April 1958](https://doi.org/10.2307/2089002)). Its first page sets out the chronology: from 23 March 1954 Seattle papers carried intermittent reports of damaged windshields in a city 80 miles to the north; on 14 April in a town about 65 miles away, and that afternoon at a naval air station 45 miles from Seattle; on the evening of 15 April the mayor declared the damage "no longer a police matter" and appealed to the governor and to President Eisenhower; then "On April 16, calls to police dropped from 242 to 46; 10 persons called the police on the 17th, but from the 18th on no more calls were received" ([Medalia and Larsen, 1958, p. 180](https://doi.org/10.2307/2089002)). The same page dates the Mattoon scare to "September 1945", a year late. <Claim status="established" by="by the university committee and the Seattle police in 1954">The pits were ordinary road damage that people noticed for the first time</Claim>, and no vandal or weapon was found.

## The "June bug", 1962: a sourcing note

Alan Kerckhoff and Kurt Back published a book-length study of an outbreak among workers in a textile plant, The June Bug: A Study of Hysterical Contagion (1968), and an earlier paper on its "sociometric patterns" ([Internet Archive, catalogue record of the 1968 book](https://archive.org/details/junebugstudyofhy0000kerc); [Kerckhoff and Back, Sociometry 28(1), March 1965, record](https://doi.org/10.2307/2786081)). Neither text could be read for this part, so the plant, the date and the numbers are not given here. A 2016 review of the nocebo effect cites the case for one finding, that "even if symptoms initially occurred in workers with social stress risk factors, secondary spreading occurred by contiguity in workers without such risk factors"; the same review places the outbreak "in Montana" ([Planès, Villier and Mallaret, Pharmacology Research & Perspectives 4(2), 2016, full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC4804316/fullTextXML)), a location the book's own account has to confirm. The case is told here with its sourcing problem, as an example of a famous outbreak whose details are usually repeated from memory.

## Koro: the fear that something is being taken

### The condition and the epidemics

Koro is a fear, sometimes a conviction, that the genitals are shrinking or retracting and that this may be fatal. A 2024 paper in Cognitive Neuropsychiatry calls it a "socially-transmitted non-neuropsychological delusional belief" that "occurs (in epidemic form) in South-East and South Asia", and concludes that whether the idea is adopted as a belief depends on "individual factors such as prior belief in the Koro concept or limited formal education and sociocultural factors such as deference to culture, to media, or to rumours spread by word of mouth" ([Coltheart and Davies, Cognitive Neuropsychiatry 29(1), 2024, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=38348821)). The historian Ivan Crozier traces how psychiatry built the category from the first Dutch colonial descriptions in the 1890s to the inclusion of culture-bound syndromes in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-IV) in 1994, with the 1967 Singapore epidemic among his cases ([Crozier, Journal of the History of Medicine and Allied Sciences 67(1), 2012, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=21511718)).

### Singapore, 1967

The Singapore epidemic followed rumours about pork. A 2016 account in the Singaporean press, drawing on the Singapore Medical Journal's report of December 1969 and a British Medical Journal letter of March 1968, describes rumours that eating pork from pigs inoculated against swine fever would make the genitals shrink, a fall in pork sales, a government statement that only "caused the people to panic even more", a peak of 70 to 80 cases a week at the Singapore General Hospital, an emergency televised press conference at which the Singapore Medical Association said the causes were "fear and rumours", and a sharp decline within a month, with "over 460 Koro cases that were officially reported in the hospitals" ([Mothership, November 2016](https://mothership.sg/2016/11/sporean-men-panicked-in-1967-over-strange-case-of-shrinking-genitals-known-as-koro)). Indian psychiatrists writing in the 1980s cite the Singaporean psychiatrist P. Ngui's 1969 report of "a series of 228 cases" ([Dutta and others, Indian Journal of Psychiatry 24(4), October 1982, full text](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=3012798)) and describe it as "The largest Koro epidemic reported", lasting "three weeks" ([Chowdhury and others, Indian Journal of Psychiatry 30(1), January 1988, full text](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=3010814)). The Koro Study Team's own paper, "The Koro 'epidemic' in Singapore" ([Singapore Medical Journal 10(4), December 1969, PubMed record](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/esummary.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=5371145,5688940,9364894&retmode=json)), could not be read, so the gap between 228 and "over 460" cannot be resolved here: the two figures may be one series of patients and a hospital total.

### India, China and West Africa

In June 1982 a wireless message from a subdivisional office in Assam reported "Shrinkage of testicles of males and nipples of females affected 8 villages since mid-June"; a team of physicians "opined that there was no disease, only a fear psychosis based on rumour", and psychiatrists at Gauhati Medical College surveyed 83 cases, 19 of them women, mostly young men ([Dutta and others, 1982](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=3012798)). In West Bengal the epidemic spread through the state to the end of December 1982, and its victims came from all communities, "a large majority" from the poorer sections ([Chakraborty and others, Indian Journal of Psychiatry 25(2), April 1983, full text](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=3012338)). A study team counted 405 cases, 357 men and 48 women, in four districts of North Bengal from July 1982 to the end of 1985 ([Chowdhury and others, 1988](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=3010814)). A village case study from Malda shows the mechanism in miniature: "within the span of a few hours five persons were afflicted", in a village "agog with gossip and rumour about the disease" ([Nandi and others, Indian Journal of Psychiatry 26(4), October 1984, full text](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=3011196)).

In Guangdong, China, epidemics "involving more than 2000 victims, mainly young men" occurred on Hainan Island and the Leizhou Peninsula in 1984-1985 and 1987; a survey of 214 victims in 1988 supported the hypotheses that an attack is "a psychologically induced anxiety-panic condition", that belief in koro makes a person vulnerable, and that "social crisis or tension serves as a trigger" ([Tseng and others, Journal of Nervous and Mental Disease 180(2), February 1992, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=1737973); [Tseng and others, American Journal of Psychiatry 145(12), December 1988, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=3264117)). In West Africa a "small-scale epidemic of genital shrinking" occurred in six countries between January 1997 and October 2003; V. A. Dzokoto and G. Adams analysed 56 media reports and asked whether it was "koro, juju, or mass psychogenic illness" ([Dzokoto and Adams, Culture, Medicine and Psychiatry 29(1), March 2005, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=16108203)). Newer Indian outbreaks keep the pattern: 70 patients over five days at a Guwahati hospital, where "Media had a major role to spread this epidemic" ([Kumar and others, Asian Journal of Psychiatry 12, December 2014, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=25150397)), and 19 workers in a jute mill in South Bengal, whose cases all had "a self-limiting course and reasonably good outcome" ([Chakraborty and Sanyal, Industrial Psychiatry Journal 20(1), 2011, full text](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=3425249)).

### Culture-bound syndrome or collective misperception?

The classification is itself disputed. Psychiatrists who treat the epidemics speak of a culture-bound syndrome; Bartholomew argued that this treats a normal social process as pathology, and proposed reclassifying the epidemics as collective misperception ([Bartholomew, 1994](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=8005778)); Coltheart and Davies treat it as a delusion that society transmits ([Coltheart and Davies, 2024](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=38348821)). A 2020 methodological update asks researchers to report, for each epidemic, the "index case, number of cases and their demographic features, mode of transmission of news (mobile phone, radio, newspaper, and TV news)" and the community and professional response ([Chowdhury and Brahma, Indian Journal of Psychiatry 62(1), 2020, full text](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=6964453)), which is also the list of what the older reports often lack.

## The Monkey Man of Delhi, 2001

In May 2001 reports spread through East Delhi of a creature, half man and half monkey, attacking people at night. A Reuters dispatch of 18 May quoted Joint Commissioner of Police Suresh Roy: "It's definitely not one person ... it could be a number of mischief-makers who have formed a small gang"; newspapers said three people had panicked and fallen to their deaths from buildings in a week, police offered a reward of 50,000 rupees, and of 80 calls in one night "most were hoaxes", with only three people having injuries to show ([Deseret News, Reuters dispatch, 18 May 2001](https://www.deseret.com/2001/5/18/19586831/monkey-man-hoax-is-blamed-on-gang)). The Indian Rationalist Association called it a product of mass delusion ([Deseret News, 2001](https://www.deseret.com/2001/5/18/19586831/monkey-man-hoax-is-blamed-on-gang)). By 21 May attacks had fallen; police had taken the help of the Central Forensic Science Laboratory (CFSL), psychologists and doctors; of 17 calls one night nine were "bogus", and eight people were arrested for making fake calls ([The Tribune, Chandigarh, 22 May 2001](https://www.tribuneindia.com/2001/20010522/nation.htm)).

A medical board examined the injured. The study that followed, by S. K. Verma and D. K. Srivastava in the Indian Journal of Medical Sciences (2003), states that "During the summer month of May 2001 East Delhi came in grip of an outbreak of mass hysteria", that "The incidents occurred mainly during night at the time of power failure", and that "The type, distribution and characteristic of the injuries suggested of their accidental nature"; most of those examined were adult men of low socioeconomic status and education ([Verma and Srivastava, Indian Journal of Medical Sciences 57(8), August 2003, abstract record](https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/gim/resource/en/sea-67288)). <Claim status="established" by="by the medical board and the 2003 study">The injuries were accidental, not the work of a creature</Claim>; the deaths came from the panic itself, which is the reason to take such episodes seriously.

## Colares, 1977: the chupa-chupa and Operação Prato

### What the people of the Pará coast reported

In 1977 residents of Colares, an island town about 100 kilometres from Belém, and of other towns on the Pará coast reported lights in the sky that struck people, caused burns, paralysis and pallor, and were believed to suck blood, hence the name "chupa-chupa" (roughly, "the sucker") ([Fernandes and Barbosa, Conexão - Comunicação e Cultura 19(37), 2020](https://sou.ucs.br/etc/revistas/index.php/conexao/article/download/10179/4698/38123)). Phillippe Fernandes and Marialva Barbosa, who analysed the three main newspapers of Belém, describe the fear: fishermen stopped going out before dawn, fields emptied, children were kept indoors, neighbours gathered in single houses with women inside and men keeping watch around fires, and the town hall bought fireworks because noise was believed to drive the lights away; the first news appeared in O Liberal in May 1977, the wave declined in December, and the cases began near the Gurupi River in Maranhão before spreading west ([Fernandes and Barbosa, 2020](https://sou.ucs.br/etc/revistas/index.php/conexao/article/download/10179/4698/38123)). A historian of the Air Force's investigations, quoting the researcher Hélio Aniceto, notes that a fisherman's death off São Luís, Maranhão, in late April 1977 had been linked by the press to the lights, although "Nenhuma das vítimas relatou a imprensa ou a pesquisadores do fenômeno ufológico essa versão" ("None of the victims gave that version to the press or to UFO researchers") ([Schramm, master's dissertation, Universidade de Brasília, 2016](https://repositorio.unb.br/bitstream/10482/21434/1/2016_Jo%c3%a3oFranciscoSchramm.pdf)).

### What the press and the doctors said

The newspapers did not speak with one voice. In 1977 A Província do Pará published five editions on the subject, O Estado do Pará sixteen and O Liberal thirteen in the four months Fernandes and Barbosa examined; O Estado do Pará first treated it in a column in October and then, on 1 November, on its front page ([Fernandes and Barbosa, 2020](https://sou.ucs.br/etc/revistas/index.php/conexao/article/download/10179/4698/38123)). By November the papers turned to doctors and psychologists: A Província do Pará wrote of a population "que está sendo vítima de auto-sugestão e crises de histeria" ("that is falling victim to autosuggestion and fits of hysteria"), and the state security secretary told O Estado do Pará that "O problema é puramente médico" ("The problem is purely medical"), as quoted by the same authors. They also record a physician's view that the population of Belém had been influenced by the coverage itself ([Fernandes and Barbosa, 2020](https://sou.ucs.br/etc/revistas/index.php/conexao/article/download/10179/4698/38123)).

The doctor who ran the health post in Colares, Wellaide Cecim Carvalho, said in a 2005 documentary that she had treated many patients and at first believed they were caught in a kind of collective hysteria, until she herself saw an object over the town; she also said that two patients sent to Belém had died ([Schramm, 2016](https://repositorio.unb.br/bitstream/10482/21434/1/2016_Jo%c3%a3oFranciscoSchramm.pdf)). These accounts were given decades later and could not be checked against medical records for this part.

### Operação Prato and its records

At the request of local authorities, the First Regional Air Command (1º COMAR) in Belém sent a team, and a second team under Captain Uyrangê Hollanda ran what became known as Operação Prato ("Operation Saucer") from September to December 1977, part of whose task was to calm a population that was leaving its villages ([Schramm, 2016](https://repositorio.unb.br/bitstream/10482/21434/1/2016_Jo%c3%a3oFranciscoSchramm.pdf)). Schramm, who examined the material at the Arquivo Nacional (National Archives) in Brasília, writes that it "não contemplam o relatório completo da operação" ("do not include the operation's complete report"): there is no cover and no conclusion, only scattered photocopies, 160 typewritten sheets with maps, drawings, newspaper cuttings and photographs ([Schramm, 2016](https://repositorio.unb.br/bitstream/10482/21434/1/2016_Jo%c3%a3oFranciscoSchramm.pdf)). In an interview with the editors of a ufology magazine in 1997, after he had retired, Hollanda described seeing a large disc-shaped object and said that more than 500 photographs and four films had been sent to Brasília and that he never learned where they went ([Schramm, 2016](https://repositorio.unb.br/bitstream/10482/21434/1/2016_Jo%c3%a3oFranciscoSchramm.pdf)). The journalist Carlos Mendes, who covered the case for O Estado do Pará, has said that Hollanda ordered the paper's editor to hand over about 200 photographs taken by its photographer, which were never recovered ([Fernandes and Barbosa, 2020](https://sou.ucs.br/etc/revistas/index.php/conexao/article/download/10179/4698/38123)).

What the state says it holds has been tested through the Access to Information Law (Lei de Acesso à Informação, Law 12,527 of 2011). In 2012 the Comissão Mista de Reavaliação de Informações (CMRI), the federal appeals commission for access requests, unanimously rejected an appeal for "relatórios, vídeos, imagens e demais documentos referentes à Operação Prato" after the Air Force Command (COMAER) answered that its UFO records "foram encaminhadas ao Arquivo Nacional" ("were sent to the National Archives") ([CMRI, decision on appeal 60502.000774/2012-53, 2012](https://www.gov.br/casacivil/pt-br/assuntos/colegiados/comissao-mista-de-reavaliacao-de-informacoes-cmri/decisoes-de-recurso-de-4a-instancia/deliberacoes-anteriores/2012/decisao-0035-60502-000774-2012-53.pdf)). In 2015 the commission declined another appeal after COMAER declared that all its documents produced between 1952 and 2014 had been sent to the archive; the appellant noted that the archive's 16 cassette tapes concerned witness accounts from Operação Prato ([CMRI, decision 00211/2015, 11 August 2015](https://www.gov.br/casacivil/pt-br/assuntos/colegiados/comissao-mista-de-reavaliacao-de-informacoes-cmri/decisoes-de-recurso-de-4a-instancia/deliberacoes-anteriores/2015/decisao-0212-2015-nup-60502-000921-2015-38.pdf)). In 2013 the Ministry of Defence said UFOs were "disparado" ("by far") the subject most requested under the law in the military area, with 107 requests, and that part of the Army's documents might have been destroyed under a 1977 decree that allowed it ([Agência Brasil, 19 April 2013](http://memoria.ebc.com.br/agenciabrasil/noticia/2013-04-19/ministerio-da-defesa-e-ufologos-abrem-canal-para-troca-de-informacoes-sobre-ovnis)); in 2014 the Air Force's press office said all files on the operation had already gone to the archive, which a Belém newspaper said held 300 pages and 150 photographs ([Diário Online, 1 June 2014](https://dol.com.br/noticias/para/noticia-288018-documentos-ja-foram-revelados-diz-aeronautica.html)).

In September 2022 a federal court in Florianópolis ruled on a citizen's request to force the release of the operation's films. The Arquivo Nacional had told the court that a search for "operação prato" in its database, the Sistema de Informações do Arquivo Nacional (SIAN), "retornam 6 (seis) documentos. Em nenhum deles há documentos de caráter audiovisual" ("returns six documents. None of them is audiovisual"). The judge held that the claim that the films exist "tem lastro especialmente em matérias jornalísticas e relatos de testemunhas" ("rests mainly on press reports and witness accounts"), not sufficient evidence, and dismissed the request; the ruling also records Air Force Order 551/GC3 of 9 August 2010, which limits the Air Force's role on UFOs to recording occurrences and sending them to the archive ([Justiça Federal, 3ª Vara Federal de Florianópolis, case 5002417-48.2022.4.04.7200, judgment signed 30 September 2022](https://www.migalhas.com.br/arquivos/2026/1/A4577DFF00845A_sentenca3.pdf)).

<Claim status="disputed" by="by ufologists, former participants and the Air Force">What the people of Colares saw and what caused their burns</Claim> remains unsettled in the record: the press of 1977 and the doctors it quoted leaned to hysteria and autosuggestion; Hollanda and Carvalho later described objects they said they saw; the official files are fragmentary, and the films they mention were not found by the court. The wider history of Brazil's sightings, including the official night of 19 May 1986, is in the part on [UFO waves and skies seen together](/learn/ufo-waves-and-skies-seen-together).

## Havana syndrome: a contested outbreak

Havana syndrome is the case in this part where the answer is still officially in dispute, where the affected people include intelligence officers who say their government failed them, and where governments accuse one another. It is set out here by date, with each finding attributed.

### The medical studies

The first clinical series, from the University of Pennsylvania, examined 21 people "exposed to directional audible and sensory phenomena" and found "cognitive, vestibular, and oculomotor dysfunction, along with auditory symptoms, sleep abnormalities, and headache", which "raised concern for a novel mechanism of a possible acquired brain injury from a directional exposure of undetermined etiology" ([Swanson and others, Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA) 319(11), 2018, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=5885885)). A 2019 imaging study of 40 personnel against 48 controls found differences in white matter volume and connectivity, while stating that "the clinical relevance of these differences is uncertain" ([Verma and others, JAMA 322(4), 2019, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=6652163)). Bartholomew and Baloh argue that these studies "drew conclusions that were not warranted by the data", noting for example that of the 21 patients "only 3 exhibited white matter changes, which was within the range of normal" ([Bartholomew and Baloh, 2024](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC10913303/fullTextXML)).

In 2020 a committee of the National Academies found "the unusual presentation of acute, directional or location-specific early phase signs, symptoms and observations" to be "consistent with the effects of directed, pulsed radio frequency (RF) energy", and concluded that such energy "appears to be the most plausible mechanism in explaining these cases among those that the committee considered", with persistent postural-perceptual dizziness as a secondary mechanism and "the possible additive effects of psychological conditions"; it found the symptoms "difficult to ascribe to psychological and social factors" but said these "cannot be ruled out as contributing to some of the cases", and that it "cannot rule out other possible mechanisms" ([NASEM, An Assessment of Illness in U.S. Government Employees and Their Families at Overseas Embassies, 2020, summary](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/25889/chapter/2)).

In March 2024 two NIH studies reported the opposite of a lesion. The clinical study compared 86 people with anomalous health incidents (AHIs), the government's term, from "Cuba, Austria, China, and other locations" with 30 controls between June 2018 and July 2022 and found "no significant differences ... in most tests of auditory, vestibular, cognitive, or visual function as well as levels of the blood biomarkers", but significantly more fatigue, depression, post-traumatic stress and imbalance, and "Twenty-four patients (28%) with AHI presented with functional neurological disorders" ([Chan and others, JAMA 331(13), published 18 March 2024, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=10949151)). The imaging study of 81 participants and 48 controls found "no significant differences between participants with AHIs and control participants ... for any MRI modality" after correction for multiple comparisons, and "did not replicate the results of a previously published investigation" ([Pierpaoli and others, JAMA 331(13), published 18 March 2024, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=10949155)). Leighton Chan, the lead author, said at the time that "these symptoms are very real, cause significant disruption in the lives of those affected and can be quite prolonged, disabling and difficult to treat" ([The Register, 4 September 2024](https://www.theregister.com/2024/09/04/us_halts_havana_syndrome)).

The NIH study then ended in controversy. A group of participants alleged irregularities, including pressure to join and cooperation between NIH and the Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) ([Stars and Stripes, republishing the Miami Herald, 29 August 2024](https://stripes.com/theaters/us/2024-08-29/havana-syndrome-study-pressured-coercion-15013679.html)). NIH said its investigation "found that regulatory and NIH policy requirements for informed consent were not met due to coercion, although not on the part of NIH researchers", that it had stopped the study "out of an abundance of caution", and that "these investigative findings do not impact the conclusions of the study"; the CIA said it takes "any claim of coercion, or perceived coercion, extremely seriously" ([The Register, 2024](https://www.theregister.com/2024/09/04/us_halts_havana_syndrome)). At least 334 government employees and relatives, including 15 children, had qualified for treatment in military health facilities, according to the Government Accountability Office as reported at the time ([Stars and Stripes, 2024](https://stripes.com/theaters/us/2024-08-29/havana-syndrome-study-pressured-coercion-15013679.html)).

### The intelligence assessments

On 1 March 2023 the Director of National Intelligence, Avril Haines, said that most agencies judged it "very unlikely" that a foreign adversary was responsible, that critical assumptions about the first Cuban cases were not supported by later analysis, and that symptoms were probably due to preexisting conditions, conventional illnesses and environmental factors, while stressing that the findings did not dismiss personnel's experiences ([ODNI statement, 1 March 2023, GlobalSecurity.org copy](https://www.globalsecurity.org/intell/library/news/2023/intell-230301-dni01.htm); the 2024 update restates the 2023 judgment as "very unlikely": [National Intelligence Council, 10 January 2025, p. 1](https://archive.dni.gov/files/ODNI/documents/assessments/NIC-Unclassified-ICA-Updated-Assessment-AHI-December2024.pdf)). The assessment, as Bartholomew and Baloh quote it, said that personnel "sincerely and honestly reported their experiences, including those that were painful or traumatic", and pointed to the framing of symptoms "as possible attacks by an unknown mechanism that could cause permanent harm such as brain damage" ([Bartholomew and Baloh, 2024](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC10913303/fullTextXML)). Cuba's deputy foreign minister, Carlos Fernández de Cossío, said the conclusion "confirms what we already knew" and accused Washington of using the issue "to derail bilateral relations ... and discredit Cuba" ([Reuters, via CTV News, 3 March 2023](https://guelph.ctvnews.ca/health/article/cuba-blasts-us-for-years-of-disregarding-evidence-on-havana-syndrome)); in a later statement Cuba's foreign ministry listed the "allegedly acoustic attacks" among the slanders it said US officials had fabricated ([Cuban Ministry of Foreign Affairs, statement, June 2023](https://cubaminrex.cu/en/statement-deputy-foreign-minister-republic-cuba-carlos-fernandez-de-cossio)).

The updated assessment released on 10 January 2025 kept the main judgment: five agencies "very unlikely", one "unlikely", one abstaining. Two agencies shifted on capability, one now judging it "likely" that a foreign actor has a radio-frequency capability that could cause some reported effects, the other a "roughly even chance", both citing reporting that foreign "directed-energy research programs have been making progress"; both still judged it "unlikely" that such a weapon was used in any reported event, and the assessment repeated, citing the NIH research, that personnel "do not have a consistent set of physical injuries" ([National Intelligence Council, Updated Assessment of Anomalous Health Incidents, as of December 2024, released 10 January 2025](https://archive.dni.gov/files/ODNI/documents/assessments/NIC-Unclassified-ICA-Updated-Assessment-AHI-December2024.pdf)). The Washington Post later reported that the two agencies were the National Security Agency and the Army's National Ground Intelligence Center ([Stars and Stripes, republishing The Washington Post, 14 February 2026](https://www.stripes.com/theaters/europe/2026-02-14/pentagon-havana-syndrome-experiment-norway-20740362.html)).

### The case for an attack

Those who believe in an attack point to three things. First, a 2024 investigation by The Insider, Der Spiegel and CBS's 60 Minutes alleged that members of the Russian military intelligence (GRU) Unit 29155 were placed at the scene of reported incidents and that senior members received awards for work on "non-lethal acoustic weapons", and reported possible attacks in Frankfurt in 2014 ([The Insider, 31 March 2024](https://theins.ru/en/politics/270425)). The Kremlin's spokesman, Dmitry Peskov, replied: "This is all nothing more than a groundless, unfounded accusation by the media ... Not once has anyone published or announced the slightest bit of convincing evidence for these unfounded allegations" ([The Irish Times, 1 April 2024](https://www.irishtimes.com/world/middle-east/2024/04/01/kremlin-denies-its-spies-are-behind-us-havana-syndrome-cases)). Second, devices: CNN reported in January 2026 that the Defense Department had spent more than a year testing a device that "produces pulsed radio waves", contains Russian components and was bought by Homeland Security Investigations for "eight figures" in an undercover operation ([CNN, via CTV News, 14 January 2026](https://www.ctvnews.ca/world/article/pentagon-bought-device-through-undercover-operation-some-investigators-suspect-is-linked-to-havana-syndrome/)), and The Washington Post that a Norwegian government scientist who built a pulsed microwave machine "to prove such devices are harmless to humans" tested it on himself in 2024 and suffered neurological symptoms similar to those of Havana syndrome, though one source said the effects differed from those of a classic case ([Stars and Stripes, 2026](https://www.stripes.com/theaters/europe/2026-02-14/pentagon-havana-syndrome-experiment-norway-20740362.html)). Third, the affected officers themselves: Marc Polymeropoulos, a retired senior CIA officer treated for a line-of-duty traumatic brain injury after an incident in Moscow in 2017, wrote in March 2026 that "a foreign adversary appears likely responsible" and that agency leaders had privately accused victims "of fabricating our conditions for financial gain" ([War on the Rocks, 10 March 2026](https://warontherocks.com/2026/03/we-were-right-about-havana-syndrome/)).

### The case against

Those who see no attack point to the NIH studies' negative findings, to the absence of any weapon shown to cause the full pattern, and to the history of the other cases in this part. Bartholomew and Baloh list what they see as the errors: conclusions beyond the data, media leaks of preliminary findings, "the prevalence of popular misconceptions about psychogenic illness", "the inability to identify historical parallels" and "the mixing of politics with science" ([Bartholomew and Baloh, 2024](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC10913303/fullTextXML)). They note that in 2021 the head of a government panel was "forced to resign after refusing to rule out MPI as a possible cause", that a senator asked a State Department physician in 2018 whether this was "a bunch of people ... just being hypochondriacs and making it up?", and that such remarks treat mass psychogenic illness (MPI) as faking, which it is not ([Bartholomew and Baloh, 2024](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC10913303/fullTextXML)). In intercepted conversations, US officials have said, adversaries were heard expressing surprise at the incidents and denying involvement ([Stars and Stripes, 2026](https://www.stripes.com/theaters/europe/2026-02-14/pentagon-havana-syndrome-experiment-norway-20740362.html)).

### 2026: the recall and the payments

On 11 June 2026 the chairman of the House Intelligence Committee, Rick Crawford, welcomed DNI Tulsi Gabbard's recall of "the two flawed Intelligence Community Assessments (ICAs)", calling them "flawed, fraudulent, and manufactured" ([House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence, 11 June 2026](https://intelligence.house.gov/2026/06/11/chairman-crawford-statement-on-director-gabbards-recall-of-the-flawed-intelligence-community-assessments-on-ahi/)). According to CNN, an ODNI memo to the whole intelligence community said the assessments had selectively excluded relevant intelligence, suppressed alternative analysis and relied on an ethically flawed medical study, and had "limited intelligence collection to maintain an analytic line which relied on absence of evidence"; CNN noted that Gabbard was expected to leave office that month and that it was unclear whether a new investigation would follow ([CNN, via CTV News, 12 June 2026](https://www.ctvnews.ca/world/article/gabbard-rescinds-biden-era-intel-assessments-that-were-skeptical-about-havana-syndrome/)). The Washington Times reported that an ODNI official described the two reports as "Biden-era Intelligence Community Assessments" that were rescinded "because of faulty tradecraft" ([The Washington Times, 12 June 2026](https://m.washingtontimes.com/news/2026/jun/12/dni-rebukes-faulty-intelligence-analyses-havana-syndrome/)). The recall withdrew the earlier judgments; no new assessment had been published by the time this part was written. On 10 July 2026 the Defense Department said it had "disbursed nearly $3 million in compensation, representing the first HAVANA Act payments made under any presidential administration", and renamed its anomalous health incidents team the Directed Energy Bio-Effects cross-functional team ([DefenseScoop, 13 July 2026](https://defensescoop.com/2026/07/13/havana-syndrome-payments-directed-energy-bio-effects/)); the HAVANA Act itself, signed on 8 October 2021, provides payments for "a qualifying injury to the brain" ([Public Law 117-46, 8 October 2021](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-117publ46/pdf/PLAW-117publ46.pdf)).

<Claim status="disputed" by="by intelligence agencies, the NIH, the National Academies, Congress and the affected officers">Whether any Havana syndrome case was caused by a foreign device</Claim> is open in 2026. <Claim status="established" by="by every official body cited here">That the affected people experienced real symptoms</Claim> is not in dispute; what is disputed is their cause, and whether the institutions that judged it treated the people fairly.

## Who steered, and who did not

In most of these cases no one is known to have steered the fear. The Mattoon press built the story and then took it apart; Seattle's scientists and police ended theirs with an explanation that was humble and correct; the koro epidemics ran on rumour and ended with reassurance from doctors and, in Singapore, from a medical association on television. That pattern, a claim repeated until it feels true and then corrected, is the subject of [the human attack surface](/learn/the-human-attack-surface-why-repetition-works) and [why misbeliefs persist](/learn/why-misbeliefs-persist); the role of reach is in [from the lodge to the network](/learn/from-the-lodge-to-the-network-how-reach-changed).

Two cases add a state. In Colares the Air Force investigated under a military government, and, according to the journalist quoted above, seized a newspaper's photographs; what it concluded is not in the partial files. In the Havana case governments accuse one another: the US intelligence community has itself changed its judgments, Russia denies involvement, and Cuba says the affair was used against it. Where intelligence services, foreign ministries and investigative media make competing claims, the tools in [verification and fact-checking](/learn/verification-and-fact-checking) apply, and the techniques that states use to shape belief are mapped in [the psychological operations series](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign). The mechanisms of contagion that run through every case are in [conformity, cascades and contagion](/learn/conformity-cascades-and-contagion-the-mechanisms).

## What to remember

- Phantom-attacker panics share an invisible agent, real fear and symptoms, heavy coverage and an investigation that finds less than was feared; they differ in what remains afterwards.
- Mattoon (1944): about 25 to 30 reports in two weeks, no gasser found; Johnson called it hysteria, Smith a real prankster. Read the dates on the front pages: the first report is of 2 September.
- Seattle (1954): more than 3,000 pitted windshields were old road damage seen for the first time; "5 per cent hoodlum-ism, and 95 per cent public hysteria", in the police chemist's words.
- Koro epidemics (Singapore 1967, India 1982, Guangdong 1984 to 1987, West Africa 1997 to 2003) follow rumour, fear and folk belief, and end with reassurance; whether they are a syndrome or a collective misperception is disputed.
- Delhi (2001): a medical board found accidental injuries; three people died in falls during the panic.
- Colares (1977): the press and doctors of the day leaned to hysteria; the operation's chief and a local doctor later described objects; the official files are partial, and a court found no record of the films.
- Havana syndrome: clinical studies disagree, intelligence agencies assessed and then recalled their judgments, compensation is being paid, and the cause is still disputed.
