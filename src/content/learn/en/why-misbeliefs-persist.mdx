The earlier part on [the human attack surface](/learn/the-human-attack-surface-why-repetition-works) set out three findings: repetition makes a claim feel truer, a correction reduces but does not erase what it corrects, and the backfire effect did not hold up as a general rule. This part of the series opened by [the map from the small lie to the campaign](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign) asks the next question, which is why. Psychologists and political scientists offer several answers that compete with one another: memory keeps a corrected story because the correction leaves a hole; repetition makes the false version familiar; people do not stop to think; people think hard, but in the service of their group. Each answer has its studies, its replications and its critics, and most of them were measured in the United States (US). After reading, you can tell the mechanisms apart, place each finding on the scale from replicated to disputed, and set the Brazilian evidence beside the American.

## Three kinds of persistence

The word *persist* covers different things, and the literature keeps them apart. In a 2020 review, Briony Swire-Thompson, Joseph DeGutis and David Lazer define the continued influence effect as the case "where individuals still use inaccurate information in their reasoning and memory after a credible correction has been presented"; belief regression as the case "where individuals initially update their belief after being exposed to the correction, but this belief change is not sustained over time"; and the backfire effect as the narrower case where "a correction inadvertently increases misinformation belief relative to a precorrection or no-correction baseline" ([Swire-Thompson et al., Journal of Applied Research in Memory and Cognition, September 2020, full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7462781/fullTextXML)). In the first two, they note, "people at least still update their beliefs in the intended direction promoted by the correction".

A fourth kind sits outside belief altogether. The Debunking Handbook 2020, written by 22 researchers, warns that "updates to factual beliefs, even if successful, may not translate into attitude or behaviour change", citing studies in which people in polarized societies "indicate that they will continue to vote for their favored politician even if they discover that the majority of the politician's statements are false" ([Lewandowsky, Cook, Ecker and others, The Debunking Handbook 2020, October 2020](https://sciencelearning.umd.edu/files/2020/10/DebunkingHandbook2020.pdf)). A correction can win the factual point and lose everything that depended on it.

## Continued influence: the warehouse fire

### How it is measured

The standard experiment is a story. Saoirse Connor Desai and Stian Reimers describe it in a 2023 paper: participants read "a report of an unfolding event (e.g., a warehouse fire) that includes causal information", for example that the fire was caused by carelessly stored flammable liquids; that cause is then corrected, left uncorrected or never mentioned, and later questions such as "what could have caused the explosions?" show that the corrected cause keeps shaping reasoning "despite memory for event-related details remaining intact" ([Connor Desai and Reimers, Memory & Cognition 51(2), 2023, repository copy](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/28822/1/ConnorDesai%26Reimers2022.pdf)). They trace the paradigm to Wilkes and Leatherbarrow (1988) and to Hollyn Johnson and Colleen Seifert (1994), whose paper gave the effect its name; only secondary descriptions of those two papers could be read for this part. The typical result, in their words: a correction "often fails to reduce reliance on misinformation to the level of a no misinformation baseline". <Claim status="established" by="by repeated experiments and a meta-analysis cited in the earlier part">A correction reduces reliance on a false cause without bringing it back to the level of people who never heard it</Claim>; the earlier part gives the meta-analytic numbers.

The 2012 review by Stephan Lewandowsky, Ullrich Ecker, Colleen Seifert, Norbert Schwarz and John Cook set out to explain "why retractions of misinformation are so ineffective in memory updating" and noted that misinformation can come from "rumors but also from works of fiction, governments and politicians, and vested interests" ([Lewandowsky et al., Psychological Science in the Public Interest 13(3), December 2012, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=26173286)). Only its abstract could be read for this part.

### Two explanations from memory research

Researchers give two accounts, which Connor Desai and Reimers summarize side by side ([Connor Desai and Reimers, 2023](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/28822/1/ConnorDesai%26Reimers2022.pdf)):

| Account | What goes wrong | What it predicts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Selective retrieval | "the misinformation is successfully retrieved but the correction is not" | the false cause can come back without its correction |
| Model updating | a "failure to integrate the updated information into a mental model of the described event" | the specific fact can be corrected while the mental model stays as it was, "unless an alternative explanation is available" |

Ecker, Lewandowsky and Matthew Chadwick add a detail to the retrieval account: the false claim may come back automatically through familiarity, and "misinformation is typically repeated within a correction, boosting its familiarity"; their example is that "clarifying that vaccines do not cause autism all but requires repetition of the false vaccine-autism association" ([Ecker, Lewandowsky and Chadwick, Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications 5, 26 August 2020, full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7447737/fullTextXML)).

### What helps, and what did not

The model-updating account is behind the most cited practical advice. The Debunking Handbook says "Do not rely on a simple retraction" and calls a factual alternative, one that fills the causal gap left when the misinformation is corrected, "an effective method of debunking" ([Debunking Handbook 2020](https://sciencelearning.umd.edu/files/2020/10/DebunkingHandbook2020.pdf)). Its recommended order is fact, a warning that a myth is coming ("mention it once only"), an explanation of how the myth misleads, then the fact again, which should provide "an alternative causal explanation".

Not every intuitive fix worked. Connor Desai and Reimers tested whether a correction works better when it says why the false report appeared, as a deliberate lie or an honest error; they found "no evidence that corrections explaining the reason the misinformation was presented, were more effective than a correction not accompanied by an explanation" and concluded that explaining the cause "may not be an effective correction strategy over and above stating that the misinformation is false" ([Connor Desai and Reimers, 2023](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/28822/1/ConnorDesai%26Reimers2022.pdf)).

The familiarity account raised a fear that a correction which repeats a myth spreads it. Ecker, Lewandowsky and Chadwick tested the hardest case, correcting claims the audience had never heard, in three experiments with 1,718 participants and a one-week delay: "Corrections that exposed participants to novel misinformation did not lead to stronger misconceptions compared to a control group never exposed to the false claims or corrections", which, they write, "suggests that it is safe to repeat misinformation when correcting it" ([Ecker et al., 2020](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7447737/fullTextXML)). The handbook, written afterwards with Ecker among its authors, still advises repeating the myth once, directly before the correction.

## Repetition, and where it stops

Familiarity also explains why a false claim heard often is harder to dislodge. The earlier part reported the illusory truth effect and its decline over a month; the studies below test its limits.

**A single exposure is enough to matter.** Gordon Pennycook, Tyrone Cannon and David Rand showed participants real fake-news headlines "as they were seen on Facebook" and found that "even a single exposure increases subsequent perceptions of accuracy, both within the same session and after a week", even "when the stories are labeled as contested by fact checkers or are inconsistent with the reader's political ideology" ([Pennycook, Cannon and Rand, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General 147(12), December 2018, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=30247057)). They also found a boundary: prior exposure "does not impact entirely implausible statements", such as the claim that the earth is a perfect square, though "only a small degree of potential plausibility is sufficient for repetition to increase perceived accuracy".

**Implausibility is a weaker shield than it looks.** Lisa Fazio, Rand and Pennycook then argued that the apparent immunity of implausible claims is partly an artefact of the rating scale: in a simulation and a preregistered experiment, "belief in all statements is increased by repetition", and the larger effect for ambiguous items "can be explained by the psychometric properties of the task"; they conclude that "even highly implausible statements will become more plausible with enough repetition" ([Fazio, Rand and Pennycook, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review 26(5), October 2019, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=31420808)). The two papers share two authors and differ on where the boundary lies.

**The return on repetition diminishes.** Aumyo Hassan and Sarah Barber showed trivia statements up to 9 and then up to 27 times; perceived truth rose with repetitions, but "these truth rating increases were logarithmic in shape. The largest increase in perceived truth came from encountering a statement for the second time" ([Hassan and Barber, Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications 6, 13 May 2021, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=33983553)).

**Strong prior beliefs are an open question.** Emma Henderson, Daniel Simons and Dale Barr, who measured the effect in 567 adults over a month, caution that "it is not clear that this result will generalise to topics on which participants hold strong prior beliefs" ([Henderson, Simons and Barr, Journal of Cognition, 2021](https://journalofcognition.org/articles/10.5334/joc.161)). Most of these studies used trivia or headlines, not the identity-laden claims taken up below.

## Backfire: what is left of it

The backfire story is told in [the earlier part](/learn/the-human-attack-surface-why-repetition-works); what matters here is why it attracted so much attention and what replaced it. Swire-Thompson and her co-authors separate a worldview backfire effect, "said to occur when a person is motivated to defend their worldview because a correction challenges a person's belief system", from a familiarity backfire effect, "presumed to occur when misinformation is repeated within the retraction", and report that "Failures to find or replicate both backfire effects have been widespread" ([Swire-Thompson et al., 2020](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7462781/fullTextXML)).

They add a measurement explanation for the reports that remain. Because "81% of backfire effects found in our review of the worldview and familiarity literatures are found with single item measures", poor reliability could be at work, and unreliable measures are "more susceptible to regression to the mean", which "can give rise to spurious backfire effects in individuals and subgroups". They also note that worldview backfire effects "have almost exclusively been found in either political or attitudinal subgroups, rather than communities as a whole", and that the media had "over-generalized backfire effects found in subgroups to the population as a whole". The handbook's verdict: "concern about worldview backfire has been disproportionate" ([Debunking Handbook 2020](https://sciencelearning.umd.edu/files/2020/10/DebunkingHandbook2020.pdf)). <Claim status="disputed" by="by later replications and reviews">A general backfire effect</Claim> is no longer the leading explanation, but reports under specific conditions are not closed.

### Decay and elite cues instead

Brendan Nyhan, co-author of the 2010 study that described backfire, now places the durability of false beliefs elsewhere. Corrections "often do not last or accumulate; instead, they frequently seem to decay or be overwhelmed by cues from elites and the media promoting more congenial but less accurate claims. As a result, misperceptions typically persist in public opinion for years after they have been debunked" ([Nyhan, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS) 118(15), 13 April 2021, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=33837144)). His remedy is structural: "the best approach is to disrupt the formation of linkages between group identities and false claims and to reduce the flow of cues reinforcing those claims from elites and the media".

The decay has been measured. Preregistered experiments in the US, Great Britain and Canada by John Carey and co-authors found that fact-checks on coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) "reduce targeted misperceptions, especially among the groups who are most vulnerable to these claims", but that "these reductions in COVID-19 misperception beliefs do not persist over time in panel data even after repeated exposure" ([Carey et al., Nature Human Behaviour 6, February 2022, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=35115678)). Other experiments found longer effects: Ethan Porter and Thomas Wood's simultaneous studies in Argentina, Nigeria, South Africa and the United Kingdom (UK) report "most effects still apparent more than 2 wk later" ([Porter and Wood, PNAS 118(37), 14 September 2021, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=34507996)). <Claim status="disputed">How long a correction lasts</Claim> depends on the topic, the country and the follow-up, and the panel evidence and the two-week evidence measure different spans.

## Lazy or biased? The inattention account

### The claim

If corrections work in the moment and fade, perhaps people were never reasoning about the claim at all. Pennycook and Rand put this account against the motivated-reasoning account in two studies of 3,446 Mechanical Turk workers, using the Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT) "as a measure of the propensity to engage in analytical reasoning" ([Pennycook and Rand, Cognition 188, July 2019, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=29935897)). CRT performance was "negatively correlated with the perceived accuracy of fake news, and positively correlated with the ability to discern fake news from real news - even for headlines that align with individuals' political ideology", and "overall discernment was actually better for ideologically aligned headlines than for misaligned headlines". Their conclusion, in the title and the abstract: <Claim status="disputed" by="by the authors (peer-reviewed), contested by later work">"susceptibility to fake news is driven more by lazy thinking than it is by partisan bias per se"</Claim>.

A 2021 Nature paper by Pennycook, Ziv Epstein, Mohsen Mosleh, Antonio Arechar, Dean Eckles and Rand extended the idea to sharing. It found that "the veracity of headlines has little effect on sharing intentions, despite having a large effect on judgments of accuracy", while "most participants say it is important to share only accurate news"; four survey experiments and a field experiment on Twitter showed that "subtly shifting attention to accuracy increases the quality of news that people subsequently share" ([Pennycook et al., Nature 592, April 2021, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=33731933)). In their synthesis, "poor truth discernment is associated with lack of careful reasoning and relevant knowledge, and the use of heuristics such as familiarity", and the gap between belief and sharing "is largely driven by inattention, more so than by purposeful sharing of misinformation" ([Pennycook and Rand, Trends in Cognitive Sciences 25(5), May 2021, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=33736957)).

### The replications

The intervention that follows, an accuracy prompt, was tested repeatedly. Pennycook and Rand's internal meta-analysis of 20 experiments run by their group from 2017 to 2020 (N = 26,863) found that prompts improved the quality of what people said they would share "primarily by reducing sharing intentions for false headlines by 10% relative to control", with larger effects "for older, more reflective, and more attentive participants"; the authors disclose research funding from Google and, for Rand, from Meta ([Pennycook and Rand, Nature Communications 13, 28 April 2022, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=35484277)).

An independent preregistered replication of one of the group's COVID-19 studies, run by Jon Roozenbeek, Alexandra Freeman and Sander van der Linden as part of the Systematizing Confidence in Open Research and Evidence (SCORE) programme, was less encouraging: the first stage "(n = 701) was unsuccessful (p = .67)"; with a second round (pooled N = 1,583) they found "a small but significant interaction" (treatment d = 0.14, control d = 0.10), and they discuss "an unreported change in the hypothesis (but not the analysis plan) from the preregistration in the original study" ([Roozenbeek, Freeman and van der Linden, Psychological Science 32(7), July 2021, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=34114521)). <Claim status="disputed">The size of the accuracy-prompt effect</Claim> is small in both accounts; whether it is reliable outside the original group's studies is where they differ.

### The critics

The motivated-reasoning side answered in the same journal: Bertram Gawronski published "Partisan bias in the identification of fake news", and Pennycook and Rand replied with "Lack of partisan bias in the identification of fake (versus real) news" ([Gawronski, Trends in Cognitive Sciences 25(9), September 2021](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=34226126); [Pennycook and Rand, September 2021](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=34226127)); only the records of these two short comments could be read, so their arguments are not summarized here.

An experimental test of motivation came from Steve Rathje, Roozenbeek, Jay Van Bavel and van der Linden. Across four experiments (n = 3,364) they paid US participants for correct judgements of true and false political headlines: "Financial incentives improved accuracy and reduced partisan bias in judgements of headlines by about 30%, primarily by increasing the perceived accuracy of true news from the opposing party", while paying people to identify news their political allies would like "decreased accuracy" ([Rathje et al., Nature Human Behaviour 7, published 6 March 2023, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=36879042)). Their conclusion is that "a substantial portion of people's judgements of the accuracy of news reflects motivational factors". Their incentives, like the accuracy prompts, raised accuracy by changing what participants were trying to do, so both camps have experiments in which attention to accuracy helps; they differ on whether the default failure is inattention or a competing social goal.

## Identity-protective cognition

### Kahan's account

The strongest form of the motivational view comes from Dan Kahan of Yale. In his account, when people display identity-protective cognition, their processing of information "will more reliably guide them to perceptions of fact that are congruent with their membership in ideologically or culturally defined affinity groups than to ones that reflect the best available scientific evidence" ([Kahan, Judgment and Decision Making 8(4), July 2013](https://journal.sjdm.org/13/13313/jdm13313.pdf)). He argues it is individually rational: a single person's error about the science of climate change "will not affect the level of risk for her or for any other person or thing she cares about", whereas a belief out of line with her group "could mark her as untrustworthy or stupid, and thus compromise her relationships with others".

The account predicts something the inattention account does not: that more capable reasoners should be more polarized, because they are better at fitting evidence to their side. Kahan's 2013 study of 1,750 US adults found that "subjects who scored highest in cognitive reflection were the most likely to display ideologically motivated cognition", and that conservatives did "no better or worse than liberals" on the CRT ([Kahan, 2013](https://journal.sjdm.org/13/13313/jdm13313.pdf)). A 2012 study by Kahan and colleagues reported that the people with "the highest degrees of science literacy and technical reasoning capacity were not the most concerned about climate change. Rather, they were the ones among whom cultural polarization was greatest" ([Kahan et al., Nature Climate Change 2, 27 May 2012, abstract](https://www.nature.com/articles/nclimate1547)).

### The replications and the confound

Two lines of work have weakened the strongest version. A preregistered replication of Kahan, Ellen Peters, Erica Dawson and Paul Slovic's "motivated numeracy" experiment, by Erik Persson and colleagues in Sweden, reports: "We did not find good evidence for motivated numeracy"; ideologically congruent responding appeared but was "not moderated by numeracy", and where moderation appeared it was "only at the highest levels of numeracy", so "the cumulative evidence for motivated numeracy is weaker than previously thought" ([Persson et al., Cognition 214, September 2021, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=34051421)).

Ben Tappin, Pennycook and Rand pointed to a design problem: in such studies "political group identity is often confounded with prior factual beliefs about the issue in question; and, crucially, reasoning can be affected by such beliefs in the absence of any political group motivation" ([Tappin, Pennycook and Rand, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General 150(6), June 2021, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=33119359)). Controlling for prior beliefs in three studies, they "observed a robust direct effect of political group identity on reasoning but found no evidence that cognitive sophistication magnified this effect". James Druckman and Mary McGrath make a related argument for climate change: the evidence usually cited for directional motivated reasoning "is equally consistent with a theory in which citizens strive to form accurate beliefs but vary in what they consider to be credible evidence" ([Druckman and McGrath, Nature Climate Change, 21 January 2019, abstract](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-018-0360-1)).

<Claim status="established" by="by Tappin, Pennycook and Rand, among others">Group identity has a direct effect on how people reason about contested facts</Claim>; <Claim status="disputed" by="by the Swedish replication and the confound critique">that greater reasoning skill magnifies the effect</Claim> is contested. The inattention and identity accounts were also measured on different tasks, judging headlines in one case and interpreting data or risks on polarized policies in the other.

## Outside the United States

Ceren Budak, Nyhan and colleagues stress the regions outside the US and Western Europe, "where research and data are scant and harms may be more severe" ([Budak et al., Nature 630, 5 June 2024, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=38840013)). Two Brazilian studies are among the few, and both found corrections weaker than the American and multi-country results above.

**The 2018 election.** Frederico Batista Pereira, Natália Bueno, Felipe Nunes and Nara Pavão ran a face-to-face survey experiment with 2,236 voters aged 16 to 75 in Minas Gerais from 4 to 6 October 2018, days before the first round, presenting six widely circulated false stories with or without a note that a fact-checking site had found them false ([Batista Pereira et al., The Journal of Politics 84(4), October 2022, author's copy](https://nataliabueno.github.io/files/719419.pdf)). About a third of the sample accepted the stories, and "unlike what has been found in the developed world, follow-up corrective information about the falsity of the fake news fails to change minds during elections in Brazil". Corrections failed "even among a large share of self-identified nonpartisan voters and also when fact checking confirms what is suggested by individuals' political identities"; so although partisan and anti-partisan motivated reasoning predicted belief, it "does not seem to be the main driving force behind the (in)effectiveness of corrections in Brazil". The authors disclose that the data were collected and funded by Quaest, whose chief executive is the co-author Nunes, that the study was exploratory without a pre-analysis plan, and that the journal's replication analyst reproduced the analysis.

**Zika and yellow fever.** Carey, Victoria Chi, D. J. Flynn, Nyhan and Thomas Zeitzoff found in a nationally representative Brazilian survey that conspiracy theories and misperceptions about the Zika epidemic "are widely believed"; in three preregistered experiments, "corrective information not only fails to reduce targeted Zika misperceptions but also reduces the accuracy of other beliefs about the disease" ([Carey et al., Science Advances 6(5), 29 January 2020, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=32064329)). Corrective information about the better-known threat from yellow fever "was more effective", but "none of these corrections affected support for vector control policies or intentions to engage in preventive behavior".

These findings set limits on what the American literature can claim. In Porter and Wood's four-country experiments, by contrast, "fact-checks reduced belief in misinformation by at least 0.59 points on a 5-point scale" ([Porter and Wood, 2021](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=34507996)). Why corrections failed in Minas Gerais in 2018, whether through the election's intensity, distrust of fact-checkers, the face-to-face format or the stories chosen, is not settled by the paper, which calls for research across contexts. The Brazilian context of those stories, mass messaging on WhatsApp, is set out in [the part on Brazil's courts and the CPMI](/learn/brazil-mass-messaging-the-courts-and-the-cpmi).

## What the evidence suggests for those who correct

The sources above support a short list, each with its limit.

- **Replace, do not just retract.** A causal alternative fills the gap the model-updating account describes ([Debunking Handbook 2020](https://sciencelearning.umd.edu/files/2020/10/DebunkingHandbook2020.pdf)); saying why the error appeared adds little ([Connor Desai and Reimers, 2023](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/28822/1/ConnorDesai%26Reimers2022.pdf)).
- **Repeating the myth once is not the danger it was thought to be.** Even for claims the audience had never heard, a correction did not leave people worse off than if they had never seen it ([Ecker et al., 2020](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7447737/fullTextXML)).
- **Expect decay, and plan for repetition of the truth.** Fact-check effects faded in panel data ([Carey et al., 2022](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=35115678)), and the largest gain in familiarity comes from a second exposure ([Hassan and Barber, 2021](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=33983553)), which works for true statements as for false ones.
- **Make accuracy the goal of the moment.** Prompts and incentives both raised discernment in experiments, by modest and disputed amounts ([Pennycook and Rand, 2022](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=35484277); [Rathje et al., 2023](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=36879042)).
- **Do not make the correction an attack on a group.** The handbook advises "using inclusive language and avoiding the stigmatization of groups for holding inaccurate beliefs", since activating group identities may affect "whom a person will believe" ([Debunking Handbook 2020](https://sciencelearning.umd.edu/files/2020/10/DebunkingHandbook2020.pdf)); Nyhan points to the elites and media who keep the link between identity and claim alive ([Nyhan, 2021](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=33837144)).

The defences arc of this series takes up fact-checking, lateral reading and warning people in advance in the parts on verification and on prebunking, and [the part on generative AI](/learn/generative-ai-and-influence-operations) reports experiments in which conversations with language models reduced, and also increased, belief in conspiracy theories.

## What to remember

- Persistence takes several forms: continued influence after an accepted correction, belief regression over time, the rare and disputed backfire, and belief change without attitude or behaviour change.
- In the warehouse-fire experiments a corrected cause keeps shaping inferences; the retrieval and model-updating accounts explain it, and a causal alternative helps more than a bare retraction or an explanation of why the error appeared.
- One exposure raises the perceived accuracy of a false headline; the effect grows logarithmically with repetition, and how far implausibility and strong priors protect is disputed.
- Backfire reports cluster in subgroups and single-item measures; Nyhan now attributes persistence to decay and to cues from elites and the media.
- Pennycook and Rand argue that inattention, more than partisan bias, drives belief in and sharing of false news; an independent replication found the accuracy-prompt effect small, and paying for accuracy cut partisan bias by about 30%.
- Kahan's identity-protective cognition explains polarization among skilled reasoners; a Swedish replication and the confound critique weakened the claim that skill magnifies it, while a direct effect of group identity held.
- In Brazil, corrections failed for 2018 election rumours in Minas Gerais and for Zika misperceptions, against positive results in other countries.
