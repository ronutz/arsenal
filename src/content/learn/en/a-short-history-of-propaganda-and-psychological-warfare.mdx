Propaganda was first the name of a church office in Rome. Within one world war it became a word its own users avoided, and over the next century states on every side built ministries, radio services and covert units to shape what audiences believed, renaming the work as names wore out. This part of the [series](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign) follows those programmes through the records, in their makers' words where those survive. After reading, you can date each stage, say what each programme did and who says so, and separate settled facts from open disputes.

## A name from a Roman congregation

The Holy See says that Pope Gregory XV established the office by a papal bull of 22 June 1622, that it was long known as the Congregation for the Propagation of the Faith, or Propaganda Fide, and that Pope Urban VIII founded a college for it in 1627 ([Holy See, profile](https://www.vatican.va/content/romancuria/en/congregazioni/congregazione-per-levangelizzazione-dei-popoli/profilo.html)); Agenzia Fides, the Vatican's mission news agency, dates the founding itself to 6 January 1622 ([Fides, 2022](https://www.fides.org/en/news/71395-VATICAN_On_January_6_1622_Pope_Gregory_XV_founded_the_Congregation_de_Propaganda_Fide_for_the_reunification_of_Christians_and_the_spread_of_the_faith)).

Edward Bernays, in 1928, quotes the Scientific American, which dated the congregation to 1627 and found the word's meaning distorted: "The change took place mainly during the late war when the term took on a decidedly sinister complexion" ([Bernays, 1928, chapter 2](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/78634/pg78634.txt)).

## The First World War: ministries and a committee (1914 to 1919)

In Britain, The National Archives (TNA) records that from 1914 several bodies shared news, censorship and propaganda work; these merged into a Department of Information in February 1917, a Ministry of Information from March 1918, while a Department of Propaganda in Enemy Countries reported to the War Cabinet from February 1918 ([TNA, research guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/propaganda/)).

In the United States (US), Executive Order 2594 of 13 April 1917 created the Committee on Public Information (CPI), chaired by a civilian, George Creel ([Executive Order 2594](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/executive-order-2594-creating-committee-public-information)). The National Archives and Records Administration (NARA) says it "Released government news during World War I. Sustained morale. Administered voluntary press censorship." Domestic work ended after the Armistice and foreign work on 30 June 1919 ([NARA, Record Group 63](https://www.archives.gov/research/guide-fed-records/groups/063.html)). Creel's 1920 account contests this: "In no degree was the Committee an agency of censorship", he wrote, but "the world's greatest adventure in advertising" ([Creel, 1920](https://archive.org/download/howweadvertameri00creerich/howweadvertameri00creerich_djvu.txt)). In Brazil, by Creel's own account, Ambassador Edwin V. Morgan led the work, the Brazilian wireless was the only South American station to relay the committee's news as planned, and films sent through the Rio de Janeiro embassy soon became the public's favourites.

The US Army's 2026 doctrine, Army techniques publication (ATP) 3-53, says that in that war it ran a small Propaganda Section within its Press and Censorship Division, using leaflets and loudspeakers ([ATP 3-53, appendix A](https://armypubs.army.mil/epubs/DR_pubs/DR_a/ARN46327-ATP_3-53-000-WEB-1.pdf)).

## Between the wars: a suspect word, a ministry and the radio

Creel wrote that the committee avoided the word because German use had tied it to deceit and corruption (see [the words](/learn/psyop-propaganda-and-disinformation-the-words)); Bernays defended it in his 1928 book of that name, which opens: "The conscious and intelligent manipulation of the organized habits and opinions of the masses is an important element in democratic society" ([Bernays, chapter 1](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/78634/pg78634.txt)). He traced the craft to "the astounding success of propaganda during the war" (chapter 2).

In Germany the word became a ministry. From March 1933, the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum (USHMM) writes, the Nazi regime centralized its propaganda in a new ministry led by Joseph Goebbels ([USHMM, propaganda and censorship](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/nazi-propaganda-and-censorship)), who had at first opposed the term, "recognizing that in popular usage, both in Germany and abroad, it was associated with lies" ([USHMM, ministry](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/ministry-of-propaganda-and-public-enlightenment)). Film, radio, theatre and the press largely fell under his ministry; an Editors Law of 4 October 1933 required editors and journalists to register with the Reich Press Chamber and excluded Jews from the profession, and daily directives set what newspapers could print (same).

The Voice of America (VOA) lists a Dutch [shortwave](/learn/radio-spectrum) service to the Far East from 1927, a Soviet radio centre broadcasting in 50 languages and dialects by the end of 1930, Italian and British empire services from 1932, a French service in 1933, and German broadcasts into Austria and to Latin America from 1933; in 1939, it says, the United States was the only world power without a government international radio service ([VOA, history](https://www.insidevoa.com/a/3794247.html)).

## The Second World War: white and black (1939 to 1945)

TNA records that the Ministry of Information ran British news, censorship and publicity from September 1939 ([TNA, research guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/propaganda/)). Its catalogue describes the Political Warfare Executive (PWE), formed in August 1941 "to undermine enemy morale and resistance by various forms of propaganda" from parts of the British Broadcasting Corporation (BBC), the ministry and the Special Operations Executive (SOE), as a secret department working under Foreign Office cover; its papers include a complete set of the leaflets dropped by air over Germany, Italy and occupied Europe ([TNA, FO 898](https://discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk/details/r/C8197)). In the Central Intelligence Agency's (CIA) journal, J. R. Seeger relays Ann Todd's 2017 description of the PWE as "focused on conducting black propaganda operations in both theatres" ([Seeger, 2017](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/OSS-operation-black-mail.pdf)). Its black radio stations are not documented here.

The United States divided the work alike. In the War Report of the Office of Strategic Services (OSS) and in Todd's book, Seeger writes, black propaganda "must appear to be coming from among the enemy troops or originating in the Axis homelands", while white was associated with the Office of War Information (OWI), whose functions an executive order defined "as strictly white and overt, which left the need for black, or covert, propaganda" (Seeger).

White went out by radio. On 1 February 1942 the Foreign Information Service sent its first broadcast to Europe over BBC transmitters, opening in German: "The news may be good for us. The news may be bad. But we shall tell you the truth" ([VOA, history](https://www.insidevoa.com/a/3794247.html)). VOA long marked 25 February; Chris Kern, a retired VOA official, found that the scripts' sequence number "certainly points to February 1, 1942, as the date of the first broadcast", though not definitively ([Kern, 2010](https://chriskern.net/essay/voaFirstBroadcast.html)). The OWI existed from 13 June 1942 to 31 August 1945 ([NARA, Record Group 208](https://www.archives.gov/research/guide-fed-records/groups/208.html)).

Black went to the OSS Morale Operations Branch. The CIA's account of its officer Barbara Lauwers describes Operation Sauerkraut, launched in Italy hours after the failed attempt on Hitler's life in 1944 to spread "rumors, fake orders and leaflets about growing unrest among German military leaders", and her invented League of Lonely War Women, meant to make soldiers doubt their wives' fidelity. The agency says a US daily reported the league as real and that <Claim status="reported" by="by the CIA">six hundred Czechoslovak soldiers crossed to the Allies, many carrying her leaflets</Claim> ([CIA, 2019](https://cia.gov/stories/story/barbara-lauwers-deceiving-the-enemy/)). The sources here give no wartime leaflet total and do not document Fascist Italy's or Imperial Japan's programmes.

## The Cold War: open and hidden programmes on both sides

### Radios, jamming and an agency (1946 to 1999)

TNA records a Foreign Office Information Research Department set up in 1946 "to help counter Russian/Stalinist expansion, propaganda and infiltration, both in Britain and abroad" ([TNA](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/propaganda/)). In the United States the Smith-Mundt Act became law on 27 January 1948 ([US Code, title 22, section 1461, notes](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/22/1461)). Its domestic limit now reads: "No funds authorized to be appropriated to the Department of State or the Broadcasting Board of Governors shall be used to influence public opinion in the United States"; section 1078 of the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2013 allowed such material to be made available at home ([US Code, title 22, section 1461-1a](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/22/1461-1a)).

Radio Free Europe (RFE), founded in 1950, broadcast to five Eastern European countries, and Radio Liberty (RL) to the Soviet Union from 1953. In the stations' own account, "Initially, RFE and RL were funded principally by the U.S. Congress through the Central Intelligence Agency (CIA)"; CIA involvement ended in 1971 ([RFE/RL, history](https://pressroom.rferl.org/history)). Soviet-bloc documents published by the Wilson Center, edited by two former RFE/RL officials, show the other side: a 1951 Soviet decree instituting the jamming of Western broadcasts to Poland; a 1967 memo by the head of the Soviet broadcasting committee crediting Western radio, in the editors' summary, with being "an effective tool of ideological intervention"; a 1971 report by the chairman of the Soviet Committee for State Security (KGB), Andropov, on measures to weaken RFE and RL; and a 1986 Politburo memo proposing to stop jamming VOA and the BBC while still jamming RL and RFE ([Johnson and Parta, 2015](https://www.wilsoncenter.org/publication/cold-war-broadcasting)). Jamming of RL ended in November 1988, according to R. Eugene Parta, RFE/RL's former audience-research director ([Parta, 2007](https://www.hoover.org/sites/default/files/uploads/documents/978-0-8179-4732-3_xix.pdf)).

A presidential reorganization plan effective on 1 August 1953 established the United States Information Agency (USIA) ([Reorganization Plan No. 8 of 1953](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-67/pdf/STATUTE-67-Pg642.pdf)). A State Department draft of a National Security Council directive to the agency, dated 15 July 1953 and top secret at the time, proposed that "a much greater percentage of the information program should be unattributed", and stated: "Covert propaganda operations shall be centralized in the Central Intelligence Agency" ([Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952 to 1954, document 345](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v02p2/d345)). It is a proposal, not the directive as adopted. The agency was abolished on 1 October 1999 ([NARA, Record Group 306](https://www.archives.gov/research/foreign-policy/related-records/rg-306)), under the Foreign Affairs Reform and Restructuring Act of 1998 ([White House, 1998](https://clintonwhitehouse6.archives.gov/1998/12/1998-12-30-fact-sheet-on-foreign-affairs-reorganization.html); [US Code, title 22, section 6531](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/22/6531)).

### Soviet active measures and the AIDS story (1983 to 1992)

The State Department's report of August 1987 on Soviet influence, the target government's account, used the Russian term active measures for forgeries, front groups such as the World Peace Council and agents of influence ([State Department, 1987](https://sspconline.org/sites/default/files/2026-01/soviet-influence-activities-1987_0.pdf)); no Soviet reply to it is among the sources here.

The best-documented case is the story that the virus behind acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS) came from US weapons research. It surfaced on 17 July 1983 in an anonymous letter in the Indian newspaper Patriot, headlined "AIDS may invade India: Mystery disease caused by US experiments", and by late 1987, Thomas Boghardt writes, had run in over 200 periodicals in 25 languages in 80 countries ([Boghardt, 2009](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/1-Operation_INFEKTION_Soviet_Bloc_AIDS_Disinformation_Campaign.pdf)). For Brazil the State Department lists a placement in Jornal do Brasil on 18 March 1987, a Belgian biologist telling Veja in November 1986 that the theory had no scientific basis, and an attack by the Soviet Literaturnaya Gazeta in December 1986 on O Estado de S. Paulo for having retracted the story ([State Department, 1987](https://sspconline.org/sites/default/files/2026-01/soviet-influence-activities-1987_0.pdf)). In Bulgarian archives Christopher Nehring later found a KGB telegram of 7 September 1985 whose stated goal was "to create a favorable opinion for us abroad that this disease is the result of secret experiments with a new type of biological weapon by the secret services of the USA and the Pentagon" ([Selvage and Nehring, 2019](https://www.wilsoncenter.org/blog-post/operation-denver-kgb-and-stasi-disinformation-regarding-aids)).

After Secretary of State George Shultz told Mikhail Gorbachev on 23 October 1987 that the Soviet Union had peddled "bum dope" on AIDS, the Soviet Academy of Sciences disavowed the thesis in Izvestia, while Sovetskaya Rossiya repeated it the same day; Boghardt writes that <Claim status="reported" by="by Boghardt">"Moscow had never publicly acknowledged authorship of the campaign"</Claim>. In 1992 Yevgeny Primakov, director of Russia's Foreign Intelligence Service (SVR), <Claim status="reported" by="by Boghardt">"confirmed the KGB's participation"</Claim>, saying the KGB had acted to expose the "perfidious" work of US military scientists; Boghardt cites the Boston Globe and Izvestiya of 19 March 1992, not read for this part. The story outlived it: in 1992, Boghardt reports, 15 percent of randomly selected Americans thought it definitely or probably true that the virus was made deliberately in a government laboratory, though he cautions against ascribing such beliefs to the campaign alone ([Boghardt](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/1-Operation_INFEKTION_Soviet_Bloc_AIDS_Disinformation_Campaign.pdf); see [the human attack surface](/learn/the-human-attack-surface-why-repetition-works)).

### Still disputed: the code name and the Stasi's part

All the sources here agree that the KGB led the campaign, but not on its code name. Boghardt wrote that East Germany's foreign intelligence service, the Hauptverwaltung Aufklärung (HVA), code-named the operation INFEKTION and VORWÄRTS II, citing letters from a former HVA officer, Günter Bohnsack ([Boghardt](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/1-Operation_INFEKTION_Soviet_Bloc_AIDS_Disinformation_Campaign.pdf)). Selvage and Nehring found it registered in the HVA's card file on 17 July 1986 as Denver, and call Infektion "a word that we found nowhere in the files of the Bulgarian or East German State Security with regard to AIDS" ([Selvage and Nehring](https://www.wilsoncenter.org/blog-post/operation-denver-kgb-and-stasi-disinformation-regarding-aids)).

Also contested is <Claim status="disputed">the part played by the Stasi, East Germany's Ministry for State Security, and its relation to the biologist Jakob Segal</Claim>. Erhard Geissler and Robert Hunt Sprinkle found in 2013 that the Stasi did not direct Segal and "were prone to interpretive error and self-aggrandizement" ([Geissler and Sprinkle, 2013](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/8CFEA2F6B8FA780208DFAC39DC57A03E)), and reported in 2019 that "In 2017 a German appellate court found our 2013 analysis persuasive in a defamation suit" ([Geissler and Sprinkle, 2019](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/politics-and-the-life-sciences/article/were-our-critics-right-about-the-stasi/4216D41CEA8551BF569C33D46366C77F)). Selvage and Nehring, whose 2014 study that reply answers, write that the KGB and the Stasi "both left open to what extent the Stasi influenced him", that Segal always said publicly he began his research on his own initiative, and that 90 percent of the HVA's files were destroyed or lost in 1989 and 1990 ([Selvage and Nehring](https://www.wilsoncenter.org/blog-post/operation-denver-kgb-and-stasi-disinformation-regarding-aids)).

## Later names: from psychological warfare back to psychological operations

The US Army's appendix traces its own vocabulary: propaganda in the First World War, psychological warfare (then abbreviated PsyWar) in the Second, a Psychological Warfare Center at Fort Bragg from 1952, and in Vietnam "what it formally termed psychological operations (PSYOP)" ([ATP 3-53, appendix A](https://armypubs.army.mil/epubs/DR_pubs/DR_a/ARN46327-ATP_3-53-000-WEB-1.pdf)). The renaming then went in a circle, detailed in [the words](/learn/psyop-propaganda-and-disinformation-the-words): military information support operations (MISO) under a Secretary of Defense memo of 3 December 2010 ([Joint Publication (JP) 3-13.2](https://www.bits.de/NRANEU/others/jp-doctrine/JP3-13.2C1%2811%29.pdf)); PSYOP again for Army units from 27 October 2017 ([Cook and Cowen, 2018](https://www.psywarrior.com/PSYOPvsMiso.htm)); and PSYOP for the function under a memorandum stamped 2 December 2025, saying the 2010 change was meant "to disassociate such activities from misleading or negative connotations" ([memorandum, 2025](https://media.defense.gov/2025/Dec/08/2003839440/-1/-1/0/CHANGING-THE-TERM-MILITARY-INFORMATION-SUPPORT-OPERATIONS-BACK-TO-PSYCHOLOGICAL-OPERATIONS.PDF)). ATP 3-53 of 20 April 2026 keeps MISO "only to provide historical context" and reserves propaganda for adversaries (introduction; paragraph 5-163).

## What to remember

- Propaganda takes its name from the Congregation for the Propagation of the Faith, which the Holy See dates to a bull of 22 June 1622; by 1928 the Scientific American said the war had made the word sinister.
- In the First World War Britain built a Ministry of Information and the United States the CPI, which Creel called advertising and NARA says administered voluntary press censorship.
- From March 1933 a German propaganda ministry under Goebbels controlled most film, radio, theatre and press.
- In the Second World War Britain and the United States split white from black: VOA and the OWI spoke openly; the PWE and the OSS made material that seemed to come from the enemy.
- In the Cold War both blocs ran open and hidden programmes: the CIA funded RFE and RL until 1971, the Soviet Union jammed RL until November 1988, and a KGB-led campaign spread the AIDS story from 1983.
- Every source here agrees that the KGB led the AIDS campaign; the Stasi's share and the code name, INFEKTION or Denver, are disputed.
- US terms moved from propaganda to psychological warfare, PSYOP, MISO (2010) and PSYOP again (2025); ATP 3-53 (2026) keeps propaganda for adversaries.
