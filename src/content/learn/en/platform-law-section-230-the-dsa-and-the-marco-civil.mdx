A false accusation, a call to violence or a fake profile is posted by a user, carried by a platform and seen by thousands. The person who posted it is liable under ordinary law almost everywhere. The question that divides legal systems is the next one: does the platform answer too, from what moment, and who decides that the content was unlawful in the first place, a judge, a regulator or the platform's own staff? [The map that opens this series](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign) called this arbitration. This part sets out the main answers on the record: Section 230 in the United States (US), the directive of 2000 and the Digital Services Act (DSA) of the European Union (EU), Germany's deadline law, the Online Safety Act of the United Kingdom (UK), India's reading of its intermediary rule and Brazil's Marco Civil da Internet, which the Supremo Tribunal Federal (STF, Federal Supreme Court) rewrote in 2025 and 2026. Each regime's defenders and critics are quoted in their own words; this page reaches no verdict between them. How platforms enforce their own rules, and with what numbers, is the subject of the part on [content moderation at scale](/learn/content-moderation-at-scale); the state's power to watch communications is the subject of the [next part](/learn/surveillance-capability-and-restraint).

## Three questions every regime answers

Every intermediary liability rule, however worded, answers three questions. Who counts as an intermediary rather than a publisher? What event makes the intermediary liable: never, knowledge of the content, a private notice, a court order, or a failure of its systems as a whole? And who decides that a given item is unlawful? The table sets the regimes covered below side by side; each line is sourced in its section.

| Regime | What makes the platform liable for a user's content | Who decides legality first | Main sanction or remedy |
|---|---|---|---|
| United States, 47 U.S.C. 230 (1996) | In general nothing: the platform is not treated as the publisher; exceptions for federal crimes, intellectual property, privacy law and, since 2018, sex trafficking | Courts, in suits against the author | Suit against the author; criminal law |
| European Union, Directive 2000/31 and Digital Services Act (2000 and 2022) | Failing to act expeditiously once it has actual knowledge, which a sufficiently precise notice can give | The platform on notice, subject to complaint, out-of-court settlement and courts | Liability for the content; for the largest platforms, fines of up to 6% of worldwide turnover for breaching procedural and risk duties |
| Germany, Network Enforcement Act (Netzwerkdurchsetzungsgesetz, NetzDG), 2017 to 2024 | Failing to run a complaint procedure that removes "manifestly unlawful" content within 24 hours and other unlawful content, as a rule, within seven days | The platform, under fines | Fines of up to 50 million euros; repealed in substance when the DSA applied |
| United Kingdom, Online Safety Act 2023 | Failing duties to assess and mitigate the risk of illegal content and of content harmful to children | The platform, under codes of practice from the regulator | Fines of up to the greater of £18 million and 10% of qualifying worldwide revenue |
| India, section 79 as read in 2015 | Failing to act on a court order or a government notification | A court or the government | Loss of the exemption |
| Brazil, Marco Civil da Internet, art. 19 (2014) | Failing to obey a specific court order | A court | Civil damages |
| Brazil, STF thesis (2025, final in 2026) | Failing to remove crimes or unlawful acts after notice; presumed fault for paid ads and artificial distribution; systemic failure for a closed list of grave crimes; court order still required for offences against honour | The platform on notice, then the courts | Civil damages, jointly with the author |

## United States: Section 230

**The text.** Section 230 of the Communications Act, enacted as part of the Communications Decency Act (CDA) of 1996, has two operative sentences. The first: "No provider or user of an interactive computer service shall be treated as the publisher or speaker of any information provided by another information content provider." The second protects "any action voluntarily taken in good faith to restrict access to or availability of material that the provider or user considers to be obscene, lewd, lascivious, filthy, excessively violent, harassing, or otherwise objectionable, whether or not such material is constitutionally protected" ([47 U.S.C. 230, United States Code, 2024 edition](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title47/html/USCODE-2024-title47-chap5-subchapII-partI-sec230.htm)). Congress stated as policy "to preserve the vibrant and competitive free market that presently exists for the Internet and other interactive computer services, unfettered by Federal or State regulation", and also "to ensure vigorous enforcement of Federal criminal laws to deter and punish trafficking in obscenity, stalking, and harassment by means of computer". Subsection (e) keeps federal criminal law, intellectual property law and the Electronic Communications Privacy Act outside the shield, and since 2018 certain sex-trafficking claims as well (same source).

**Why it was written.** The conference report on the Telecommunications Act says: "One of the specific purposes of this section is to overrule Stratton-Oakmont v. Prodigy and any other similar decisions which have treated such providers and users as publishers or speakers of content that is not their own because they have restricted access to objectionable material" ([House of Representatives, conference report 104-458, 31 January 1996](https://www.congress.gov/104/crpt/hrpt458/CRPT-104hrpt458.pdf)). The section's two authors, Senator Ron Wyden and former Representative Christopher Cox, told the Supreme Court in 2023 that Prodigy had been held potentially liable "because it had tried but failed to screen all harmful content from its site", and that Congress enacted the section "to protect Internet platforms' ability to publish and present user-generated content in real time, and to encourage them to screen and remove illegal or offensive content" ([Wyden and Cox, amicus brief in Gonzalez v. Google, 19 January 2023](https://www.supremecourt.gov/DocketPDF/21/21-1333/252645/20230119135536095_21-1333%20bsac%20Wyden%20Cox.pdf)).

**The free-expression reading: Zeran (1997).** The first appellate ruling to apply the section read it broadly. Kenneth Zeran sued America Online, arguing that it "unreasonably delayed in removing defamatory messages posted by an unidentified third party" and that the section "leaves intact liability for interactive computer service providers who possess notice of defamatory material". The Fourth Circuit held that "Congress recognized the threat that tort-based lawsuits pose to freedom of speech in the new and burgeoning Internet medium" and that "The specter of tort liability in an area of such prolific speech would have an obvious chilling effect" ([Zeran v. America Online, US Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit, 12 November 1997](https://www.ca4.uscourts.gov/Opinions/Published/971523.P.pdf)). On notice-based liability specifically, the court reasoned that providers "would have a natural incentive simply to remove messages upon notification, whether the contents were defamatory or not", and that notice would give "third parties with a no-cost means to create the basis for future lawsuits". The court added that this did not mean "the original culpable party who posts defamatory messages would escape accountability". That reasoning, which rejects liability on notice as a censorship incentive, recurs in every jurisdiction below, from German critics in 2017 to a Brazilian dissent in 2025.

**The first limit: material contribution (2008).** The shield covers information "provided by another information content provider", and the statute defines that provider as anyone "responsible, in whole or in part, for the creation or development" of the information (47 U.S.C. 230(f)(3)). Sitting en banc, the Ninth Circuit held in 2008 that a housing website that required users to answer discriminatory questions was not immune for those answers, and defined "development" as "materially contributing to its alleged unlawfulness": "a website helps to develop unlawful content, and thus falls within the exception to section 230, if it contributes materially to the alleged illegality of the conduct" ([Fair Housing Council v. Roommates.com, US Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, en banc, 3 April 2008](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2008/04/02/0456916.pdf)).

**A carve-out by statute (2018).** The Allow States and Victims to Fight Online Sex Trafficking Act of 2017 (FOSTA), signed on 11 April 2018, was enacted "to clarify that section 230 of such Act does not prohibit the enforcement against providers and users of interactive computer services of Federal and State criminal and civil law relating to sexual exploitation of children or sex trafficking" ([Public Law 115-164, 11 April 2018](https://www.congress.gov/115/plaws/publ164/PLAW-115publ164.pdf)); it is the source of subsection (e)(5).

**The harm-prevention critique.** In June 2020 the Department of Justice (DOJ) concluded that "significant technological changes since 1996 and the expansive interpretation that courts have given Section 230" had "left online platforms both immune for a wide array of illicit activity on their services and free to moderate content with little transparency or accountability" ([Department of Justice, Section 230 review, key takeaways, June 2020](https://www.justice.gov/file/1286331/download)). It proposed a "Bad Samaritan" carve-out for a platform "that purposefully facilitates or solicits third-party content or activity that would violate federal criminal law", carve-outs for child exploitation, terrorism and cyber-stalking, and an exception where a platform "had actual knowledge or notice that the third party content at issue violated federal criminal law or where the platform was provided with a court judgment that content is unlawful in any respect". In October 2020 Justice Clarence Thomas wrote, in agreeing that the Supreme Court should not hear a particular case, that "Courts have long emphasized nontextual arguments when interpreting §230, leaving questionable precedent in their wake", and that "Extending §230 immunity beyond the natural reading of the text can have serious consequences", citing cases in which platforms were held immune for "knowingly host[ing] illegal child pornography" and for race discrimination ([Malwarebytes v. Enigma Software, statement of Justice Thomas respecting the denial of certiorari, 13 October 2020](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/20pdf/19-1284_869d.pdf)).

**The Supreme Court declines (2023).** The family of a 23-year-old US citizen killed in the 2015 Paris attacks sued Google under the Anti-Terrorism Act, alleging that YouTube had aided the Islamic State (ISIS); the Ninth Circuit had held most of the claims barred by Section 230 ([Gonzalez v. Google, Supreme Court, per curiam, 18 May 2023](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/21-1333_6j7a.pdf)). The Supreme Court did not rule on the section. In a companion case it held unanimously, in an opinion by Justice Thomas, that the platforms' "recommendation" algorithms were "merely part of that infrastructure", that "the algorithms appear agnostic as to the nature of the content", and that "The mere creation of those platforms, however, is not culpable" ([Twitter v. Taamneh, Supreme Court, 18 May 2023](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/21-1496_d18f.pdf)). Having so held, it said in Gonzalez that it would "decline to address the application of §230 to a complaint that appears to state little, if any, plausible claim for relief". Wyden and Cox had urged it to hold that the section covers such recommendations, which they called "the direct descendants of the early content curation efforts that Congress had in mind" (amicus brief, 2023).

**Curation as the platform's own speech (2024).** Ruling on statutes that Florida and Texas enacted in 2021 to regulate large social media platforms, the Supreme Court held in July 2024 that "the First Amendment offers protection when an entity engaged in compiling and curating others' speech into an expressive product of its own is directed to accommodate messages it would prefer to exclude" ([Moody v. NetChoice, Supreme Court, 1 July 2024](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/22-277_d18f.pdf)). Eight weeks later the Third Circuit drew the other consequence. A ten-year-old girl died after TikTok's "For You Page" showed her a "Blackout Challenge" video; her mother sued, and the district court dismissed the claims under Section 230 ([Anderson v. TikTok, US Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit, 27 August 2024](https://www2.ca3.uscourts.gov/opinarch/223061p.pdf)). The appeals court reversed in part: since the algorithm, as alleged, decided what to include and present, "TikTok's algorithm ... was TikTok's own 'expressive activity,' id., and thus its first-party speech", outside the shield for third-party content. The panel quoted Justice Thomas's earlier observation that "[i]n the platforms' world, they are fully responsible for their websites when it results in constitutional protections, but the moment that responsibility could lead to liability, they can disclaim any obligations". The ruling binds only the Third Circuit; the Taamneh opinion of the year before had described recommendation algorithms as neutral infrastructure for the different question of aiding and abetting.

## European Union: from the 2000 directive to the Digital Services Act

**The directive of 2000.** The Electronic Commerce Directive gave three safe harbours. A "mere conduit" is not liable for what it transmits if it does not initiate the transmission, select the receiver or modify the information; a cache is protected on similar conditions; and a host is not liable for stored information if it "does not have actual knowledge of illegal activity or information" or, "upon obtaining such knowledge or awareness, acts expeditiously to remove or to disable access to the information" ([Directive 2000/31/EC, articles 12 to 14, 8 June 2000](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32000L0031)). Article 15 forbade member states to impose "a general obligation on providers ... to monitor the information which they transmit or store". The model is knowledge-based, close to the notice regimes Section 230 was written to avoid.

**How far a removal order may reach (2019).** Eva Glawischnig-Piesczek, a former member of the Austrian National Council who had chaired the Greens' parliamentary party, sued Facebook in Austria over a user's comment that the Austrian courts found defamatory. The Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) held that EU law "does not preclude a host provider such as Facebook from being ordered to remove identical and, in certain circumstances, equivalent comments previously declared to be illegal", and that it "does not preclude such an injunction from producing effects worldwide, within the framework of the relevant international law" ([CJEU, press release 128/19, case C-18/18, 3 October 2019](https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/upload/docs/application/pdf/2019-10/cp190128en.pdf)).

**The Digital Services Act (DSA).** Regulation (EU) 2022/2065 kept the three exemptions and the ban on general monitoring: "No general obligation to monitor the information which providers of intermediary services transmit or store, nor actively to seek facts or circumstances indicating illegal activity shall be imposed on those providers" ([Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, Digital Services Act, article 8, 19 October 2022](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065)). It added its own version of Section 230's second sentence: providers do not lose the exemptions "solely because they, in good faith and in a diligent manner, carry out voluntary own-initiative investigations" into illegal content (article 7). Around that core it built procedure:

- **Notice and action** (article 16): hosts must let anyone flag "specific items of information that the individual or entity considers to be illegal content"; a notice that lets "a diligent provider of hosting services to identify the illegality ... without a detailed legal examination" gives actual knowledge, which ends the exemption if the host does not act.
- **Orders** (article 9): an authority's order to act against content must state its legal basis, explain why the content is illegal, and give "information about redress mechanisms".
- **Reasons and redress**: a "clear and specific statement of reasons" for every removal, demotion, demonetization or suspension (article 17); a free internal complaint system for at least six months (article 20); certified out-of-court dispute settlement bodies, which "shall not have the power to impose a binding settlement" and do not displace the courts (article 21).
- **Trusted flaggers** (article 22): entities certified by national regulators whose notices get priority, who must publish yearly reports on their notices.
- **Advertising**: no advertisements "based on profiling ... using special categories of personal data" (article 26(3)), and none based on profiling when the platform knows "with reasonable certainty" that the user is a minor (article 28(2)).
- **Very large platforms**: services with at least 45 million average monthly active users in the Union (article 33) must assess "systemic risks", among them "any actual or foreseeable negative effects for the exercise of fundamental rights", including "freedom of expression and information", and "any actual or foreseeable negative effects on civic discourse and electoral processes, and public security" (article 34); take "reasonable, proportionate and effective mitigation measures" (article 35); offer at least one recommender option "not based on profiling" (article 38); and give vetted researchers access to data (article 40).
- **Fines**: up to "6 % of its total worldwide annual turnover in the preceding financial year" (article 74). The regulation applies in full from 17 February 2024 (article 93).

The Commission designated the first very large platforms and search engines on 25 April 2023 ([European Commission, supervision of the designated very large online platforms and search engines, read 15 November 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/list-designated-vlops-and-vloses)).

**Enforcement.** On 5 December 2025 the Commission issued its first non-compliance decision, fining X 120 million euros for "the deceptive design of its 'blue checkmark', the lack of transparency of its advertising repository, and the failure to provide access to public data for researchers"; it wrote that "While the DSA does not mandate user verification, it clearly prohibits online platforms from falsely claiming that users have been verified, when no such verification took place" ([European Commission, Commission fines X €120 million under the Digital Services Act, 5 December 2025](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-fines-x-eu120-million-under-digital-services-act); date from [the Commission's supervision page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/list-designated-vlops-and-vloses)). The same page lists a fine of 200 million euros on Temu on 28 May 2026 and of 550 million euros on AliExpress on 20 July 2026.

**The dispute over the DSA.** <Claim status="alleged" by="by the Republican majority staff of the House Judiciary Committee">"The DSA is the EU's comprehensive digital censorship law"</Claim>, the Republican staff of the US House Judiciary Committee wrote in July 2025, arguing that requiring platforms to mitigate risks such as "disinformation" and "hate speech" "requires a platform to censor user content" ([House Judiciary Committee majority staff, The Foreign Censorship Threat, 25 July 2025](https://judiciary.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/republicans-judiciary.house.gov/files/2025-07/DSA_Report&Appendix%2807.25.25%29.pdf)). The committee's Democrats replied that "There is no evidence that the European Commission classifies political debate as 'hate speech,' and the Report does not present a single case substantiating this distortion", and that trusted flaggers "don't have a magic delete button" because "the platform alone determines whether or not to remove content flagged" ([House Judiciary Committee Democrats, Debunking Republicans' Misleading Report on the EU's Digital Services Act, 2025](https://democrats-judiciary.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/democrats-judiciary.house.gov/files/evo-media-document/debunking-republicans-misleading-report-on-the-eu-s-digital-services-act-dsa.pdf)). When X was fined, US Vice President JD Vance said the EU was fining X "for not engaging in censorship", and the chair of the Federal Communications Commission (FCC), Brendan Carr, spoke of "fining a successful U.S. tech company for being a successful U.S. tech company"; a Commission spokesman, Thomas Regnier, answered that "The DSA applies equally to all online platforms offering their services in the EU" ([The Register, 5 December 2025](https://www.theregister.com/2025/12/05/eu_fines_x_dsa)). The Commission's executive vice-president Henna Virkkunen said that "Misleading users with blue checkmarks, obscuring ad information, and excluding researchers are unacceptable in the EU"; Regnier added that "The decision is about transparency obligations under the DSA, not about freedom of speech", and Elon Musk vowed a court battle ([Creatives Unite, December 2025](https://creativesunite.eu/article/eu-fines-x-million-musk-and-top-us-officials-respond-with-fury)).

## Germany: the deadline model (2017 to 2024)

**The bill's case.** The German government's bill of May 2017 opened by describing debate online as "oft aggressiv, verletzend und nicht selten hasserfüllt" (often aggressive, hurtful and not seldom hateful), and stated that "Nach den Erfahrungen im US-Wahlkampf hat überdies auch in der Bundesrepublik Deutschland die Bekämpfung von strafbaren Falschnachrichten („Fake News“) in sozialen Netzwerken hohe Priorität gewonnen": after the experience of the US election campaign, fighting criminal false news on social networks had become a high priority in Germany too ([Deutscher Bundestag, Drucksache 18/12356, government bill, 16 May 2017](https://dserver.bundestag.de/btd/18/123/1812356.pdf)). The goal was to remove "objektiv strafbare Inhalte" (objectively criminal content), such as incitement to hatred, insult and defamation, "unverzüglich" (without delay).

**What it required.** As Human Rights Watch (HRW) summarized the Network Enforcement Act (Netzwerkdurchsetzungsgesetz, NetzDG) in force from 2017, companies with more than 2 million registered users in Germany had to "block or remove 'manifestly unlawful' content within 24 hours of receiving a complaint but have up to one week or potentially more if further investigation is required", could refer complex cases to an industry-funded but government-authorized body, had to report every six months, and faced fines of up to 50 million euros for failing to set up a compliance system ([Human Rights Watch, Germany: Flawed Social Media Law, 14 February 2018](https://www.hrw.org/news/2018/02/14/germany-flawed-social-media-law)).

**The critique.** Before the law passed, the United Nations (UN) Special Rapporteur on freedom of expression, David Kaye, wrote to the German government that "The liability placed upon private companies to remove third party content absent a judicial oversight is not compatible with international human rights law", and that "The short deadlines, coupled with the afore-mentioned severe penalties, could lead social networks to over-regulate expression" ([UN Special Rapporteur, communication OL DEU 1/2017, 1 June 2017](https://www.ohchr.org/Documents/Issues/Opinion/Legislation/OL-DEU-1-2017.pdf)). Wenzel Michalski, HRW's Germany director, called it "vague, overbroad, and turns private companies into overzealous censors to avoid steep fines, leaving users with no judicial oversight or right to appeal"; HRW reported that at least three countries, Russia, Singapore and the Philippines, "have directly cited the German law as a positive example" (HRW, 2018).

**What remains.** The NetzDG's sections 2 to 3f, the complaint and deadline duties, are now marked "weggefallen" (repealed); the law as last amended in May 2024 keeps a duty for some providers to name an agent for service in Germany and fines of up to 500,000 euros for not doing so, and its transition clause refers to the version in force until 16 February 2024, the eve of the DSA's full application ([Netzwerkdurchsetzungsgesetz, consolidated text, gesetze-im-internet.de](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/netzdg/BJNR335210017.html)).

## United Kingdom: the Online Safety Act 2023

**Purpose and duties.** The Act "provides for a new regulatory framework which has the general purpose of making the use of internet services regulated by this Act safer for individuals in the United Kingdom", by requiring providers "to identify, mitigate and manage the risks of harm" from "illegal content and activity" and "content and activity that is harmful to children", with the Office of Communications (Ofcom) as regulator; services are to be "safe by design" and operated so that "users' rights to freedom of expression and privacy are protected" ([Online Safety Act 2023, section 1, legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/section/1)). User-to-user services must use "proportionate measures" to "prevent individuals from encountering priority illegal content" and must "swiftly take down" illegal content when alerted ([section 10](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/section/10)); since 29 June 2026 an amendment by the Crime and Policing Act 2026 requires content subject to an intimate image report to be taken down "no later than 48 hours" after the report, save for stated exceptions (same source). A separate section imposes "a duty to have particular regard to the importance of protecting users' right to freedom of expression within the law" ([section 22](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/section/22)). Penalties can reach the greater of "£18 million" and "10% of the person's qualifying worldwide revenue" ([Schedule 13](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/schedule/13)).

**Timetable.** The government's explainer records that the illegal content duties became enforceable on 17 March 2025, and that services likely to be accessed by children had until 24 July 2025 to complete their children's risk assessments ([Department for Science, Innovation and Technology, Online Safety Act explainer, updated 24 April 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/online-safety-act-explainer/online-safety-act-explainer)). On disinformation it says the Act captures it "where it is illegal or harmful to children", including "illegal, state-sponsored disinformation through the Foreign Interference Offence" (same source).

**Encryption.** Section 121 lets Ofcom require a service to "use accredited technology to identify CSEA content, whether communicated publicly or privately", meaning child sexual exploitation and abuse material ([section 121](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/section/121)). During the bill's final stage in September 2023 the minister, Lord Parkinson, said "A notice can be issued only where technically feasible and where technology has been accredited as meeting minimum standards of accuracy"; Matthew Hodgson, chief executive of the encrypted messaging developer Element, replied that "Scanning is fundamentally incompatible with end-to-end encrypted messaging apps" ([The Register, 7 September 2023](https://www.theregister.com/2023/09/07/uk_government_clause_online_safety_bill/)). The Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) wrote that "Nothing in the law's text has changed" ([EFF, 8 September 2023](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2023/09/uk-government-knows-how-extreme-online-safety-bill)).

**Wikipedia in court.** The Wikimedia Foundation challenged the regulations that define "Category 1", the tier with the heaviest duties, arguing that Wikipedia would be caught although it was not the policy intention. On 11 August 2025 the High Court dismissed the claim, but Mr Justice Johnson stressed "that this does not give Ofcom and the Secretary of State a green light to implement a regime that would significantly impede Wikipedia's operations", which would have to be "justified as proportionate if it were not to amount to a breach of the right to freedom of expression" ([Wikimedia Foundation v Secretary of State for Science, Innovation and Technology, [2025] EWHC 2086 (Admin), 11 August 2025](https://www.judiciary.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Wikimedia-Foundation-v-Secretary-of-State-for-Science-Innovation-and-Technology.pdf)).

## India: a court order by interpretation (2015)

India's Information Technology Act exempts intermediaries unless, on receiving "actual knowledge", they fail to remove unlawful material. In 2015 the Supreme Court of India read that clause down: "Section 79(3)(b) has to be read down to mean that the intermediary upon receiving actual knowledge that a court order has been passed asking it to expeditiously remove or disable access to certain material must then fail to expeditiously remove or disable access to that material", because "otherwise it would be very difficult for intermediaries like Google, Facebook etc. to act when millions of requests are made and the intermediary is then to judge as to which of such requests are legitimate and which are not" ([Shreya Singhal v. Union of India, Supreme Court of India, 24 March 2015](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/110813550/)). The final order also allowed notification "by the appropriate government or its agency" (same source). The same reasoning about volume and private judgment appears in the Brazilian law of the year before.

## Brazil: from the court order to the duty of care

**The Marco Civil (2014).** Law 12.965/2014 frees connection providers from civil liability for third-party content (article 18). For application providers, article 19 opens with its purpose, "Com o intuito de assegurar a liberdade de expressão e impedir a censura" (to ensure freedom of expression and prevent censorship), and makes them liable for third-party content only "se, após ordem judicial específica, não tomar as providências" (if, after a specific court order, they fail to act); the order must contain, "sob pena de nulidade" (on pain of nullity), a "identificação clara e específica do conteúdo" (clear and specific identification of the content) ([Lei nº 12.965, de 23 de abril de 2014, Senado Federal, legislation portal](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/584776/publicacao/15630642)). One exception was written in: for private nude or sexual images published without consent, article 21 makes the provider subsidiarily liable if it fails to remove them after a notice that identifies the material and the requester's standing (same source).

**The 2025 judgment.** Two extraordinary appeals (recursos extraordinários, RE) reached the STF with general effect: a fake Facebook profile created in the name of a person who had no account, which the platform did not remove after a report through its own tool (Tema 987, RE 1.037.396, rapporteur Dias Toffoli), and an Orkut community created to insult a teacher, which the network refused to remove (Tema 533, RE 1.057.258, rapporteur Luiz Fux; Google is the party) ([STF, case summary, Temas 987 and 533, 2025](https://sipla.ip.mpg.de/fileadmin/Files/Files/STF_Responsabilidade_de_plataformas_digitais_por_conteudo_de_terceiros.pdf)). On 26 June 2025, by 8 votes to 3, with Justices André Mendonça, Edson Fachin and Nunes Marques dissenting, <Claim status="established" by="by the STF">the court held article 19 "parcialmente inconstitucional", because its general rule "não confere proteção suficiente a bens jurídicos constitucionais de alta relevância (proteção de direitos fundamentais e da democracia)"</Claim>, that is, it gives insufficient protection to fundamental rights and democracy (same source).

**The thesis as finalized in 2026.** The court heard motions for clarification (embargos de declaração) by Facebook, Google and admitted parties, and on 17 June 2026 its president, Fachin, presented the final thesis; the court unanimously declared the cases closed to further appeal ([Teletime, 17 June 2026](https://teletime.com.br/17/06/2026/stf-conteudos-marco-civil-internet/)). The final text ([STF, tese de repercussão geral, Marco Civil da Internet, June 2026](https://noticias-stf-wp-prd.s3.sa-east-1.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/wpallimport/uploads/2026/06/17180658/MCI_tese.pdf)) provides that, until Congress legislates:

- **Notice, for crimes and unlawful acts.** Providers are liable "de forma solidária" (jointly with the author), under the article 21 model, for damages from third-party content in cases of "crime ou atos ilícitos", "salvo se demonstrada dúvida razoável quanto à ilicitude, após análise de diligência qualificada por parte do provedor" (unless they show reasonable doubt about the illegality after a qualified diligence analysis). The same applies to accounts reported as inauthentic.
- **Court order, for honour.** For crimes or civil wrongs against honour, article 19 still applies, "sem prejuízo da possibilidade de remoção por notificação extrajudicial" (removal on private notice remains possible); once a court has found a statement offensive, all social networks must remove identical copies on judicial or private notice.
- **Article 19 intact** for e-mail and instant messaging "exclusivamente no que diz respeito às comunicações interpessoais" (only as regards interpersonal communications), protected by the secrecy of communications, for closed video or voice meetings, and for providers with no interference in the flow of information.
- **Presumed fault** for unlawful content in "anúncios e impulsionamentos pagos" (paid ads and boosting) and "mecanismos artificiais de disseminação inorgânica" (artificial mechanisms of inorganic dissemination), with liability possible without notice unless the provider shows it acted diligently and in reasonable time.
- **A duty of care for grave crimes.** Providers are liable for failing to make content unavailable immediately in a closed list: anti-democratic acts under listed articles of the Penal Code, terrorism, inducement to suicide or self-harm, incitement to discrimination by race, colour, ethnicity, religion, national origin, sexuality or gender identity, crimes against women because of their sex, sexual crimes against vulnerable people and child sexual abuse material, and human trafficking. This liability requires "falha sistêmica" (systemic failure), defined as failing to adopt adequate prevention or removal measures; "A existência de conteúdo ilícito de forma isolada, atomizada, não é, por si só, suficiente" (an isolated item is not enough by itself). A user whose post is removed under this item may ask a court to restore it, with no damages owed by the provider.
- **Other duties.** Marketplaces answer under the Código de Defesa do Consumidor (CDC, Consumer Defence Code); providers must adopt self-regulation covering notices, due process and yearly transparency reports, open channels for users and non-users, and keep a headquarters and legal representative in Brazil with full powers. There is no strict liability, the court appeals to Congress to legislate, the thesis applies from the publication of the judgment's minutes on 5 August 2025, and providers have 60 days from the publication of the minutes of the clarification judgment to implement the duty of care.

**The arguments in the court.** Justice Alexandre de Moraes, in the majority, asked whether "as Big Techs podem impor a todos os países, inclusive ao Brasil, o seu modelo de negócio agressivo e perverso" (whether big technology companies may impose their aggressive and perverse business model on every country, Brazil included), whether social networks should remain a "terra sem lei" (lawless land), and said that "a liberdade de expressão não é liberdade de agressão" (freedom of expression is not freedom of aggression), attributing the spread of fraudulent news to "a falida autorregulação" (failed self-regulation) ([Moraes, transcript of vote, 12 June 2025](https://static.poder360.com.br/2025/06/transcricao-voto-AlexandreMoraes-STF-responsabilizacaodasredes-12-jun-2025.pdf)). Fachin, dissenting, warned that a liability regime independent of court orders "traz consigo o risco de notificações extrajudiciais abusivas ou infundadas" (carries the risk of abusive or unfounded private notices), citing Jack Balkin's concept of "censura colateral" (collateral censorship), and held that "a necessidade de ordem judicial para se remover conteúdo gerado por terceiros é a única forma constitucionalmente adequada de compatibilizar a liberdade de expressão com o regime de responsabilidade ulterior" (the requirement of a court order is the only constitutionally adequate way to reconcile free expression with liability after the fact) ([Fachin, proposed thesis and vote, 25 June 2025](https://static.poder360.com.br/2025/06/proposta-tese-EdsonFachin-MarcoCivilInternet-25-jun-2025-1.pdf)). Mendonça, opening the dissent, held article 19 constitutional, proposed that profiles be removable only when fake or engaged in proven unlawful activity, argued that automated moderation cannot replace human judgment on sensitive speech, and criticized what he called "censura disfarçada" (disguised censorship) ([Convergência Digital, 5 June 2025](https://convergenciadigital.com.br/governo/stf-mendonca-diz-que-artigo-19-do-marco-civil-e-constitucional-e-proibe-exclusao-de-perfis/)).

**The platforms' answer.** After the 2025 judgment Google said it was concerned about the liability of social networks and was analysing the impact on its products, and Meta said that "enfraquecer o Artigo 19 do Marco Civil da Internet traz incertezas jurídicas" (weakening article 19 brings legal uncertainty) ([Olhar Digital, 27 June 2025](https://olhardigital.com.br/2025/06/27/internet-e-redes-sociais/google-e-meta-citam-riscos-a-liberdade-de-expressao-apos-decisao-do-stf/)). Electoral content follows its own rules, which the thesis expressly reserves; those rules, set by the Tribunal Superior Eleitoral, are covered in [the part on Brazil's mass messaging](/learn/brazil-mass-messaging-the-courts-and-the-cpmi), together with the stalled bill PL 2630 (Projeto de Lei, bill).

## The arguments, side by side

| Argument | Who made it, in the sources read |
|---|---|
| Liability on notice pushes intermediaries to remove lawful speech, since removal costs nothing and keeping content may cost a lawsuit | Fourth Circuit in Zeran (1997); Kaye (2017) and HRW (2018) on the NetzDG; Fachin, citing Balkin, in the STF (2025); Supreme Court of India (2015) |
| Legality should be decided by a court or an independent body, not by a company | Kaye (2017); HRW (2018); Fachin (2025); Supreme Court of India (2015) |
| Broad immunity leaves victims without redress and removes the incentive to act against illicit activity | DOJ (2020); Justice Thomas (2020) |
| A platform's ranking and recommending is its own conduct, so it should answer for it | Third Circuit in Anderson (2024), building on Moody (2024); Moraes on business models (2025) |
| Recommending is neutral infrastructure, or a descendant of the curation Section 230 was meant to protect | Supreme Court in Taamneh (2023), on aiding and abetting; Wyden and Cox (2023) |
| Self-regulation failed, and fraudulent news and hate threaten democracy | Moraes (2025); the NetzDG bill (2017) |
| Systemic risk duties and trusted flaggers amount to censorship | House Judiciary Republicans (2025); Vance and Carr (2025) |
| The DSA concerns transparency and illegal content, and platforms keep the final say on flagged content | House Judiciary Democrats (2025); Commission spokesman Regnier (2025) |
| Laws written in democracies are copied by governments with weaker rule of law | HRW (2018), citing Russia, Singapore and the Philippines |
| Scanning private messages cannot coexist with end-to-end encryption | EFF and Element (2023); against them, the UK minister: only "where technically feasible" (2023) |

Two points of agreement run through the record. Every regime above keeps the author of unlawful content liable, as Zeran itself noted. And every regime that creates a removal duty also creates, sooner or later, a route back: the DSA's complaints and out-of-court bodies, the UK Act's freedom-of-expression duty, the STF's right to ask a court to restore removed content.

## What users and defenders can do

- **Use the notice routes the law creates.** In the EU a notice that identifies the item and explains why it is illegal gives the host actual knowledge (DSA, article 16); in Brazil a notice now suffices for crimes and unlawful acts, but offences against honour still need a court order, and small claims courts can hear damage claims for online content (Marco Civil, article 19, § 3, and the STF thesis).
- **Ask for reasons and appeal.** A user in the EU is owed a statement of reasons and a free complaint for six months, and can go to a certified out-of-court body or a court (DSA, articles 17, 20 and 21); a Brazilian user whose post is removed under the duty of care can ask a court to restore it (STF thesis, item 5.5).
- **Keep evidence.** Every regime turns on what was posted, when, and what the platform was told; the web address, the date and a copy of any notice are what a court or regulator will ask for.
- **Read the platforms' own disclosures.** The DSA's statements of reasons are published in a public database, as the [opener](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign) shows; the STF requires yearly transparency reports in Brazil.

## What to remember

- Section 230 says no platform "shall be treated as the publisher or speaker" of users' content, and protects good-faith removal; it was written to overrule Stratton Oakmont v. Prodigy, and courts from Zeran (1997) read it broadly on free-expression grounds.
- Its limits came from courts (material contribution, Roommates.com, 2008) and from Congress (FOSTA, 2018); the DOJ and Justice Thomas argued in 2020 that it had been stretched; the Supreme Court declined to rule on it in 2023, and the Third Circuit held in 2024 that algorithmic curation can be the platform's own speech.
- The EU kept the 2000 safe harbours and the ban on general monitoring, and the DSA added notice and action, reasons, complaints, risk duties for platforms with 45 million users and fines of up to 6%; the first fines, from December 2025, concerned transparency, and US officials called the regime censorship while the Commission and House Democrats disputed that.
- Germany's NetzDG imposed 24-hour and seven-day removal deadlines from 2017; the UN Special Rapporteur and HRW warned of over-removal without judicial oversight; its core was repealed when the DSA applied in 2024.
- The UK Online Safety Act imposes risk duties enforced by Ofcom since March 2025, with fines of up to 10% of worldwide revenue; its power to require scanning of private messages remains contested.
- Brazil's Marco Civil required a specific court order; in 2025 and 2026 the STF made platforms liable on notice for crimes and unlawful acts, presumed fault for paid and artificial distribution, and a duty of care with systemic failure for grave crimes, while keeping the court order for offences against honour and for private messaging.

<ReaderQuestions>

- If a notice is enough to make a platform liable, what keeps notices from becoming a tool to silence lawful criticism, and if a court order is required, what protects people harmed while a case waits?
- When a platform ranks and recommends content, is it carrying other people's speech or speaking itself, and should the answer change what it is liable for?
- Should the decision that a given post is unlawful rest with a judge, an independent regulator or the platform, and what would each choice cost in speed, accuracy and accountability?
- A rule written by one democracy can be cited by governments elsewhere; should lawmakers weigh how their law could be used abroad, and how?
- Which provisions of these laws, if any, could be tested by measuring what platforms actually removed and restored, and who should have access to the data needed to test them?
