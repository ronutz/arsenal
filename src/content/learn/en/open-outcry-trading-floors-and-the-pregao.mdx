The Portuguese word for a trading session, pregão, comes from apregoar, to announce in a loud voice, as the museum of B3 (Brasil, Bolsa, Balcão), the Brazilian exchange, explains ([Museu da Bolsa do Brasil (MUB3), June 2025](https://mub3.org.br/docs/Caderno%20do%20Professor%20-%20Site.pdf)). For most of the history of organized markets that is literally how prices were found: brokers called out bids and offers to one another, closed deals with a word or a gesture and wrote them on cards. This part of the series opened by [Electronic trading: from the pit to the microsecond](/learn/electronic-trading-from-the-pit-to-the-microsecond) visits four floors: the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE), the Chicago futures pits, the London Stock Exchange before 1986 and the São Paulo floors of the Bolsa de Valores de São Paulo (Bovespa) and the Bolsa de Mercadorias & Futuros (BM&F).

After reading, you will be able to explain how a floor auction worked, read the basic Chicago hand signals, say what a specialist, a jobber and a floor operator did, give the date each floor closed with its source, describe how trades were cleared and settled in the floor era, and weigh the reasons floors were trusted against what they cost. Every fact links to the document it comes from, with the document's date where it has one.

## How a floor auction worked

A trading floor was an auction run by its members, face to face. Explaining the Chicago pits, the futures broker StoneX puts the rule this way: the highest bid or the lowest offer sets the market, a trader must "best" that price to set a new one, and the process is called price discovery ([StoneX, Trading Pit, undated](https://futures.stonex.com/futures-options-101/trading-pit)). At the NYSE, the United States (US) Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) recorded in 2008, "price discovery on the Exchange took place almost exclusively on the Floor in the form of face-to-face interactions among brokers and specialists" ([SEC, Release No. 34-58845, 24 October 2008](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2008-10-29/pdf/E8-25797.pdf)).

Every floor needed three things: a place where interest in one instrument gathered (a post, a pit, a jobber's pitch), a way to signal over the noise, and a record of each deal that could be checked afterwards. The four floors below solved those three problems in different ways.

## New York: from the Buttonwood Agreement to the specialist

### The agreement and the legend

The NYSE traces its origins to the Buttonwood Agreement, signed by 24 stockbrokers on 17 May 1792 in response to the first financial panic of the young country; it set rules for trading and fixed commissions, and aimed to make sure deals were done between trusted parties ([NYSE, History of the NYSE, undated](https://www.nyse.com/history-of-nyse)). The full text has only 82 words, as the historian Peter Eisenstadt notes: the subscribers pledged not to buy or sell public stock for anyone "at a less rate than one quarter per cent Commission on the Specie value", and to "give a preference to each other in our Negotiations" ([Eisenstadt, Prospects vol. 19, October 1994](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/prospects/article/how-the-buttonwood-tree-grew-the-making-of-a-new-york-stock-exchange-legend/68D4F4577C720CFC004C6BB6076D46F6)).

The founding story is told differently by the exchange and by its historian. The NYSE says the agreement "marks the official founding" of the exchange ([NYSE, undated](https://www.nyse.com/history-of-nyse)); Eisenstadt argues that the traditional account of brokers gathering under a buttonwood tree is <Claim status="disputed" by="by the historian Peter Eisenstadt">"largely legendary"</Claim>, and that claims that the agreement established the exchange "are without any foundation" ([Eisenstadt, October 1994](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/prospects/article/how-the-buttonwood-tree-grew-the-making-of-a-new-york-stock-exchange-legend/68D4F4577C720CFC004C6BB6076D46F6)). Both describe the same document: a minimum commission and a promise of mutual preference.

### Seats, posts and the continuous market

The NYSE dates its formal organization to a constitution adopted on 8 March 1817, which created the New York Stock & Exchange Board, laid down detailed rules and imposed fines "to keep disorderly brokers in check" ([NYSE, undated](https://www.nyse.com/history-of-nyse)). The board rented a room at 40 Wall Street where brokers met twice a day to trade a list of 30 stocks and bonds: the president called out each security in turn and the brokers shouted bids and offers from assigned chairs, which is why a membership is still called a "seat" ([NYSE, undated](https://www.nyse.com/history-of-nyse)).

The call gave way to a continuous market. In the exchange's account, rising volume after the Civil War led it to trade all stocks simultaneously, each assigned to a trading post on a large open floor, where brokers roamed to deal directly with one another ([NYSE, undated](https://www.nyse.com/history-of-nyse)); the NYSE dates continuous trading to 1871 and says brokers dealing in a particular stock "remained at one location, giving rise to the specialist" ([NYSE, 30 December 2005](https://mondovisione.com/media-and-resources/news/new-york-stock-exchange-ends-member-seat-sales-today-trading-license-auction-occ-2011124/)). In 1868 the exchange fixed the number of memberships and let members sell their seats ([NYSE, 30 December 2005](https://mondovisione.com/media-and-resources/news/new-york-stock-exchange-ends-member-seat-sales-today-trading-license-auction-occ-2011124/)).

Communication technology arrived on the floor early: the stock ticker of 1867 carried prices across the country, telephones were installed in 1878, and on 15 December 1886 volume topped one million shares for the first time; the building of 1903 had tickers, telephones and a pneumatic tube system to send orders and market data around the floor ([NYSE, undated](https://www.nyse.com/history-of-nyse)). From 1928 a Quotation Department of uniformed clerks with headsets posted the current bid and ask for member firms, furnishing about 35,000 quotations a day in 1931, until an automated system replaced it in 1960 ([NYSE, undated](https://www.nyse.com/history-of-nyse)).

### What a specialist was obliged to do

The specialist was a member allowed to act both as broker for others and as dealer for itself. Section 11(b) of the Securities Exchange Act permits exchange rules to let a member register as a specialist in that double role, and SEC Rule 11b-1 imposes two obligations, as the SEC summarized them in 2008: a negative one, that the specialist's dealings be restricted "so far as practicable" to those reasonably necessary to maintain a fair and orderly market, and an affirmative one, to deal for its own account to assist in maintaining such a market ([SEC, 24 October 2008](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2008-10-29/pdf/E8-25797.pdf)). Historically the NYSE specialist oversaw the execution of all orders arriving at the exchange in its stocks, ran the auctions on the floor and kept order in assigned securities ([SEC, 24 October 2008](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2008-10-29/pdf/E8-25797.pdf)).

The position was a privileged one, and the SEC acted on its abuse: on 30 March 2004 five specialist firms (Bear Wagner, Fleet, LaBranche, Spear Leeds & Kellogg and Van der Moolen) agreed to pay $241,823,257, of which $87,735,635 in penalties and $154,087,622 in disgorgement, to settle findings that between 1999 and 2003 their specialists had traded for the firms' own accounts ahead of executable customer orders, "interpositioning" the firms between customers whose orders should have met; the firms neither admitted nor denied the allegations ([SEC, press release 2004-42, 30 March 2004](https://www.sec.gov/news/press/2004-42.htm)). The SEC's enforcement director, Stephen Cutler, said such conduct "fundamentally undermines the fair and orderly operation of the exchange auction system" ([SEC, 30 March 2004](https://www.sec.gov/news/press/2004-42.htm)).

### Hybrid, designated market makers and a floor that remains

Automation came to New York in steps. The exchange says that in the 1970s its SuperDot system delivered an order electronically from a broker's office to the trading post and sent an execution report back "within seconds" ([NYSE, undated](https://www.nyse.com/history-of-nyse)). NYSE-listed stocks were nonetheless traded mainly by hand on the floor until October 2006, when the exchange began to offer fully automated access to its quotations ([SEC, Concept Release on Equity Market Structure, 21 January 2010](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-01-21/pdf/2010-1045.pdf)). The NYSE describes that Hybrid Market as a blend of floor auction and electronic trading that led to "the elimination of the open outcry system on the floor" in 2006 ([NYSE, undated](https://www.nyse.com/history-of-nyse)).

On 24 October 2008 the SEC approved the NYSE's proposal to phase out the specialist system in favour of designated market makers (DMMs), noting that the floor's face-to-face interactions had diminished and that information once exclusive to the floor, such as the latest quotes and last-sale prices, was now widely available off it ([SEC, 24 October 2008](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2008-10-29/pdf/E8-25797.pdf)). Under the new model DMMs no longer received the order-by-order advance "look" at incoming orders that the exchange's systems had given specialists under the Hybrid Market ([SEC, 24 October 2008](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2008-10-29/pdf/E8-25797.pdf)).

The floor still exists. On 23 March 2020, because of the COVID-19 pandemic, the NYSE operated without its trading floor for the first time in its history; it said electronic trading was uninterrupted but that data showed the floor provides investors the highest level of market quality, and it partially reopened the floor on 26 May 2020 ([NYSE, undated](https://www.nyse.com/history-of-nyse)).

## Chicago: pits, hand signals and the clearing house

### From grain to futures

The Chicago Board of Trade (CBOT) was founded on 3 April 1848 as a cash market for grain, and forward or "to-arrive" contracts began trading there almost immediately, according to the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC), the US futures regulator ([CFTC, history before the CFTC, undated](https://www.cftc.gov/About/HistoryoftheCFTC/history_precftc.html)). Its rules grew in steps the CFTC lists one by one: an Illinois charter of 18 February 1859 gave the CBOT self-regulatory authority over its members and binding grain inspection; formal rules on margin and delivery followed on 13 October 1865; on 13 October 1868 the CBOT banned "corners", which the CFTC calls the first known regulatory attempt to deter manipulation; in 1877 it began publishing futures prices regularly; and in 1883 the first clearing organization for CBOT contracts was established, at first on a voluntary basis ([CFTC, undated](https://www.cftc.gov/About/HistoryoftheCFTC/history_precftc.html)).

The Chicago Mercantile Exchange (CME) began in 1898 as the Chicago Butter and Egg Board and took its present name in 1919 ([CFTC, undated](https://www.cftc.gov/About/HistoryoftheCFTC/history_precftc.html)). In 1972 it introduced futures on foreign currencies, the first futures contract on a financial instrument, in the CFTC's account ([CFTC, the 1970s, undated](https://www.cftc.gov/About/HistoryoftheCFTC/history_1970s.html)). The two Chicago exchanges merged on 12 July 2007 to form CME Group ([CME and CBOT, 12 July 2007](https://mondovisione.com/news/cme-and-cbot-complete-merger-creating-the-leading-global-financial-exchange-2011124/)).

### The pit

Each commodity had its own pit: StoneX lists a lumber pit, a Eurodollar pit and an S&P 500 pit among CME's ([StoneX, undated](https://futures.stonex.com/futures-options-101/trading-pit)). Brokers filling customers' orders and traders dealing for themselves stood together and competed to "best" the current price, and, in StoneX's description, "a trader's action or word is a bond": what looks like chaos is a sequence of recorded trade agreements ([StoneX, undated](https://futures.stonex.com/futures-options-101/trading-pit)).

### Hand signals

Voices did not carry far enough, so the pits developed a sign language ([StoneX, Hand Signals, undated](https://futures.stonex.com/futures-options-101/hand-signals)). StoneX's guide to the signals used at CME gives three reasons for them: they work across distances of as much as 30 or 40 yards between the pits and the order desks, they are more practical than voices given the crowd and the noise, and they help customers stay anonymous because large orders do not sit on a desk where they might be seen ([StoneX, Hand Signals, undated](https://futures.stonex.com/futures-options-101/hand-signals)). The same guide says the signals came into wide use at CME in the early 1970s, after the exchange created the International Monetary Market (IMM) for financial futures, where traders could profit from price differences between CME and other markets if they were fast enough ([StoneX, undated](https://futures.stonex.com/futures-options-101/hand-signals)). It warns that some signals are unique to particular pits, and that a similar signal may mean different things in different pits ([StoneX, undated](https://futures.stonex.com/futures-options-101/hand-signals)).

The basic vocabulary, as StoneX describes it ([StoneX, Hand Signals, undated](https://futures.stonex.com/futures-options-101/hand-signals); [StoneX, Trading floor hand signals, undated](https://futures.stonex.com/blog/trading-floor-hand-signals-the-sign-language-of-futures-trading)):

| What is signalled | How | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Buy (a bid) | Palms facing the trader, as if bringing something in | Both hands raised, palms in |
| Sell (an offer) | Palms facing away, as if pushing something away | Both hands raised, palms out |
| Price, 1 to 5 | Hand held out in front of the body, fingers straight up | Two fingers up for a 2 |
| Price, 6 to 9 | The signals for 1 to 4 repeated with the hand held sideways | One finger sideways for a 6 |
| Price, 0 ("even") | A clenched fist | A fist for a bid ending in 0, such as 40 |
| Quantity, 1 to 9 contracts | The number signal touched to the chin | An open hand on the chin for 5 |
| Quantity, tens | The number signal touched to the forehead | One finger on the forehead for 10 |
| Quantity, hundreds | The number flashed near the forehead, then a fist touched to it | Open hand, then fist, for 500 |

Price signals show only the last digit of a bid or offer, because everyone in the pit knows the rest ([StoneX, undated](https://futures.stonex.com/futures-options-101/hand-signals)).

### When the pit was cheated

Open outcry could be abused by the people in it: in August 1989 a trader whose appeal later reached the US Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit was indicted with 18 other soybean traders and a CBOT clerk, in a case the court described as the result of a sting operation by the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) in the CBOT soybean futures pit; the indictment charged hundreds of violations, including mail and wire fraud and breaches of the Commodity Exchange Act, and that trader was convicted after a three-month jury trial on four Commodity Exchange Act counts and one wire fraud count ([US Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, Ryan v. CFTC, 21 May 1998](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/us-7th-circuit/1011365.html)). A concurring opinion in that appeal recalls that in the criminal case, United States v. Ashman (1992), the same court "rejected the government's argument that open outcry trading in and of itself implicated a property right that was protected under the mail and wire fraud statutes" ([Seventh Circuit, 21 May 1998](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/us-7th-circuit/1011365.html)).

### The pits close

Chicago moved to screens alongside the floor rather than all at once. CME's Globex electronic platform began trading at 6 p.m. Chicago time on 25 June 1992, and its first night's volume was 1,893 Deutsche mark and Japanese yen futures and options contracts; on 9 September 1997 it launched E-mini S&P 500 futures, which "successfully combined open outcry and electronic trading" in the exchange's words ([CME, 25 June 2002](https://mondovisione.com/media-and-resources/news/cme-celebrates-10th-anniversary-of-globexsup-sup-electronic-trading-on-heels-of--2011124/)). By February 2015 open-outcry futures trading had fallen to about 1% of CME Group's futures volume, and the company announced that most futures pits in Chicago and New York would close by 2 July 2015, keeping the S&P 500 futures pit and most options pits ([CME Group, 4 February 2015](https://www.cmegroup.com/media-room/press-releases/2015/2/04/cme_group_to_closemostopenoutcryfuturestradinginchicagoandnewyor.html)). The pits closed in March 2020 because of the pandemic, and on 4 May 2021 CME Group said it would not reopen them, except the Eurodollar options pit, which had reopened in August 2020 ([CME Group, 4 May 2021](https://www.cmegroup.com/media-room/press-releases/2021/5/04/cme_group_to_permanentlyclosemostopenoutcrytradingpitseurodollar.html)).

## London: jobbers, brokers and the Big Bang

### Coffee houses to Capel Court

The London Stock Exchange dates its history to 1698, when John Castaing published a list of currency, stock and commodity prices at Jonathan's Coffee House; in 1773 a more formal club, "New Jonathan's" or "The Stock Exchange", opened in Sweeting's Alley, and in 1802 a new building went up at Capel Court ([London Stock Exchange, Our History, undated](https://www.londonstockexchange.com/discover/lseg/our-history)). Its group dates the first regulated exchange in London to 1801 ([London Stock Exchange Group, history, undated](https://www.lseg.com/en/about-us/history)). The first rule book, of 1812, covered matters from trade settlement to defaults, and forbade setting off fireworks on the premises ([London Stock Exchange, undated](https://www.londonstockexchange.com/discover/lseg/our-history)).

Technology shortened distances long before screens. The exchange adopted the telegraph in 1840, and by 1866 prices from the floor in New York reached London in 20 minutes instead of 16 days; the ticker arrived in 1872 and the telephone in 1880, and in 1970 the Market Price Display System gave brokers real-time prices for more than 700 leading securities ([London Stock Exchange, undated](https://www.londonstockexchange.com/discover/lseg/our-history)).

### Single capacity

Until 1986 the London floor ran on a strict separation of roles. In December 1985 the Bank of England described an exchange of 4,852 individual members in 209 firms, mostly partnerships: 192 broking firms acted as agents for investors, arranging deals with dealers called jobbers for a minimum commission, and the jobbers made markets on the floor, between two and twelve of them in each stock ([Bank of England, Quarterly Bulletin, December 1985](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1985/change-in-the-stock-exchange-and-regulation-of-the-city.pdf)). The jobbing system was highly concentrated: only five of the seventeen jobbing firms could generally handle deals of institutional size, and jobbers had no direct contact with non-members ([Bank of England, December 1985](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1985/change-in-the-stock-exchange-and-regulation-of-the-city.pdf)).

Information was scarce by design. Each jobber quoted a price privately to a broker who approached him, so a broker walked the floor to several jobbers to find the best price; the exchange displayed only indicative mid-prices on its TOPIC screens (Teletext Output of Price Information by Computer), and the official list, published the next day, gave deal prices without the time or size of any deal ([Bank of England, December 1985](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1985/change-in-the-stock-exchange-and-regulation-of-the-city.pdf)). The Bank explained the logic: limited disclosure "has always been regarded as an important protection for the jobbers and hence for the liquidity of the market", while investors were protected by the separation of capacity, because the broker's role was to get them the best available price ([Bank of England, December 1985](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1985/change-in-the-stock-exchange-and-regulation-of-the-city.pdf)).

### Pressure, a court case and a deal

Commissions on most domestic securities had followed minimum scales since 1912, and member firms could take only limited outside capital: a single non-member could hold 10% from 1969 and 29.9% from 1982 ([Bank of England, December 1985](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1985/change-in-the-stock-exchange-and-regulation-of-the-city.pdf)). The Bank of England traced the pressure for change to institutions seeking international portfolios, helped by the US Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974 and the end of British exchange controls in 1979, to technology that spread prices worldwide almost instantly, and to competition from markets such as New York, which had had negotiated commissions since May 1975 ([Bank of England, December 1985](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1985/change-in-the-stock-exchange-and-regulation-of-the-city.pdf)). The Office of Fair Trading referred the exchange's rules to the Restrictive Practices Court in 1979; in July 1983 the government exempted the exchange from the restrictive practices legislation in return for its agreement to abolish fixed minimum commissions by the end of 1986 and to change its constitution ([Bank of England, December 1985](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1985/change-in-the-stock-exchange-and-regulation-of-the-city.pdf)).

### 27 October 1986, and an empty floor

On 27 October 1986, "Big Bang" day, the exchange ended single capacity, so that firms could deal both as principals and as agents, moved from fixed to negotiable commissions, and began disseminating prices through the Stock Exchange Automated Quotations system (SEAQ), which resembled Nasdaq ([Bank of England, Quarterly Bulletin, February 1987](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1987/change-in-the-stock-exchange-and-regulation-of-the-city.pdf)). The floor emptied faster than expected; the Bank called the speed of its decline "unexpected" ([Bank of England, February 1987](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1987/change-in-the-stock-exchange-and-regulation-of-the-city.pdf)). Before that day, 28 of the firms planning to make markets had decided to keep a presence on the floor, against 19 jobbers there before; within the first few weeks as much as three quarters of business other than traded options was being done off the floor, by telephone from dealing rooms watching screens, and "by December this figure had risen to perhaps 95%" ([Bank of England, February 1987](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1987/change-in-the-stock-exchange-and-regulation-of-the-city.pdf)). The exchange itself sums up the change as "computerised trading replacing the old pit-style trading floor" ([London Stock Exchange, undated](https://www.londonstockexchange.com/discover/lseg/our-history)); in 1997 its Stock Exchange Electronic Trading Services (SETS) began matching orders automatically ([London Stock Exchange, undated](https://www.londonstockexchange.com/discover/lseg/our-history)).

## São Paulo: the pregão viva-voz of Bovespa and BM&F

### From the Bolsa Livre to Bovespa

B3, the Brazilian exchange (Brasil, Bolsa, Balcão), dates the first Brazilian stock exchange, the Bolsa Livre, to 1890, and the name Bolsa de Valores de São Paulo (Bovespa) to 1967 ([B3 Bora Investir, glossary, 23 July 2025](https://borainvestir.b3.com.br/glossario/b3-s-a)). Its museum, MUB3, calls the Bolsa de Fundos Públicos, created in 1895 by a group of São Paulo brokers, the embryo of Bovespa, and says the word bolsa came into use in 1876, when auctions held in public spaces moved indoors into what was called the "pregão da Bolsa" ([MUB3, June 2025](https://mub3.org.br/docs/Caderno%20do%20Professor%20-%20Site.pdf)). B3 dates the history of Bovespa's open-outcry floor, the pregão viva-voz, to 1895 ([B3 Bora Investir, 30 September 2025](https://borainvestir.b3.com.br/noticias/fim-do-pregao-viva-voz-faz-20-anos-e-b3-celebra-historia-com-exposicao/)).

### Chalk, gestures and the T-SCAN

The museum describes the tools of the São Paulo floor. Prices were written in chalk on a slate or wooden board in the trading room, a period it calls the Stone Age (Idade da Pedra), until the first electronic panels replaced the boards in 1970; until the end of open outcry, offers were cried out and a broker who closed a deal filled in a trade ticket (boleta) by hand so it could be recorded ([MUB3, June 2025](https://mub3.org.br/docs/Caderno%20do%20Professor%20-%20Site.pdf)). In 1965 brokerage companies (sociedades corretoras) were created, and with them the floor operators (operadores de pregão), who developed the gestures that became the trademark of the exchange ([MUB3, June 2025](https://mub3.org.br/docs/Caderno%20do%20Professor%20-%20Site.pdf)). In the 1970s a T-SCAN machine, adapted from equipment used to mark answer sheets, read each completed ticket, passed it to a computer that recorded the trade and showed it on the electronic panel, and returned the ticket confirmed ([MUB3, June 2025](https://mub3.org.br/docs/Caderno%20do%20Professor%20-%20Site.pdf)). In the 1930s a radio programme, Hora da Bolsa, carried prices live to remote regions during the session ([MUB3, June 2025](https://mub3.org.br/docs/Caderno%20do%20Professor%20-%20Site.pdf)).

### A day on the floor: 1 July 1994

B3 reconstructed the first day of trading in the new currency, the real, on 1 July 1994. André Demarco, who controlled one of the trading posts that year, told B3 that operators traded in person the shares of about 10 companies, the most liquid, which accounted for 95% of the exchange's trades; each company had its own post and a controller managing its buying and selling, and the less liquid shares were already traded on electronic systems called CATS and WINCATS ([B3, 5 July 2024](https://www.b3.com.br/pt_br/noticias/como-era-a-bolsa-de-valores-em-1994.htm)). That day Bovespa recorded 4,204 trades worth R$74,974,160; it had 280 listed companies, only 119 of which traded ([B3, 5 July 2024](https://www.b3.com.br/pt_br/noticias/como-era-a-bolsa-de-valores-em-1994.htm)).

The life of an order on that floor, as B3 and its museum describe it, can be laid out step by step ([B3, 5 July 2024](https://www.b3.com.br/pt_br/noticias/como-era-a-bolsa-de-valores-em-1994.htm); [MUB3, June 2025](https://mub3.org.br/docs/Caderno%20do%20Professor%20-%20Site.pdf); [B3, 4 April 2025](https://www.b3.com.br/pt_br/noticias/avanco-em-tecnologia.htm)):

| Step | Who | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Investor and broker | The client's order reached the brokerage firm, often by telephone |
| 2 | Floor operator | The firm passed it to its operator on the floor, who went to the post of that company's shares |
| 3 | Operators at the post | Bids and offers were cried out and signalled until two operators agreed |
| 4 | The operators | The deal was written on a trading card with the company code, quantity, seller's code, buyer's code and price |
| 5 | Exchange staff | An exchange employee checked the card and the data were entered into the system |
| 6 | Exchange systems | The trade appeared on the price panel and in the daily bulletin |

### The busiest years, and the end

B3 says the floor reached its peak in the 1990s, when Bovespa had about 1,400 floor operators and BM&F about 1,200 more ([B3, 4 April 2025](https://www.b3.com.br/pt_br/noticias/avanco-em-tecnologia.htm)). Bovespa's pregão viva-voz ended on 30 September 2005; B3 marked the twentieth anniversary in 2025 with an exhibition at its museum, and noted that the floor handled 10,000 to 15,000 trades a day, against an average of 8.4 million a day now ([B3 Bora Investir, 30 September 2025](https://borainvestir.b3.com.br/noticias/fim-do-pregao-viva-voz-faz-20-anos-e-b3-celebra-historia-com-exposicao/)).

### BM&F: a derivatives floor and its last trade

BM&F was founded in 1986, as the Bolsa Mercantil e de Futuros, by Bovespa's board to trade derivatives such as futures on commodities, currencies and gold, and in 1991 it absorbed the Bolsa de Mercadorias de São Paulo while keeping its acronym, according to MUB3 ([MUB3, Manoel Felix Cintra Neto, undated](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/iwWxkfwSiAYGAQ)). Its electronic system, introduced in 2000, carried just over 3% of contracts that year; it passed the floor in 2006 with 57.59%, reached 72.95% in 2007 after side-by-side trading let products trade on screen and on the floor at once, and by June 2009 carried over 98% of the 22 million contracts traded ([BM&FBOVESPA, 30 June 2009](https://mondovisione.com/media-and-resources/news/trading-in-the-bmandf-segment-at-bmandfbovespa-to-become-fully-electronic-as-of--2011124/)).

The exchange then closed the floor, calling the migration a response to market demand that mirrored an international trend of two decades; it offered floor traders and runners free courses and a 24-month discount on exchange fees, and planned to open the floor area to public visits ([BM&FBOVESPA, 30 June 2009](https://mondovisione.com/media-and-resources/news/trading-in-the-bmandf-segment-at-bmandfbovespa-to-become-fully-electronic-as-of--2011124/)). The last open-outcry trade took place at 5:14 p.m. on 30 June 2009: a floor trader of Bradesco's brokerage sold ten Ibovespa futures contracts expiring in August 2009, at 52,020 points, to a trader of SLW Corretora; from 1 July all trades ran on the electronic systems ([BM&FBOVESPA, 1 July 2009](https://mondovisione.com/media-and-resources/news/the-last-open-outcry-transaction-is-carried-out-at-bmandfbovespa-2011124/)). A B3 article of April 2025 dates the end of BM&F's open outcry "em meados de 2006", in mid-2006 ([B3, 4 April 2025](https://www.b3.com.br/pt_br/noticias/avanco-em-tecnologia.htm)); the exchange's announcements made at the time, quoted above, record the last trade in 2009.

## Clearing and settlement in the floor era

A trade on a floor was a promise between two members. Clearing turns it into obligations someone guarantees, today usually a clearing house acting as central counterparty; settlement delivers the securities and the money ([Banco Central do Brasil, November 2002](https://www.bcb.gov.br/content/publicacoes/ref/200211/RELESTAB2002-ref200201c3p.pdf)).

**Chicago.** The CBOT set formal rules on margin and delivery in 1865, and a clearing organization for its contracts was established in 1883, at first voluntary ([CFTC, undated](https://www.cftc.gov/About/HistoryoftheCFTC/history_precftc.html)).

**London.** The exchange's first rule book of 1812 already covered trade settlement and defaults ([London Stock Exchange, undated](https://www.londonstockexchange.com/discover/lseg/our-history)).

**New York, and the paper crunch.** Floors could trade faster than back offices could settle: in the late 1960s US brokers' back offices could not handle the rise in volume: failures to deliver securities on settlement day soared, firms that switched abruptly to computers were generally disappointed, the SEC shortened the trading day in August 1967 and early 1968 with little effect, and about 160 NYSE members failed in the period, according to Seligman (2003) as cited by the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) ([BIS, Quarterly Review, December 2007](https://www.bis.org/publ/qtrpdf/r_qt0712z.htm)). A 1973 NYSE report found that three out of ten investors had experienced lost or late-delivered securities ([BIS, December 2007](https://www.bis.org/publ/qtrpdf/r_qt0712z.htm)). The Depository Trust Company (DTC) was created in 1973 to solve the paperwork crisis, and its successor group, the Depository Trust & Clearing Corporation (DTCC), says one of its first actions was to create "the first electronic security" ([DTCC, 2023](https://www.dtcc.com/insights/2023/celebrating-dtccs-50th-anniversary)).

**São Paulo.** On the floor the trading card was the record, checked by exchange staff before entry ([B3, 5 July 2024](https://www.b3.com.br/pt_br/noticias/como-era-a-bolsa-de-valores-em-1994.htm)). MUB3 credits BM&F with creating a foreign exchange clearinghouse in 2002 and an assets clearinghouse in 2004, alongside its derivatives clearinghouse ([MUB3, undated](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/iwWxkfwSiAYGAQ)). By November 2002 the Banco Central do Brasil listed BM&F's derivatives and foreign exchange systems, the Companhia Brasileira de Liquidação e Custódia (CBLC) for equities and the Central de Custódia e de Liquidação Financeira de Títulos (Cetip) for private fixed income among the country's settlement systems; systemically important clearing houses had to act as central counterparty, and equity trades on an exchange could settle up to three business days after the trade ([Banco Central do Brasil, November 2002](https://www.bcb.gov.br/content/publicacoes/ref/200211/RELESTAB2002-ref200201c3p.pdf)).

## Why floors were trusted

**A word that bound.** The London Stock Exchange took the motto Dictum Meum Pactum, "My Word is My Bond", with its coat of arms in 1923 ([London Stock Exchange, undated](https://www.londonstockexchange.com/discover/lseg/our-history)), and its group says "Our word is our bond. True in 1801" ([London Stock Exchange Group, undated](https://www.lseg.com/en/about-us/history)). In the pits, StoneX writes, "a trader's action or word is a bond" ([StoneX, undated](https://futures.stonex.com/futures-options-101/trading-pit)). 
**Rules written and enforced by members.** The NYSE's 1817 constitution fined disorderly brokers ([NYSE, undated](https://www.nyse.com/history-of-nyse)); the CBOT's 1859 charter gave it authority over its members and its 1868 rule banned corners ([CFTC, undated](https://www.cftc.gov/About/HistoryoftheCFTC/history_precftc.html)). Public regulators came later: Congress created the SEC in 1934 to supplement the NYSE's own self-regulation after the 1929 crash ([NYSE, undated](https://www.nyse.com/history-of-nyse)), and the CFTC in 1974 ([CFTC, the 1970s, undated](https://www.cftc.gov/About/HistoryoftheCFTC/history_1970s.html)).

**Obligations attached to privileges.** Specialists had an affirmative duty to help keep the market fair and orderly ([SEC, 24 October 2008](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2008-10-29/pdf/E8-25797.pdf)); London's brokers were there to get investors the best price, which the separation of capacity was meant to guarantee ([Bank of England, December 1985](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1985/change-in-the-stock-exchange-and-regulation-of-the-city.pdf)).

**Evidence that people added value.** Comparing similar stocks on the automated Paris Bourse and the floor-based NYSE, Kumar Venkataraman found execution costs higher in Paris, by 14 basis points after controlling for adverse selection, tick size and other differences, and concluded that the automated system "may not be able to fully replicate the benefits of human intermediation on a trading floor" ([Venkataraman, Journal of Finance vol. 56 no. 4, 2001](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:56:y:2001:i:4:p:1445-1485); [Southern Methodist University Scholar repository, abstract](https://scholar.smu.edu/business_finance_research/22)). In 2020 the NYSE said its data showed the floor gives investors <Claim status="attributed" by="by the NYSE">the highest level of market quality</Claim> ([NYSE, undated](https://www.nyse.com/history-of-nyse)).

## What floors cost

**Fixed commissions.** The Buttonwood brokers pledged a minimum commission of a quarter of one per cent ([Eisenstadt, October 1994](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/prospects/article/how-the-buttonwood-tree-grew-the-making-of-a-new-york-stock-exchange-legend/68D4F4577C720CFC004C6BB6076D46F6)); London kept minimum commission scales from 1912 to 1986 ([Bank of England, December 1985](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1985/change-in-the-stock-exchange-and-regulation-of-the-city.pdf)). After Big Bang the Bank of England found that commissions on equity deals of £100,000 to £1 million had fallen from about 0.4% to 0.2% (the scale rates had been higher still, 0.58% on £100,000 and 0.34% on £1 million, before concessions), that rates of 0.125% could be had on the largest deals, and that for a private investor buying £1,000 of shares the usual rate stayed at 1.65%, with some firms cutting to 1.5% or 1% ([Bank of England, February 1987](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1987/change-in-the-stock-exchange-and-regulation-of-the-city.pdf)).

**The spread.** The Bank of England's comparison of jobbers' quotes in July 1986 with the SEAQ screens after Big Bang showed the market touch, the gap between the best bid and the best offer, narrowing for beta and gamma stocks, with a less clear picture for the most liquid alpha stocks, and it warned that comparisons across the two structures were "rather hazardous" ([Bank of England, February 1987](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1987/change-in-the-stock-exchange-and-regulation-of-the-city.pdf)):

| Average touch, % of share price | Alpha stocks | Beta stocks | Gamma stocks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before Big Bang, normal market size (survey of July 1986) | 0.8 | 1.8 | 3.4 |
| After Big Bang, 1,000 shares on SEAQ | 0.6 | 1.4 | 2.8 |
| After Big Bang, largest SEAQ quote | 0.8 | 1.7 | 3.2 |

**The price of a seat.** Access was limited and could be expensive. NYSE seats sold for as little as $4,000 in the late 1860s and early 1870s, reached $625,000 in 1929, sold for $250,000 in 1990 and hit a record $4 million on 1 December 2005, when 1,366 seat holders were about to become shareholders of the publicly traded NYSE Group ([NYSE, 30 December 2005](https://mondovisione.com/media-and-resources/news/new-york-stock-exchange-ends-member-seat-sales-today-trading-license-auction-occ-2011124/); [NYSE, 2 December 2005](https://mondovisione.com/news/nyse-seat-sells-for-4000000matches-record-sale-2011124/)).

**Unequal information.** Those on the floor saw what others could not. The SEC noted that the NYSE's systems gave specialists an order-by-order advance "look" at incoming orders under the Hybrid Market, a look the 2008 reform withdrew ([SEC, 24 October 2008](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2008-10-29/pdf/E8-25797.pdf)); the specialist firms settled with the SEC in 2004 over trading ahead of their customers ([SEC, 30 March 2004](https://www.sec.gov/news/press/2004-42.htm)); in London individual jobbers' quotes were not disseminated at all ([Bank of England, December 1985](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1985/change-in-the-stock-exchange-and-regulation-of-the-city.pdf)); and in Chicago the FBI investigation of the soybean pit ended in convictions ([Seventh Circuit, 21 May 1998](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/us-7th-circuit/1011365.html)).

**Speed and capacity.** In January 2005 the NYSE's average execution time for small marketable orders was 10.1 seconds; in October 2009 it was 0.7 seconds ([SEC, 21 January 2010](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-01-21/pdf/2010-1045.pdf)). Bovespa's floor handled 10,000 to 15,000 trades a day ([B3 Bora Investir, 30 September 2025](https://borainvestir.b3.com.br/noticias/fim-do-pregao-viva-voz-faz-20-anos-e-b3-celebra-historia-com-exposicao/)). The paper crunch of the late 1960s showed what happened when trading outran the processing behind it ([BIS, December 2007](https://www.bis.org/publ/qtrpdf/r_qt0712z.htm)).

**Not every cost fell with the floor.** Terrence Hendershott and Pamela Moulton found that the NYSE's Hybrid Market, introduced at the end of 2006, cut the execution time for market orders from 10 seconds to under one second but raised bid-ask spreads because of increased adverse selection, while making prices more efficient ([Hendershott and Moulton, Journal of Financial Markets vol. 14 no. 4, November 2011](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finmar/v14y2011i4p568-604.html)). The study thus measured two costs moving in opposite directions.

## What to remember

- A floor was a members' auction: interest gathered at a post or pit, prices were found by voice and gesture, and every deal was written down and checked.
- The Buttonwood Agreement of 1792 fixed a minimum commission of a quarter per cent and a preference among its signers; the story of its founding role is disputed by historians.
- Specialists at the NYSE combined broker and dealer roles under obligations to keep markets orderly, and that privilege was abused, as the SEC's 2004 settlement shows.
- London's single capacity separated brokers from jobbers and kept quotes private until Big Bang on 27 October 1986, after which the floor emptied within weeks.
- Bovespa's pregão viva-voz closed on 30 September 2005 and BM&F's on 30 June 2009, according to the exchange's own records; a 2025 B3 article gives a different date for BM&F.
- Floors were trusted because members knew each other and wrote and enforced their own rules; they cost fixed commissions, expensive access, unequal information and limited speed.

- When the trust of a floor rested on members knowing one another, what took its place once counterparties became anonymous lines in an order book?

- Hand signals kept large orders anonymous on the floor; are today's protections for large orders better or worse than a broker's closed fist?
- The NYSE kept a floor after it had the technology to close it: is that a tradition, an advantage for some members, or a feature that adds market quality, and what evidence would tell?
- Brazil's floor operators were offered courses and fee discounts when their floor closed: what should an exchange owe the people whose work its technology replaces?
