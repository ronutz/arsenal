For most of history a familiar voice on the telephone was a reasonable proof of identity, and a face on a video call an even better one. Generative artificial intelligence (AI) has weakened both. A fraudster can now ask for money in the voice of a chief executive officer (CEO), a grandson or a defence minister, or sit an employee in front of a video conference whose other participants never existed. After [Business email compromise](/learn/business-email-compromise-and-impersonation), which forges the written request, this part of the series opened by [the map from the small lie to the campaign](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign) turns to the forged voice and face. After reading, you can explain how such media are made without a recipe, weigh what each famous case actually establishes, describe what regulators in the United States (US), the European Union (EU) and Brazil did, read the detection research with its limits, and choose defences that do not depend on anyone's ears or eyes.

## What the words mean

The EU's Artificial Intelligence Act defines a deep fake as "AI-generated or manipulated image, audio or video content that resembles existing persons, objects, places, entities or events and would falsely appear to a person to be authentic or truthful" ([Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 3(60), 13 June 2024](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202401689)). The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) of the US Treasury uses a wider definition, synthetic content "to create realistic but inauthentic videos, pictures, audio, and text", and adds, citing the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), that "the threat of deepfakes and synthetic media comes not from the technology used to create it, but from people's natural inclination to believe what they see" ([FinCEN, FIN-2024-Alert004, 13 November 2024, note 2](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/shared/FinCEN-Alert-DeepFakes-Alert508FINAL.pdf)). A voice clone is the audio case: the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) calls cloned voices "'artificial' voice messages because a person is not speaking them" ([FCC 24-17, Declaratory Ruling, released 8 February 2024](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-24-17A1.pdf)).

A warning by the Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) of the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) makes a distinction worth keeping: "The creation or distribution of synthetic content is not inherently illegal; however, synthetic content can be used to facilitate crimes, such as fraud and extortion" ([FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center, I-120324-PSA, 3 December 2024](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2024/PSA241203)). Dubbing, accessibility tools and satire use the same technology. This part is about its use to deceive for money.

## How they are made, as makers and researchers describe it

This section explains the principle, as the developers and researchers themselves publish it, not a method.

**Voices from seconds of audio.** In January 2023 Microsoft researchers led by Chengyi Wang described VALL-E, a text-to-speech model trained on 60,000 hours of English speech that "can be used to synthesize high-quality personalized speech with only a 3-second enrolled recording of an unseen speaker as an acoustic prompt", and warned in the same paper of "potential risks in misuse of the model, such as spoofing voice identification or impersonating" a specific speaker ([Wang et al., VALL-E, arXiv 2301.02111](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.02111)). For its successor, VALL-E 2, Microsoft states that "Currently, we have no plans to incorporate VALL-E 2 into a product or expand access to the public", and that any real-world use "should include a protocol to ensure that the speaker approves the use of their voice and a synthesized speech detection model" ([Microsoft Research, VALL-E 2 project page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/project/vall-e-x/vall-e-2/)). Others did release tools. In 2019 the start-up Lyrebird let anyone create a "vocal avatar" from "at least a minute of real-world speech", according to The Washington Post ([The Washington Post, via The Herald, 4 September 2019](https://www.heraldnet.com/?p=675494)). For criminals the input is easy to find: the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) warned in 2023 that a scammer needs only "a short audio clip of your family member's voice", which "he could get from content posted online" ([FTC, consumer alert, 20 March 2023, read through WebFetch](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2023/03/scammers-use-ai-enhance-their-family-emergency-schemes)).

**Safeguards and their gaps.** FinCEN notes that leading developers committed to controls against malicious deepfakes, but that "some AI tools are open-source, allowing any user to access and modify the tools' code and potentially circumvent controls" ([FinCEN, 2024](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/shared/FinCEN-Alert-DeepFakes-Alert508FINAL.pdf)). In March 2025 Consumer Reports, in a report summarized by its policy analyst Grace Gedye, examined voice cloning products from six companies, Descript, ElevenLabs, Lovo, PlayHT, Resemble AI and Speechify, and found that <Claim status="reported" by="by Consumer Reports">"four fail to take basic steps to stop unauthorized voice cloning"</Claim>; it called for a technical mechanism to confirm the speaker's consent ([Consumer Reports, 10 March 2025](https://innovation.consumerreports.org/new-report-do-these-6-ai-voice-cloning-companies-do-enough-to-prevent-misuse/)). The companies' replies are not reproduced in the summary read here.

**Faces on video.** Two ways of producing a fake video matter for fraud. A recording can be prepared in advance from public footage and played into a call; the Hong Kong police said this is what happened in the 2024 case below. Or software can animate a face in real time, a "deepfake puppet" in the words of the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA), which listed the deepfake attack beside photo, video replay and three-dimensional mask attacks on remote identity checks ([ENISA, Remote Identity Proofing: Attacks & Countermeasures, January 2022](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20Remote%20Identity%20Proofing%20-%20Attacks%20%26%20Countermeasures.pdf)). FinCEN's summary of the trend is that generative tools "have greatly reduced the resources required to produce high-quality synthetic content" ([FinCEN, 2024](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/shared/FinCEN-Alert-DeepFakes-Alert508FINAL.pdf)).

## The documented cases, and what each source establishes

### A British energy company, 2019: the insurer's account

The case usually called the first AI voice heist rests on one source, the victim company's insurer. Representatives of Euler Hermes told The Washington Post, after The Wall Street Journal first reported the case, that the managing director of a British energy company, a subsidiary of a German energy firm, "believing his boss was on the phone", wired more than $240,000 to an account in Hungary one Friday afternoon in March of that year; the money, 220,000 euros, went through accounts in Hungary and Mexico and disappeared. An insurer spokeswoman said: "The software was able to imitate the voice, and not only the voice: the tonality, the punctuation, the German accent". The same report states that the call "was not recorded" and that "The insurer did not know which software was used". The fraud unravelled when a second request made the director suspicious: he called his boss directly, and the fake "Johannes" called while he was still on the line with the real one ([The Washington Post, via The Herald, 4 September 2019](https://www.heraldnet.com/?p=675494)). The company was not named and no suspect was identified. <Claim status="reported" by="by the insurer, Euler Hermes">That the voice was synthesized by AI</Claim> is therefore the insurer's account, without a recording or a forensic finding behind it in the sources.

### Hong Kong, 2024: a video conference with no one in it

In February 2024 the Hong Kong police announced that a multinational company had lost about HK$200 million (US$26 million) in 15 transfers after an employee received "video conference calls from someone posing as senior officers of the company requesting to transfer money to designated bank accounts"; Acting Senior Superintendent Baron Chan said that all the participants except the victim were impersonated, that "Scammers found publicly available video and audio of the impersonation targets via YouTube", and that the videos "were pre-recorded and did not involve dialogue or interaction with the victim" ([Hong Kong Free Press (Agence France-Presse), 5 February 2024](https://hongkongfp.com/2024/02/05/multinational-loses-hk200-million-to-deepfake-video-conference-scam-hong-kong-police-say/)).

The government's written answer to the Legislative Council in June 2024 gives the official sequence. The employee "received a phishing email from a fraudster, who impersonated the Chief Financial Officer of the informant's head office in the United Kingdom (UK)", joined a group video conference and authorized transfers to five local bank accounts; the police "believed that the pre-recorded video conference was generated using downloaded online public video clips and voices of the impersonated officer", and after giving instructions the fraudster ended the meeting under a pretext and continued over instant messaging ([Hong Kong Government, LCQ9: Combating frauds involving deepfake, 26 June 2024](https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202406/26/P2024062600192.htm)). In May 2024 the British engineering firm Arup confirmed to Cable News Network (CNN) that it was the company and that "fake voices and images were used", adding that "none of our internal systems were compromised" ([CNN, via ABC17, 16 May 2024](https://abc17news.com/money/cnn-business-consumer/2024/05/16/british-engineering-giant-arup-revealed-as-25-million-deepfake-scam-victim/)). The case was still under investigation in June 2024, and no arrest appears in the sources.

The same answer describes two other cases. In May 2024 an employee of a multinational trading company joined a video conference of nearly 30 minutes with a "fake boss" and was told to transfer nearly HK$4 million. In August 2023 an operation code-named Smashscam broke up a syndicate <Claim status="alleged" by="by the Hong Kong police">suspected of using an AI face-swapping programme to apply for online loans with stolen identities, involving HK$200,000</Claim>, with nine arrests for conspiracy to defraud ([Hong Kong Government, 26 June 2024](https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202406/26/P2024062600192.htm)). The government also reported 21 deepfake video clips impersonating officials or celebrities identified between November 2023 and May 2024, all removed at the police's request, and said that "So far, the Police have not received any reports involving members of the public being defrauded as a direct result of these deepfake video clips".

### The family emergency and the virtual kidnapping

The FTC's 2023 alert described the oldest pattern with a new tool: a call in a loved one's voice, in trouble and asking for money, sent by wire, cryptocurrency or gift cards ([FTC, 2023](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2023/03/scammers-use-ai-enhance-their-family-emergency-schemes)). The FBI warned in December 2024 that "Criminals generate short audio clips containing a loved one's voice to impersonate a close relative in a crisis situation, asking for immediate financial assistance or demanding a ransom" ([IC3, I-120324-PSA, 2024](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2024/PSA241203)). In December 2025 it described "virtual kidnapping" scams in which criminals send altered photos or videos as proof of life, which on close inspection show "inaccuracies when compared to confirmed photos of the loved one" such as missing tattoos or scars, sometimes through timed messages that limit how long the victim can look ([IC3, I-120525-PSA, 5 December 2025](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2025/PSA251205)).

The IC3's 2025 report put a number on part of this: victims claimed "losses over $5 million in 2025 to distress scams" using voice cloning, a type of scam "evolving to mimic other family members or close friends in different types of emergency scenarios" ([IC3, 2025 Annual Report, April 2026](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf)). That figure counts complaints in which an AI link was reported, which depends on victims knowing or suspecting it.

### Officials and executives on the line

In February 2025 Italian prosecutors said that fraudsters had used AI to imitate the voice of the defence minister, Guido Crosetto, in calls to prominent business figures asking for urgent help to free kidnapped Italian journalists; the calls appeared to come from government offices in Rome, and one businessman sent nearly one million euros, which the police later traced to the Netherlands and froze, according to Reuters ([Cyprus Mail (Reuters), 12 February 2025](https://cyprus-mail.com/2025/02/12/italian-police-freeze-cash-from-ai-voice-scam-that-targeted-business-leaders)). In the US the FBI reported in 2025 that actors had impersonated senior officials in AI-generated voice messages, a campaign described in [Social engineering](/learn/social-engineering-pretexting-vishing-and-baiting). In companies, the IC3 notes that "Voice cloning can also be used to request wire payment", and that businesses reported over $30 million in losses to business email compromise (BEC) scams involving AI in 2025 ([IC3, 2025 Annual Report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf)). In Brazil, a task force of São Paulo's Polícia Civil and the bar association said in May 2026 that a group running the golpe do falso advogado, the fake lawyer scam, in some cases used AI "para reproduzir a voz real dos advogados", to reproduce the real lawyers' voices ([Diário do Grande ABC, 19 May 2026](https://www.dgabc.com.br/Noticia/4319978/golpe-do-falso-advogado-operacao-com-oab-sp-revela-esquema-de-reproducao-de-vozes-por-ia)).

### Famous faces in advertisements

The largest losses linked to AI in the IC3's data are in investment fraud: "Investment clubs employ AI-generated videos and voices of celebrities, CEOs, or trusted figures", and losses in investment complaints with a reported AI link "surpassed $632 million" in 2025, against more than $8 billion in investment losses overall, which the IC3 reads as a sign that "many victims do not realize the extent AI may be involved in scams" ([IC3, 2025 Annual Report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf)).

Brazil has several police cases. In October 2025 the Polícia Civil of Rio Grande do Sul carried out Operação Modo Selva, with 26 court orders in five states, against a group that <Claim status="alleged" by="by the police">used deepfakes of the model Gisele Bündchen and the presenters Angélica, Juliette, Maísa and Sabrina Sato in social media advertisements for non-existent products such as a free anti-wrinkle kit, collecting "shipping fees" of R$ 20 to R$ 100 by Pix and moving more than R$ 20 million</Claim>; up to R$ 210 million in assets and accounts were blocked, according to the SBT News report ([SBT News, 1 October 2025](https://sbtnews.sbt.com.br/noticia/policia/policia-do-rs-mira-quadrilha-que-usava-deepfake-de-gisele-buendchen-para-aplicar-golpes)). The Polícia Civil do Distrito Federal had described a similar scheme in January 2025 using the face and voice of the presenter Marcos Mion, examined in [Scams at scale](/learn/scams-at-scale-why-a-tiny-success-rate-pays). In July 2025 the Advocacia-Geral da União (AGU), the federal government's legal service, obtained the removal from Facebook of AI-made videos in which manipulated statements by the Banco Central's governor, Gabriel Galípolo, and its international affairs director, Paulo Picchetti, announced a fictitious "compensação financeira" for Pix users, meant to lead viewers to a site that captured personal data; the AGU's notice argued that platforms can be held liable for fraudulent third-party content, "especialmente após decisão recente do Supremo Tribunal Federal (STF) sobre o Marco Civil da Internet" ([AGU, 23 July 2025](https://www.gov.br/agu/pt-br/comunicacao/noticias/facebook-remove-videos-sobre-pix-manipulados-por-ia-apos-notificacao-da-agu)). None of the suspects in these cases is named in the sources and no response from them appears.

### An election robocall, and what the courts did with it

The case that moved US regulators was political, not financial. Two days before the New Hampshire presidential primary of January 2024, thousands of robocalls carried a cloned voice of President Joseph R. Biden, Jr. telling Democrats to "save your vote for the November election" ([FCC, news release, 23 May 2024](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-402762A1.pdf)). The FCC imposed a $6,000,000 forfeiture on the political consultant Steve Kramer under the Truth in Caller ID Act for spoofing a local political operative's number; Kramer did not respond to the notice of liability, and Commissioner Simington dissented ([FCC 24-104, Forfeiture Order, released 30 September 2024](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-24-104A1_Rcd.pdf)). In June 2025 a New Hampshire jury acquitted Kramer of 11 felony voter suppression charges and 11 charges of impersonating a candidate; he had testified that he wanted to send "a wake-up call" about AI, his defence argued that the unsanctioned primary was outside the voter suppression law, and he told The Associated Press that he would not pay the FCC fine; Lingo Telecom, which transmitted the calls, had agreed to pay $1 million ([WCAX (The Associated Press), 17 June 2025](https://www.wcax.com/2025/06/17/new-hampshire-jury-acquits-consultant-behind-ai-robocalls-mimicking-biden-all-charges/)).

## What regulators did

### United States: the FCC and the FTC

The FCC's declaratory ruling, adopted on 2 February 2024, confirmed "that the TCPA's restrictions on the use of 'artificial or prerecorded voice' encompass current AI technologies that generate human voices", the TCPA being the Telephone Consumer Protection Act of 1991, so that such calls "require the prior express consent of the called party" absent an emergency purpose or exemption ([FCC 24-17](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-24-17A1.pdf)). The ruling turns an existing robocall ban into a tool against cloned voices, but it governs calls to consumers, not a fraudster who phones one victim in person.

The FTC's Rule on Impersonation of Government and Businesses took effect on 1 April 2024 ([FTC, final rule, Federal Register, 1 March 2024](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-03-01/pdf/2024-04335.pdf)). A supplemental notice of proposed rulemaking (SNPRM) published the same day would have extended it to the impersonation of individuals and to parties who provide goods or services "with knowledge or reason to know that those goods or services will be used" in impersonation, and cited voice cloning among its reasons ([FTC, supplemental notice of proposed rulemaking, 1 March 2024](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-03-01/pdf/2024-03793.pdf)). In December 2024 the Commission said it "has decided not to proceed with the SNPRM's proposed means and instrumentalities provision at this time", after commenters called it overbroad; on individuals it "held an informal hearing in January 2025 and is still evaluating its options", it wrote in an advance notice scheduled for publication on 1 October 2026, which opens a new rulemaking on how search, social media and other platforms' advertising practices may further impersonation scams ([FTC, advance notice of proposed rulemaking, public inspection copy, scheduled for 1 October 2026](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-20143.pdf)).

The FTC also ran a Voice Cloning Challenge. Announcing its four winners in April 2024, its Office of Technology wrote that there is "no silver bullet to prevent the harms posed by voice cloning", that "fraudsters may be able to remove a watermark", that studies show "a spectrum of efficacy for voice cloning detection solutions", and that wrongly marking a cloned voice as authentic "could be particularly harmful" in settings such as banking ([FTC, Approaches to Address AI-enabled Voice Cloning, 8 April 2024, read through WebFetch](https://www.ftc.gov/policy/advocacy-research/tech-at-ftc/2024/04/approaches-address-ai-enabled-voice-cloning)).

### European Union: the AI Act's transparency duties

Article 50 of the AI Act places two duties relevant here. Providers of systems that generate synthetic audio, images, video or text "shall ensure that the outputs of the AI system are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated", as far as technically feasible; deployers of a system that generates or manipulates content "constituting a deep fake, shall disclose that the content has been artificially generated or manipulated", with lighter disclosure for evidently artistic, satirical or fictional work and an exemption where the use is authorized by law to fight crime ([Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 50](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202401689)). The regulation applies generally from 2 August 2026. On 30 June 2026 the Council gave final approval to a simplifying amendment, the so-called omnibus, which sets 2 December 2026 as the deadline for providers to implement their transparency solutions for AI-generated content and adds a prohibition on AI systems that generate non-consensual sexual and intimate content or child sexual abuse material ([Council of the EU, via the EU Delegation to Chile, 30 June 2026](https://www.eeas.europa.eu/delegations/chile/artificial-intelligence-council-gives-final-green-light-simplify-and-streamline-rules_en)). On 10 June 2026 the Commission published a voluntary Code of Practice on marking and labelling AI-generated content ([European Commission, 10 June 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-publishes-code-practice-marking-and-labelling-ai-generated-content)). A labelling duty binds those who obey the law; a fraudster's deep fake is already illegal as fraud.

### Brazil: the electoral court and Congress

Brazil's Tribunal Superior Eleitoral (TSE) regulates electoral propaganda by resolution. Since 2024, its Resolution 23.610/2019 has required a label on synthetic multimedia content and, in article 9º-C, paragraph 1, banned deep fakes, defined as "conteúdo sintético em formato de áudio, vídeo ou combinação de ambos, que tenha sido gerado ou manipulado digitalmente, ainda que mediante autorização, para criar, substituir ou alterar imagem ou voz de pessoa viva, falecida ou fictícia", synthetic audio or video created or manipulated, even with authorization, to create, replace or alter the image or voice of a living, dead or fictitious person, to harm or favour candidacies ([ARTIGO 19, Aláfia Lab and IRIS, technical note on Resolution 23.755/2026, March 2026](https://artigo19.org/wp-content/blogs.dir/24/files/2026/03/NOTA_TSE2026-VF.pdf)). For the general election of October 2026, Resolution 23.755/2026 added a ban on publishing, republishing or boosting new AI-made content using the image, voice or expression of candidates or public persons "mesmo que rotulados", even if labelled, from 72 hours before to 24 hours after the vote, and an article 9º-I allowing the burden of proof to be inverted in cases about synthetic content.

The same civil society note, by ARTIGO 19, Aláfia Lab and the Instituto de Referência em Internet e Sociedade (IRIS), welcomes parts of the resolution and criticizes others. It argues that the 72-hour rule may forbid "algo que já seria proibido a qualquer momento", something already forbidden at any time, that the electoral courts have not settled what counts as a deep fake, and that the burden-of-proof inversion "pode vir a ser maliciosamente utilizada para contestar conteúdo 'orgânico' (não sintético) e verdadeiro", could be used maliciously to challenge authentic content, in situations of "prova impossível", impossible proof ([ARTIGO 19 and others, 2026](https://artigo19.org/wp-content/blogs.dir/24/files/2026/03/NOTA_TSE2026-VF.pdf)). The TSE's own pages could not be read for this part; the resolution's text above is quoted as reproduced in the note.

In criminal law, Lei 15.123 of 2025, from the projeto de lei (bill) PL 370/2024, increases by half the penalty for psychological violence against women when the crime is committed with AI or any technology that alters the victim's image or voice, according to the Senate's news agency ([Agência Senado, 23 July 2025](https://www12.senado.leg.br/noticias/materias/2025/07/23/aprovadas-no-senado-novas-leis-oferecem-mais-protecao-e-saude-para-mulheres)). For fraud, the police cases above are prosecuted as estelionato, the fraud offence of the Penal Code, as the investigators' statements in the [business email compromise](/learn/business-email-compromise-and-impersonation) part show.

### Hong Kong

The Hong Kong government told its legislature in 2024 that most laws against crime offline apply online and that a Law Reform Commission subcommittee would study cyber-enabled crime, while the police kept separate deepfake statistics, asked platforms to remove fake videos and exchanged intelligence with the International Criminal Police Organization (INTERPOL) and the AI industry ([Hong Kong Government, 26 June 2024](https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202406/26/P2024062600192.htm)).

## Detection research, and its limits

### People

Three peer-reviewed experiments point the same way. Kimberly Mai and colleagues at University College London played genuine and deepfake speech to 529 listeners in English and Mandarin: "Listeners only correctly spotted the deepfakes 73% of the time", providing examples of speech deepfakes "only improves results slightly", and the authors expect detection "to become harder" as synthesis improves ([Mai et al., PLOS ONE, 2 August 2023](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10174794/1/journal.pone.0285333.pdf)). Sarah Barrington, Emily Cooper and Hany Farid, of the University of California, Berkeley, cloned more than 200 speakers with a commercial service and tested 604 participants: they "perceived the identity of an AI-generated voice to be the same as its real counterpart approximately 80% of the time, and correctly identified a voice as AI generated only about 60% of the time" ([Barrington, Cooper and Farid, Scientific Reports 15, 2025](https://escholarship.org/content/qt8wc5d5q8/qt8wc5d5q8.pdf)). For video, Nils Köbis, Barbora Doležalová and Ivan Soraperra found in a pre-registered experiment with 210 people that "people cannot reliably detect deepfakes", that "neither raising awareness nor introducing financial incentives improves their detection accuracy", and that people lean towards taking deepfakes for authentic while overestimating their own skill ([Köbis et al., iScience, November 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8602050/)).

These results sit uneasily with official advice. The FBI's 2024 tips include looking for "subtle imperfections in images and videos" and listening "closely to the tone and word choice" ([IC3, 2024](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2024/PSA241203)); the Hong Kong government said that deepfake technology "is not infallible" and suggested asking the person to turn their head ([Hong Kong Government, 2024](https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202406/26/P2024062600192.htm)). The studies measured short clips heard or seen without context, not a live call with a chance to ask questions; they do not show that such checks never work, but they give no basis for relying on them alone.

### Machines

Automatic detectors do better in the laboratory than outside it. Nicolas Müller and colleagues, researchers at Fraunhofer, the Technical University of Munich and a private company, re-implemented twelve audio deepfake detectors and tested them on 37.9 hours of recordings of 58 politicians and celebrities collected from the internet, 17.2 hours of them deepfakes: they found "performance degradation of up to one thousand percent", which, they wrote, "could suggest that the community has tailored its solutions too closely to the prevailing ASVspoof benchmark" ([Müller et al., Does Audio Deepfake Detection Generalize?, arXiv 2203.16263](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.16263)). In the Deepfake Detection Challenge of 2020, whose dataset was built by Facebook AI, 2,114 teams took part; on the private test set only 60% of submissions had a log loss at or below 0.69, "roughly the score if one were to predict a probability of 0.5 for every video", the authors noted that "many submissions were simply random", and the best models reached an average precision of 0.753 on real videos ([Dolhansky et al., The DeepFake Detection Challenge Dataset, arXiv 2006.07397](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.07397)).

Watermarking, the approach Article 50 relies on, has its own critics. AudioMarkBench, a benchmark presented at the Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS) conference in 2024, tested three audio watermarking methods against 15 kinds of perturbation and reported findings that "highlight the vulnerabilities of current watermarking techniques" to removal and forgery ([Liu et al., AudioMarkBench, arXiv 2406.06979](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06979)). For images, Mehrdad Saberi and colleagues showed that a "diffusion purification" attack removes low-perturbation watermarks and that watermarking methods "are vulnerable to spoofing attacks where the attacker aims to have real images identified as watermarked ones" ([Saberi et al., arXiv 2310.00076](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.00076)).

### The liar's dividend

The legal scholars Robert Chesney and Danielle Citron argued in 2019 that deep fakes "make it easier for liars to avoid accountability for things that are in fact true", a "liar's dividend" that "flows, perversely, in proportion to success in educating the public about the dangers of deep fakes" ([Chesney and Citron, California Law Review 107, December 2019](https://www.californialawreview.org/print/deep-fakes-a-looming-challenge-for-privacy-democracy-and-national-security)). The Brazilian note's worry about inverting the burden of proof is a form of the same problem: once anything may be fake, a genuine recording can be challenged as synthetic.

## Defences the authorities recommend

**Agree a code word.** The FBI advises: "Create a secret word or phrase with your family to verify their identity" ([IC3, 2024](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2024/PSA241203)), and repeated it for virtual kidnapping: "Establish a code word only you or your loved ones know that you can use to communicate" ([IC3, 2025](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2025/PSA251205)). Brazil's incident response team, CERT.br, says "combine uma 'senha' com seus conhecidos", agree a password with people you know, and test the caller's knowledge ([CERT.br, Golpes: Não se Deixe Enganar, March 2026](https://cartilha.cert.br/fasciculos/golpes/fasciculo-golpes.pdf)). A code word replaces a judgement about a voice, which the studies show is unreliable, with a check on knowledge the clone does not have.

**Call back on a number you already know.** "Don't trust the voice. Call the person who supposedly contacted you and verify the story", using "a phone number you know is theirs", says the FTC ([FTC, 2023](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2023/03/scammers-use-ai-enhance-their-family-emergency-schemes)); the FBI advises hanging up, finding the organization's contact independently and calling it directly ([IC3, 2024](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2024/PSA241203)), and, for kidnapping claims, to "Always attempt to contact your loved one before considering paying any ransom demand" ([IC3, 2025](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2025/PSA251205)). CERT.br's booklet tells readers to "Duvide até de áudios e vídeos", doubt even audio and video, and to talk to the person through a contact already known ([CERT.br, 2026](https://cartilha.cert.br/fasciculos/golpes/fasciculo-golpes.pdf)). The 2019 fraud in the UK stopped exactly this way, when the director called his real boss ([The Washington Post, 2019](https://www.heraldnet.com/?p=675494)). For companies the same rule is the payment-change procedure set out in [Business email compromise](/learn/business-email-compromise-and-impersonation): a voice or a face on a call authorizes nothing by itself.

**Slow down.** The FBI's virtual kidnapping warning notes that "Scammers portray a false sense of urgency" and asks: "Stop and think; do the kidnapper's claims make sense?" ([IC3, 2025](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2025/PSA251205)). Urgency is the lever this series has followed since [the human attack surface](/learn/the-human-attack-surface-why-repetition-works); a clone only makes the voice behind it more convincing.

**Limit the raw material, within reason.** The FBI suggests, "If possible", limiting online content of one's image or voice and making social media accounts private ([IC3, 2024](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2024/PSA241203)). For executives, officials and celebrities, whose voices are public by profession, this offers little; the Hong Kong fraudsters used public YouTube footage ([Hong Kong Free Press, 2024](https://hongkongfp.com/2024/02/05/multinational-loses-hk200-million-to-deepfake-video-conference-scam-hong-kong-police-say/)).

**Liveness checks, and where they fail.** Banks verifying new customers remotely ask for a live selfie or video. FinCEN lists multi-factor authentication (MFA), "including phishing-resistant MFA", and "live verification checks in which a customer is prompted to confirm their identity through audio or video" among the practices that help, while warning that "illicit actors may be able to respond to live verification prompts or access tools that generate synthetic audio and video responses on their behalf"; its red flags include a customer who claims repeated technical glitches or uses "third-party webcam plugins, which can let a customer display previously generated video rather than live video" ([FinCEN, 2024](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/shared/FinCEN-Alert-DeepFakes-Alert508FINAL.pdf)). ENISA wrote that checks based on movements on command are "effective against unsophisticated attackers", but that as software and hardware approach the frame rates of an average camera "other security controls need to be employed as well", such as involuntary signals, depth sensing and checks that the camera feed is real ([ENISA, 2022](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/ENISA%20Report%20-%20Remote%20Identity%20Proofing%20-%20Attacks%20%26%20Countermeasures.pdf)). Hong Kong's first face-swap case was exactly such an attack on online loan applications ([Hong Kong Government, 2024](https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/202406/26/P2024062600192.htm)). Credentials that cannot be phished, explained in [Passkeys](/learn/passkeys-and-phishing-resistant-authentication), protect accounts already opened; they do not prove who is speaking on a call.

**Report quickly.** The IC3 asks victims to file at ic3.gov with phone numbers, payment details and the messages and images received ([IC3, 2025](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2025/PSA251205)); in the Italian case the money was traced and frozen after the complaint ([Cyprus Mail (Reuters), 2025](https://cyprus-mail.com/2025/02/12/italian-police-freeze-cash-from-ai-voice-scam-that-targeted-business-leaders)).

## What to remember

- A voice clone can be built from seconds to minutes of public audio, by its makers' own account; some developers hold tools back, others release them, and Consumer Reports found four of six products without basic consent checks.
- The 2019 UK case rests on the insurer's account of an unrecorded call; the 2024 Hong Kong case rests on police statements and Arup's confirmation that fake voices and images were used in a pre-recorded conference.
- The IC3 counted 22,364 complaints with an AI link in 2025 and $893 million in losses, over $5 million of them in voice-cloned distress scams; these counts depend on victims knowing that AI was involved.
- Regulators acted on channels and labels: the FCC applied the robocall law to AI voices, the FTC banned government and business impersonation and is still weighing individuals, the EU requires marking and deep-fake disclosure from 2026, and Brazil's TSE bans electoral deep fakes and restricts AI content around the vote.
- People detect cloned voices and deepfake videos little better than chance in experiments, automatic detectors degrade outside the laboratory, and watermarks can be removed or forged.
- The defences that work do not rely on perception: a code word, a callback on a number already known, a pause against urgency, and payment procedures that no voice or face can bypass.
