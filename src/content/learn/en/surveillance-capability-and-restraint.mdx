## Capability by design: lawful interception

**United States: CALEA (1994).** The Communications Assistance for Law Enforcement Act (CALEA) requires a telecommunications carrier to ensure that its equipment and services are capable of "expeditiously isolating and enabling the government, pursuant to a court order or other lawful authorization, to intercept, to the exclusion of any other communications, all wire and electronic communications carried by the carrier" to or from a subscriber, of giving access to "call-identifying information that is reasonably available to the carrier", of delivering both to a location of the government's choosing, and of doing so "unobtrusively and with a minimum of interference with any subscriber's telecommunications service" ([47 U.S.C. 1002, United States Code, 2024 edition](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title47/html/USCODE-2024-title47-chap9-subchapI-sec1002.htm)). The same section limits the duty. It does not authorize any agency "to require any specific design of equipment, facilities, services, features, or system configurations"; the requirements "do not apply to" information services; and "A telecommunications carrier shall not be responsible for decrypting, or ensuring the government's ability to decrypt, any communication encrypted by a subscriber or customer, unless the encryption was provided by the carrier and the carrier possesses the information necessary to decrypt the communication" (same source). Information services are defined as capabilities for "generating, acquiring, storing, transforming, processing, retrieving, utilizing, or making available information via telecommunications" ([47 U.S.C. 1001](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title47/html/USCODE-2024-title47-chap9-subchapI-sec1001.htm)). A separate section requires that any interception within a carrier's switching premises "can be activated only in accordance with a court order or other lawful authorization and with the affirmative intervention of an individual officer or employee of the carrier" ([47 U.S.C. 1004](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title47/html/USCODE-2024-title47-chap9-subchapI-sec1004.htm)).

**Europe: the interception standards.** The European Telecommunications Standards Institute (ETSI) writes the technical requirements that law enforcement agencies set for communications service providers. Its specification on those requirements traces them to a 1995 resolution of the Council of the European Union (EU), adopted "With the aim of harmonising the interception policy in the member states", and defines the vocabulary used worldwide: a "Handover Interface (HI)" across which interception is requested and its results delivered, the "content of communication" (CC), and "intercept related information" (IRI), the data about a communication, which "has to be provided whether or not communication activity is taking place" ([ETSI TS 101 331 V1.7.1, Lawful Interception (LI); Requirements of Law Enforcement Agencies, March 2021](https://www.etsi.org/deliver/etsi_ts/101300_101399/101331/01.07.01_60/ts_101331v010701p.pdf)). The provider must "remove any service coding or encryption which has been applied to the content of communication (i.e. en clair)" where it applied that encryption itself. Interception must be operated so that "no unauthorized person can detect any change from the unintercepted state" and "no communicating parties can detect any change"; access to the handover interface is barred to unauthorized persons, and results go only to the receiving facility named in the warrant once its authority has been proved (same source).

**The internet's engineers decline.** In May 2000 the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF), in its Request for Comments (RFC) 2804, took the opposite position for its own standards: "The IETF has decided not to consider requirements for wiretapping as part of the process for creating and maintaining IETF standards" ([RFC 2804, IETF Policy on Wiretapping, May 2000](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2804.txt)). Its reasons: it is "the wrong forum" for features "that address needs arising from the laws of individual countries"; "adding a requirement for wiretapping will make affected protocol designs considerably more complex", and "complexity almost inevitably jeopardizes the security of communications even when it is not being tapped by any legal means"; and it "does not see an engineering solution that allows such wiretapping when the end systems take adequate measures to protect their communications". It added that mechanisms for wiretapping "should be openly described, so as to ensure the maximum review of the mechanisms". The tension between those positions is the subject of [the crypto wars](/learn/the-crypto-wars-and-the-cypherpunks).

## When the capability is turned against its owners

**Athens, 2004 and 2005.** Vassilis Prevelakis and Diomidis Spinellis reconstructed, for the magazine of the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE), IEEE Spectrum, how rogue software "used the lawful wiretapping mechanisms of Vodafone's digital switches to tap about 100 phones", among them those of the Greek prime minister, his ministers of defence, foreign affairs and justice, the mayor of Athens and the Greek European Union commissioner, from around the run-up to the August 2004 Olympic Games until its discovery on 24 January 2005 ([Prevelakis and Spinellis, The Athens Affair, IEEE Spectrum, 29 June 2007](https://spectrum.ieee.org/the-athens-affair)). The interception subsystem in the Ericsson switches, they explain, was a software component "typically used for setting up wiretaps, which only law officers are supposed to have access to"; Vodafone Greece "had not purchased the lawful intercept option" and its interception management software was not installed, but a 2003 software upgrade had nonetheless placed the interception code in the switches. "We still don't know who committed this crime", they wrote (same source).

**Salt Typhoon, 2024.** A joint statement by the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) reported that actors affiliated with the People's Republic of China (PRC) had compromised "networks at multiple telecommunications companies", enabling "the theft of customer call records data", the compromise of "private communications of a limited number of individuals" and the copying of "certain information that was subject to U.S. law enforcement requests pursuant to court orders" ([FBI and CISA, joint statement on PRC targeting of commercial telecommunications infrastructure, undated on the page read](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/news/joint-statement-fbi-and-cisa-peoples-republic-china-prc-targeting-commercial-telecommunications)). The Federal Communications Commission (FCC) later recorded that the intrusions were disclosed in September 2024, reached "at least eight U.S. communications companies", and exploited "publicly known common vulnerabilities and exposures (CVEs) and other avoidable weaknesses" rather than previously unknown flaws ([FCC, Order on Reconsideration, FCC 25-81, adopted 20 November 2025](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-25-81A1_Rcd.pdf)).

**Does CALEA require carriers to secure their networks?** On 15 January 2025, five days before a change of administration, the FCC adopted a declaratory ruling which, as the Commission later summarized it, "conclud[ed] that section 105 of CALEA affirmatively requires telecommunications carriers . . . to secure their networks from unlawful access to or interception of communications" ([FCC, notice of order on reconsideration, Federal Register, 15 December 2025](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-12-15/html/2025-22830.htm)). On 20 November 2025 the Commission, by two votes to one, rescinded it, finding that the ruling "misconstrued" CALEA and that its "inflexible, across-the-board cybersecurity requirements" were ineffective, and pointing instead to commitments carriers had made after the intrusions (FCC 25-81; [The Register, 24 November 2025](https://www.theregister.com/2025/11/24/fcc_salt_typhoon_rules/)). Commissioner Anna Gomez dissented: "Simply trusting industry to police itself is an invitation for the next breach. And when the next breach occurs, there will be no standards to measure compliance and no mechanism for determining which safeguards should have been in place. That is governing by hope rather than by duty" (FCC 25-81, dissenting statement).

## Brazil: interception by court order

**Lei 9.296/1996.** The Constitution protects the secrecy of communications in article 5, item XII, and Law 9.296 of 24 July 1996, known as the telephone interception law, regulates its final part ([Senado Federal, Lei nº 9.296, record of the law](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/551172)). Interception of telephone communications "de qualquer natureza" (of any kind), "para prova em investigação criminal" (as evidence in a criminal investigation), "dependerá de ordem do juiz competente da ação principal, sob segredo de justiça" (requires an order of the judge competent for the main case, under judicial secrecy), and the law "aplica-se à interceptação do fluxo de comunicações em sistemas de informática e telemática" (applies to the interception of the flow of communications in computer and telematic systems) ([Lei nº 9.296, compiled text, Senado Federal legislation portal](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/551172/publicacao/34619655)). It is not allowed where there are no "indícios razoáveis da autoria ou participação em infração penal" (reasonable indications of authorship or participation in a criminal offence), where "a prova puder ser feita por outros meios disponíveis" (the evidence can be obtained by other available means), or where the offence carries at most "pena de detenção", the lighter of Brazil's two forms of imprisonment. The order may be given at the request of the police or the prosecution service, must be reasoned "sob pena de nulidade" (on pain of nullity), and "não poderá exceder o prazo de quinze dias, renovável por igual tempo uma vez comprovada a indispensabilidade do meio de prova" (may not exceed fifteen days, renewable for the same period once the measure is shown to be indispensable). The police conduct the interception and inform the prosecution service, and "poderá requisitar serviços e técnicos especializados às concessionárias de serviço público" (may requisition specialized services and technicians from the public service concessionaires, that is, the telecommunications operators). Recordings that do not serve as evidence are destroyed by court order (same source).

**The crime of unlawful interception.** The law makes it a crime "realizar interceptação de comunicações telefônicas, de informática ou telemática, promover escuta ambiental ou quebrar segredo da Justiça, sem autorização judicial ou com objetivos não autorizados em lei" (to intercept telephone, computer or telematic communications, carry out environmental eavesdropping or break judicial secrecy without judicial authorization or for purposes not authorized by law), punished with "reclusão, de 2 (dois) a 4 (quatro) anos, e multa" (two to four years' imprisonment and a fine); since an amendment in force from January 2020 the same penalty applies to "a autoridade judicial que determina a execução de conduta prevista no caput deste artigo com objetivo não autorizado em lei", the judge who orders such conduct for a purpose the law does not authorize (same source; amendments listed on the [Senado record](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/551172)). The 2019 anti-crime package added the capture of "sinais eletromagnéticos, ópticos ou acústicos" (electromagnetic, optical or acoustic signals), allowed by a judge when the evidence cannot be obtained otherwise and the offence carries a maximum penalty above four years.

**Logs and content under the Marco Civil.** Law 12.965/2014 guarantees the "inviolabilidade e sigilo do fluxo de suas comunicações pela internet, salvo por ordem judicial, na forma da lei" (inviolability and secrecy of internet communications in transit, save by court order under the law) and of "comunicações privadas armazenadas, salvo por ordem judicial" (stored private communications, save by court order) ([Lei nº 12.965, Senado Federal legislation portal](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/584776/publicacao/15630642)). Connection records must be kept by the administrator of each autonomous system "sob sigilo, em ambiente controlado e de segurança, pelo prazo de 1 (um) ano" (in secrecy, in a controlled and secure environment, for one year), and application access records by organized commercial providers for six months; both may be released only "mediante ordem judicial", while "O conteúdo das comunicações privadas somente poderá ser disponibilizado mediante ordem judicial" (the content of private communications may be released only by court order), and administrative authorities may obtain basic registration data, meaning "qualificação pessoal, filiação e endereço" (identity, parentage and address) (same source). Connection providers are barred from blocking, monitoring, filtering "ou analisar o conteúdo dos pacotes de dados" (or analysing the content of data packets), subject to the article's exceptions.

**What the data protection law leaves out.** The Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados (LGPD) does not apply to processing "realizado para fins exclusivos de: a) segurança pública; b) defesa nacional; c) segurança do Estado" or to criminal investigation and prosecution, and says that such processing "será regido por legislação específica" (shall be governed by specific legislation), which must provide proportionate and strictly necessary measures ([Lei nº 13.709, de 14 de agosto de 2018, original text, Senado Federal legislation portal](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/27457334/publicacao/27457731)). No such specific law was found among the sources read for this part.

**When the order meets end-to-end encryption.** In 2016 and 2017 several first-instance judges ordered WhatsApp suspended across Brazil after the company said it could not hand over conversations between people under investigation, citing the technical impossibility of breaking end-to-end encryption; the then president of the Supremo Tribunal Federal (STF, Federal Supreme Court), Ricardo Lewandowski, suspended one such order as disproportionate ([Mobile Time, 27 May 2020](https://www.mobiletime.com.br/noticias/27/05/2020/rosa-weber-vota-contra-o-bloqueio-do-whatsapp/)). Two cases on the question reached the STF's plenary in May 2020. Justice Rosa Weber, rapporteur of one, held that the Marco Civil's sanctions protect privacy and do not authorize suspending an application, and said: "O desenvolvimento e a implementação de criptografia é o que torna as comunicações mais seguras. Por isso, não se pode tornar ilegal ou limitar o uso de criptografia nas aplicações" (developing and deploying encryption is what makes communications more secure, so its use in applications cannot be outlawed or limited) (same source). She allowed, however, that a court may order the content of communications made available for criminal investigations ([Consultor Jurídico, 28 May 2020](https://www.conjur.com.br/2020-mai-28/bloqueio-judicial-whatsapp-inconstitucional-fachin//?print=1)). Justice Edson Fachin, rapporteur of the other, went further: "Por entender que o risco causado pelo uso da criptografia ainda não justifica a imposição de soluções que envolvam acesso excepcional ou ainda outras soluções que diminuam a proteção garantida por uma criptografia forte, penso que não há como obrigar que as aplicações de internet que ofereçam criptografia ponta a ponta quebrem o sigilo do conteúdo de comunicações" (because the risk from encryption does not yet justify exceptional access or other solutions that weaken strong encryption, applications offering end-to-end encryption cannot be compelled to break the secrecy of content) (same source). Justice Alexandre de Moraes asked for time to review the case, and in April 2024 the case on the Sergipe suspension was sent back to the plenary ([Teletime, 19 April 2024](https://teletime.com.br/19/04/2024/acao-sobre-bloqueio-de-apps-de-mensagens-ira-ao-plenario-do-stf/)). The sources read for this part do not record a final judgment.

**When a state agency allegedly bypasses the court.** On 20 October 2023 the Federal Police opened Operation Última Milha to investigate <Claim status="alleged" by="by the Federal Police">"o uso indevido de sistema de geolocalização de dispositivos móveis sem a devida autorização judicial por servidores da Abin"</Claim>, the improper use of a mobile geolocation system without judicial authorization by staff of the Agência Brasileira de Inteligência (ABIN, Brazilian Intelligence Agency); according to the investigation the system was "um software intrusivo na infraestrutura crítica de telefonia brasileira" (intrusive software in Brazil's critical telephone infrastructure), and the network "teria sido invadida reiteradas vezes" (would have been repeatedly breached) ([Agência Gov, 20 October 2023](https://agenciagov.ebc.com.br/noticias/202310/apuracao-de-legalidade-de-uso-de-feramenta-de-geolocalizacao)). ABIN said that its internal affairs office had reviewed the tool, acquired in December 2018, in February 2023, that it had opened an internal inquiry and shared its findings with the police and the STF, that it had cooperated from the start, and that "a ferramenta deixou de ser utilizada em maio de 2021" (the tool stopped being used in May 2021) (same source). In January 2024 the police searched addresses of Deputy Alexandre Ramagem, ABIN's director-general under President Jair Bolsonaro, stating that <Claim status="alleged" by="by the Federal Police">"o grupo criminoso criou uma estrutura paralela na Abin"</Claim> (the criminal group created a parallel structure within ABIN); Congresso em Foco reported that the tool was FirstMile, used irregularly between December 2018 and 2021 against politicians, lawyers, journalists, judges and members of the STF, and that Ramagem said he would not comment on the operation for the time being ([Congresso em Foco, 25 January 2024](https://congressoemfoco.com.br/noticia/7627/ramagem-e-alvo-de-busca-e-apreensao-da-pf-por-esquema-de-espionagem-na-abin)). In July 2024 a fourth phase alleged that members of the three branches of government and journalists had been targeted, including through fake profiles, and that the group had "acessou ilegalmente computadores, aparelhos de telefonia e infraestrutura de telecomunicações" (illegally accessed computers, telephones and telecommunications infrastructure); suspects could answer, the police said, for crimes including "interceptação clandestina de comunicações" ([Agência Gov, 11 July 2024](https://agenciagov.ebc.com.br/noticias/202407/policia-federal-operacao-ultima-milha-abin-espionagem-autoridades)). These are allegations in an investigation; the sources read for this part do not record a judgment.

## The 2013 disclosures

**What was disclosed.** "On June 5, 2013, the British newspaper The Guardian published the first of a series of articles based on unauthorized disclosures of classified documents by Edward Snowden, a contractor for the National Security Agency", the Privacy and Civil Liberties Oversight Board (PCLOB) later recorded; the first article "described an NSA program to collect millions of telephone records, including records about purely domestic calls", and further articles described a programme called PRISM ([PCLOB, Report on the Telephone Records Program Conducted under Section 215, 23 January 2014](https://documents.pclob.gov/prod/Documents/OversightReport/ec542143-1079-424a-84b3-acc354698560/215-Report_on_the_Telephone_Records_Program.pdf)). <Claim status="established" by="by authorized government disclosures, as the PCLOB records">"Subsequently, authorized disclosures from the government confirmed both programs"</Claim>: under one, the NSA collected "telephone call records or metadata", but not "the content of phone conversations", "covering the calls of most Americans on an ongoing basis", approved by the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court (FISC) under Section 215 of the USA PATRIOT Act; under the other, based on Section 702 of the amendments of 2008 to the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA), it collected "the content of electronic communications" of targets "reasonably believed to be non-U.S. persons located outside the United States" (same source).

**How PRISM and upstream collection work, officially.** The PCLOB described Section 702 collection in 2014: the government "sends written directives to electronic communication service providers compelling their assistance", and "tasks" selectors "such as telephone numbers or email addresses". In "PRISM" collection, a selector goes to a US-based provider, "and the provider is compelled to give the communications sent to or from that selector to the government". "Upstream" collection uses "the compelled assistance of providers that control the telecommunications 'backbone'", and included "about" communications, in which "the selector of a targeted person ... is contained within the communication but the targeted person is not necessarily a participant" ([PCLOB, Report on the Surveillance Program Operated Pursuant to Section 702, 2 July 2014](https://documents.pclob.gov/prod/Documents/OversightReport/823399ae-92ea-447a-ab60-0da28b555437/702-Report-2.pdf)).

**A company's answer.** Google's chief executive and chief legal officer wrote on 7 June 2013 that the company had "not joined any program that would give the U.S. government", or any other government, "direct access to our servers. Indeed, the U.S. government does not have direct access or a 'back door' to the information stored in our data centers. We had not heard of a program called PRISM until yesterday", adding that "the level of secrecy around the current legal procedures undermines the freedoms we all cherish" ([Larry Page and David Drummond, What the ...?, Google, 7 June 2013](https://blog.google/technology/safety-security/what/)).

**The oversight board on Section 215.** In January 2014 the PCLOB majority found that the telephone records programme "lacks a viable legal foundation under Section 215, implicates constitutional concerns under the First and Fourth Amendments, raises serious threats to privacy and civil liberties as a policy matter, and has shown only limited value", and recommended ending it; on value, it wrote that "we have not identified a single instance involving a threat to the United States in which the program made a concrete difference in the outcome of a counterterrorism investigation" (PCLOB, 2014). Board member Rachel Brand dissented from that recommendation "to shut down the Section 215 program without establishing an adequate alternative", wrote that the legal question "will be resolved by the courts, not by this Board", and agreed with the majority that the programme was constitutional under existing Supreme Court case law (same source, separate statement). The President's Review Group had concluded a month earlier that the information the programme contributed "was not essential to preventing attacks and could readily have been obtained in a timely manner using conventional section 215 orders" ([President's Review Group on Intelligence and Communications Technologies, Liberty and Security in a Changing World, 12 December 2013](https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/sites/default/files/docs/2013-12-12_rg_final_report.pdf)).

**The oversight board on Section 702.** In July 2014 the PCLOB concluded that "PRISM collection is clearly authorized by the statute" and that the programme's core fits the Fourth Amendment's reasonableness standard, but that "certain aspects of the Section 702 program push the program close to the line of constitutional reasonableness", naming "the unknown and potentially large scope of the incidental collection of U.S. persons' communications", "about" collection, and "the use of queries to search for the communications of specific U.S. persons" (PCLOB, 2014).

**The courts.** On 16 December 2013 Judge Richard Leon of the District Court for the District of Columbia wrote: "I cannot imagine a more 'indiscriminate' and 'arbitrary invasion' than this systematic and high-tech collection and retention of personal data on virtually every single citizen for purposes of querying and analyzing it without prior judicial approval"; he granted two plaintiffs an injunction but stayed it "in light of the significant national security interests at stake in this case and the novelty of the constitutional issues" ([Klayman v. Obama, US District Court for the District of Columbia, 16 December 2013, read from a scanned copy](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-dcd-1_13-cv-00851/pdf/USCOURTS-dcd-1_13-cv-00851-0.pdf)); the ruling was later vacated on appeal ([United States v. Moalin, US Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, 2 September 2020, citing 800 F.3d 559 (D.C. Cir. 2015)](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2020/09/02/13-50572.pdf)). On 7 May 2015, in a suit brought by the American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU), the Second Circuit held "that the text of § 215 cannot bear the weight the government asks us to assign to it, and that it does not authorize the telephone metadata program" ([ACLU v. Clapper, US Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit, 7 May 2015](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-ca2-14-00042/pdf/USCOURTS-ca2-14-00042-0.pdf)). In 2020 the Ninth Circuit, affirming convictions in a terrorism-financing case, concluded "that the government may have violated the Fourth Amendment and did violate the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act" when it collected the telephony metadata of millions of Americans, holding that the programme "exceeded the scope of Congress's authorization", but found suppression not warranted; having reviewed the classified record, it wrote that "to the extent the public statements of government officials created a contrary impression, that impression is inconsistent with the contents of the classified record" (Moalin, 2020).

**The legislature.** The Uniting and Strengthening America by Fulfilling Rights and Ensuring Effective Discipline Over Monitoring Act (USA FREEDOM Act), signed on 2 June 2015, contains sections headed "Prohibition on bulk collection of tangible things" and "Prohibition on bulk collection", and requires that applications under Section 215 name "a specific selection term to be used as the basis for the production" ([Public Law 114-23, USA FREEDOM Act of 2015, 2 June 2015](https://www.congress.gov/114/plaws/publ23/PLAW-114publ23.pdf)).

**Europe's courts.** On 8 April 2014 the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) declared the Data Retention Directive invalid because it "entails a wide-ranging and particularly serious interference with the fundamental rights to respect for private life and to the protection of personal data, without that interference being limited to what is strictly necessary" ([CJEU, press release 54/14, Digital Rights Ireland, 8 April 2014](https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/upload/docs/application/pdf/2014-04/cp140054en.pdf)). On 6 October 2015, in a case brought by an Austrian Facebook user, Maximillian Schrems, it invalidated the Commission's "Safe Harbour" decision on transfers to the United States, holding that "legislation permitting the public authorities to have access on a generalised basis to the content of electronic communications must be regarded as compromising the essence of the fundamental right to respect for private life" ([CJEU, press release 117/15, Schrems, 6 October 2015](https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/upload/docs/application/pdf/2015-10/cp150117en.pdf)). On 16 July 2020 it invalidated the successor "Privacy Shield", finding that US surveillance programmes "are not limited to what is strictly necessary", that the provisions "do not grant data subjects actionable rights before the courts against the US authorities", and that the Ombudsperson mechanism did not offer equivalent guarantees ([CJEU, press release 91/20, Data Protection Commissioner v Facebook Ireland and Maximillian Schrems, 16 July 2020](https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/upload/docs/application/pdf/2020-07/cp200091en.pdf)). On 25 May 2021 the Grand Chamber of the European Court of Human Rights held that Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights "does not prohibit the use of bulk interception to protect national security and other essential national interests against serious external threats", but that such a regime must be subject to "end-to-end safeguards", meaning independent authorization at the outset, assessment of necessity and proportionality at each stage, and supervision and independent review after the fact; the United Kingdom's former regime had "fundamental deficiencies": "the absence of independent authorisation, the failure to include the categories of selectors in the application for a warrant, and the failure to subject selectors linked to an individual to prior internal authorisation" ([Big Brother Watch and Others v. the United Kingdom, European Court of Human Rights, Grand Chamber, 25 May 2021](https://globalfreedomofexpression.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/CASE-OF-BIG-BROTHER-WATCH-AND-OTHERS-v.-THE-UNITED-KINGDOM-1.pdf)).

**Brazil.** In September 2013, as Agence France-Presse (AFP) reported, the television programme Fantástico, of TV Globo, showed a June 2012 NSA training slide that listed the oil company Petrobras among targets on "private networks"; TV Globo said it did not know the scope or objectives of the spying ([SecurityWeek, AFP report, 9 September 2013](https://www.securityweek.com/us-spied-brazils-petrobras-oil-firm-report/)). The Director of National Intelligence, James Clapper, answered that it was "not a secret" that US intelligence collects economic and financial information, and that "What we do not do, as we have said many times, is use our foreign intelligence capabilities" to steal the trade secrets of foreign companies or hand intelligence to US companies (same source). On 24 September 2013 President Dilma Rousseff told the United Nations (UN) General Assembly that "Personal data of citizens was intercepted indiscriminately", that the communications of Brazilian diplomatic missions and "the Office of the President of the Republic itself" had been intercepted, that "The arguments that the illegal interception of information and data aims at protecting nations against terrorism cannot be sustained", and that Brazil had "demanded explanations, apologies and guarantees that such procedures will never be repeated" ([Statement by Dilma Rousseff at the opening of the general debate of the 68th session of the UN General Assembly, 24 September 2013](https://gadebate.un.org/sites/default/files/gastatements/68/br_en_25.pdf)). On 18 December 2013 the General Assembly adopted resolution 68/167, "The right to privacy in the digital age", which affirmed "that the same rights that people have offline must also be protected online, including the right to privacy", and called on all states to review their procedures and legislation on surveillance, "including mass surveillance, interception and collection" ([UN General Assembly, resolution 68/167, 18 December 2013](https://documents.un.org/api/symbol/access?s=A/RES/68/167&l=en&t=pdf)).

## Section 702 after the disclosures

**The 2023 review.** The PCLOB reported in September 2023 that "During calendar year 2022, approximately 246,073 non-U.S. persons located abroad were targeted under Section 702", a number that "has nearly doubled over the last five years" ([PCLOB, Report on the Surveillance Program Operated Pursuant to Section 702, 28 September 2023](https://documents.pclob.gov/prod/Documents/OversightReport/054417e4-9d20-427a-9850-862a6f29ac42/2023%20PCLOB%20702%20Report%20%28002%29.pdf)). The majority assessed that queries using Americans' identifiers "present some of the most serious privacy and civil liberties harms", noting that agents need not make "any showing of suspicion" before such a query, and recommended that Congress "require FISC authorization of U.S. person query terms" (same source). Board members Beth Williams and Richard DiZinno voted against the report, which they called "deeply flawed", and wrote that the programme "is both legal, and incredibly valuable", that "failure to reauthorize the program would cause grave damage to the security of our country, and quite likely, lead to the loss of American lives", and that the query recommendation "would force additional, potentially invasive and unnecessary investigation of U.S. persons" (same source, summary of their separate statement).

**Reauthorization and lapse.** The Reforming Intelligence and Securing America Act extended the authority until "two years after the date of enactment" ([Public Law 118-49, 20 April 2024](https://www.congress.gov/118/plaws/publ49/PLAW-118publ49.pdf)). In April 2026, after the House failed to pass a five-year and an 18-month renewal, Congress extended Section 702 for ten days; National Public Radio (NPR) reported "nearly 350,000 targets" and wrote that "The intelligence community has argued" that requiring court approval for queries "would inhibit the efficacy of the tool and endanger national security" ([NPR, as published by KPBS, 17 April 2026](https://www.kpbs.org/news/politics/2026/04/17/congress-extends-controversial-surveillance-powers-for-10-days)). On 30 April Congress passed a 45-day extension, after the leaders of the Senate Intelligence Committee agreed to seek the declassification of a FISC opinion; Senator Ron Wyden said on the floor that "the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court found major compliance problems related to the surveillance law known as section 702" ([CyberScoop, 30 April 2026](https://cyberscoop.com/congress-extends-section-702-surveillance-45-days/)); President Donald Trump signed it after the Senate rejected a three-year extension passed by the House ([Fox News, 1 May 2026](https://www.foxnews.com/politics/trump-signs-stopgap-fisa-extension-after-senate-blocks-long-term-renewal)). In June 2026 the authority lapsed. NPR reported that collection authorized by the FISC's annual certification could continue for its duration, that the government says "more than 60% of the president's daily intelligence briefing relies on information collected under the authority", and quoted a former NSA general counsel, Glenn Gerstell: "I don't want to overhype this and say that the statute's lapse is a horrific risk. It clearly is not ... But by the same token, I just want to emphasize that it is irresponsible to accept any risk in this area under circumstances where we can control the risk" ([NPR, as published by WAMC, 12 June 2026](https://www.wamc.org/2026-06-12/a-key-u-s-spy-tool-has-lapsed-now-what)). Elizabeth Goitein of the Brennan Center told NPR the security risks of a lapse were limited and that she wanted the law updated with privacy reforms (same source). The Independent Institute, a think tank, wrote in July 2026 that Congress had "until March 2027 before surveillance authority ends" ([Independent Institute, 7 July 2026](https://www.independent.org/?p=97256)). The sources read for this part do not show whether the authority was renewed after July 2026.

## Restraint on decisions: profiling rules

**Article 22 of the European regulation.** The EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) defines profiling as "any form of automated processing of personal data consisting of the use of personal data to evaluate certain personal aspects relating to a natural person, in particular to analyse or predict aspects concerning that natural person's performance at work, economic situation, health, personal preferences, interests, reliability, behaviour, location or movements" ([Regulation (EU) 2016/679, General Data Protection Regulation, article 4(4), 27 April 2016](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32016R0679)). Article 22 gives a person "the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, which produces legal effects concerning him or her or similarly significantly affects him or her", except where the decision is necessary for a contract, authorized by law with safeguards, or based on explicit consent; in the contract and consent cases the controller must provide "at least the right to obtain human intervention on the part of the controller, to express his or her point of view and to contest the decision" (same source). In December 2023 the CJEU held that a credit agency's score "must be regarded as an 'automated individual decision' prohibited in principle by the GDPR, in so far as SCHUFA's clients, such as banks, attribute to it a determining role in the granting of credit" ([CJEU, press release 186/23, SCHUFA Holding (Scoring), 7 December 2023](https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/upload/docs/application/pdf/2023-12/cp230186en.pdf)).

**LGPD article 20.** Brazil's law, as first enacted, gave the data subject the right to request review, "por pessoa natural" (by a natural person), of decisions "tomadas unicamente com base em tratamento automatizado de dados pessoais que afetem seus interesses" (taken solely on automated processing of personal data that affect their interests), including those "destinadas a definir o seu perfil pessoal, profissional, de consumo e de crédito ou os aspectos de sua personalidade" (meant to define their personal, professional, consumer or credit profile or aspects of their personality) (LGPD, original text). Law 13.853 of 8 July 2019 rewrote the article without the words "por pessoa natural", and a new paragraph 3 was vetoed ([Lei nº 13.853, de 8 de julho de 2019, Senado Federal legislation portal](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/31172769/publicacao/31222798)). The controller must provide "informações claras e adequadas a respeito dos critérios e dos procedimentos utilizados para a decisão automatizada, observados os segredos comercial e industrial" (clear and adequate information on the criteria and procedures used, subject to trade and industrial secrets); if it withholds them on secrecy grounds, the national authority "poderá realizar auditoria para verificação de aspectos discriminatórios em tratamento automatizado de dados pessoais" (may audit automated processing for discriminatory aspects); and data used "para formação do perfil comportamental de determinada pessoa natural, se identificada" (to form the behavioural profile of an identified person) may count as personal data (LGPD, original text, articles 20 and 12).

**Platforms and recommender systems.** The Digital Services Act (DSA) forbids advertisements "based on profiling" using special categories of personal data, forbids profiling-based advertisements to users known "with reasonable certainty" to be minors, and requires the largest platforms to offer at least one recommender option "which is not based on profiling" ([Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, articles 26(3), 28(2) and 38, 19 October 2022](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065)).

**The state's own uses.** The EU's Artificial Intelligence Act (AI Act), applying its prohibitions from 2 February 2025, bans systems for "the evaluation or classification of natural persons or groups of persons over a certain period of time based on their social behaviour" leading to unjustified or unrelated detrimental treatment; systems that assess "the risk of a natural person committing a criminal offence, based solely on the profiling of a natural person or on assessing their personality traits and characteristics"; and the use of "'real-time' remote biometric identification systems in publicly accessible spaces for the purposes of law enforcement", except where strictly necessary for listed purposes such as searching for abduction victims or preventing an imminent threat, and then only with prior authorization by "a judicial authority or an independent administrative authority whose decision is binding" ([Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Artificial Intelligence Act, articles 5 and 113, 13 June 2024](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R1689)).

## Where the record disagrees

The sources above agree on some facts and disagree on their weight. The table sets out the main disagreements with who holds each position; it is a map of the dispute, not a conclusion.

| Question | One position, and who holds it | Another position, and who holds it |
|---|---|---|
| Was bulk telephone metadata collection lawful? | Not authorized by Section 215 (PCLOB majority, 2014; Second Circuit, 2015; Ninth Circuit, 2020) | A difficult statutory question that many FISC judges and one district judge upheld, for the courts to decide (Rachel Brand, PCLOB, 2014) |
| Did it work? | No instance in which it made "a concrete difference" (PCLOB majority, 2014); "not essential to preventing attacks" (Review Group, 2013) | The NSA believed it contributed on "at least a few occasions" (as the Review Group recorded, 2013) |
| Should searches for Americans in Section 702 data need a court's approval? | Yes (PCLOB majority, 2023; privacy-minded lawmakers, as NPR reported, 2026) | No: it would burden agents and the court and hinder detecting threats (Williams and DiZinno, 2023; the intelligence community, as NPR reported, 2026) |
| Is bulk interception compatible with human rights? | Only with end-to-end safeguards; generalized access to content compromises the essence of privacy (European Court of Human Rights, 2021; CJEU, 2015) | Permitted within a margin of appreciation to meet serious external threats (European Court of Human Rights, 2021) |
| Should networks be built for interception? | Yes, by statute and standard (CALEA, 1994; ETSI, 2021) | Not in internet standards, because complexity weakens security (IETF, 2000); the capability has been abused by intruders (Athens, 2004; Salt Typhoon, 2024) |
| Should carriers be legally bound to secure the interception systems? | Yes, through CALEA (FCC, January 2025; Commissioner Gomez, 2025) | No: the reading misconstrued the statute and voluntary commitments work better (FCC majority, November 2025) |
| Can courts order encrypted messaging providers to hand over content? | A court may order content made available for criminal investigations (Rosa Weber, 2020) | Providers offering end-to-end encryption cannot be compelled to break it (Fachin, 2020) |

## What defenders and readers can do

- **Treat interception systems as the most sensitive part of a network.** The ETSI requirements call for restricted staff, controlled rooms and protection of the handover interface "against misuse"; the authors of the Athens study note that a tap active in the switch with no matching request in the interception management system "is a pretty good indicator that an unauthorized tap has occurred"; the FCC recorded that the 2024 intrusions used known vulnerabilities (ETSI TS 101 331; IEEE Spectrum, 2007; FCC 25-81).
- **Know what the law can and cannot compel.** CALEA does not make a carrier decrypt what a customer encrypted; Brazilian law requires a reasoned court order, limited to fifteen days at a time, and makes unauthorized interception a crime (47 U.S.C. 1002; Lei 9.296/1996).
- **Use the profiling rights.** In the EU a person subject to a solely automated decision with significant effects can ask for human intervention and contest it; in Brazil a data subject can ask for review of a solely automated decision and for the criteria used, and the national authority can audit for discrimination; very large platforms in the EU must offer a feed not based on profiling (GDPR article 22; LGPD article 20; DSA article 38).
- **Read the oversight record.** The PCLOB's reports, the courts' opinions and the UN resolution are public; so are the rules on how long Brazilian providers keep logs and who may obtain them (Marco Civil, articles 10, 13 and 15).

## What to remember

- CALEA (1994) obliges US carriers to make interception possible on lawful authorization, but excludes information services and does not require decrypting customers' own encryption; ETSI's standards define the handover interface, content and intercept-related information, and require that interception be undetectable; the IETF declined in 2000 to build wiretapping into internet standards.
- The capability has been turned against its owners: rogue software used Vodafone Greece's lawful interception functions to tap about 100 phones in 2004 and 2005, and in 2024 the FBI and CISA reported that PRC-affiliated actors copied information subject to US court-ordered requests; the FCC's attempt to read a security duty into CALEA was rescinded in November 2025.
- In Brazil, Law 9.296/1996 allows telephone and telematic interception only by reasoned court order, for offences punished with reclusão, when no other means exists, for fifteen days at a time, and punishes unauthorized interception with two to four years; the Marco Civil requires a court order for logs and content.
- The STF's justices split in 2020 over whether courts may order encrypted content handed over, and the case awaits a final judgment in the sources read; the Federal Police allege that ABIN staff used a geolocation tool without judicial authorization from 2018 to 2021, which ABIN says it stopped in May 2021.
- After the 2013 disclosures, the PCLOB found the Section 215 programme without legal foundation and of minimal value, with a dissent; courts in 2015 and 2020 held it unauthorized; the USA FREEDOM Act prohibited bulk collection; the CJEU struck down data retention and two transfer frameworks; the European Court of Human Rights required end-to-end safeguards.
- Section 702 targeted about 246,073 people in 2022 and nearly 350,000 by 2026, by the figures cited; it lapsed in June 2026 while collection under existing certifications continued, and its renewal had not been confirmed in the sources read.
- The GDPR and the LGPD give rights against solely automated decisions; Brazil removed the requirement that the review be done by a person in 2019; the DSA and the AI Act restrict profiling-based advertising, recommender systems and predictive policing based solely on profiling.
