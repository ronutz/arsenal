A trader's screen, a broker's router and an exchange's own price checks all start from the same raw material: the quotes and trades that venues publish, known as market data. In a market with one venue that data comes from one place. In a market with many venues it comes twice, once through a consolidated feed that merges every venue into one stream under rules set by the regulator, and once through each venue's own direct feed, which is sold separately, carries more detail and usually arrives first. This part of the series opened by [Electronic trading: from the pit to the microsecond](/learn/electronic-trading-from-the-pit-to-the-microsecond) builds on [The life of an order in FIX](/learn/the-life-of-an-order-in-fix), which followed an order in the Financial Information eXchange (FIX) protocol until it traded; here the subject is what the rest of the market sees of it. The byte-level encodings of the feeds, from Nasdaq's ITCH to the binary and compressed versions of the feed of B3 (Brasil, Bolsa, Balcão), Brazil's exchange, are in [FIX encodings and successors](/learn/fix-encodings-and-successors-fixml-fast-sbe-and-exchange-binary-protocols) and are not repeated. After reading, you will be able to say what a consolidated feed contains and who pays for it, read a processor's latency statistics for what they measure and what they leave out, compute a national best bid and offer under the round lots that took effect in 2025, follow Europe from no consolidated tape to three, trace one of B3's market data channels from its multicast address to its recovery loop, and weigh both sides of the long dispute over who should own and sell market data.

## What market data is, and why it comes in two forms

Market data has a few standard layers. The last sale says what traded, at what price and in what size; the top of the book says the best price to buy and to sell and the size at each; depth says what rests behind the best prices. B3's market data policy turns the last two into licence categories, Depth Level 1 (L1) for the top of the book and Depth Level 2 (L2) for the complete order book ([B3, Market Data B3 Commercial Policy 2026, version 1.2.1](https://b3.com.br/data/files/FB/95/83/32/AD881A10F75B881AAC094EA8/Market%20Data%20B3%20Commercial%20Policy%202026%20V1.2.1.pdf)). Depth itself comes in two shapes, by price level (market by price) or order by order (market by order); the binary version of B3's Unified Market Data Feed (UMDF), as part 380 showed, publishes its books by order only ([B3, Binary UMDF messaging specification guidelines 2.3.1.2](https://www.b3.com.br/data/files/84/B7/1D/9E/AB611A103BBB511AAC094EA8/BinaryUMDF-MessageSpecificationGuidelines-v.2.3.1.2-enUS.pdf)).

Where several venues trade the same stock, someone has to combine their quotes, or nobody can tell which venue has the best price. The United States Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) traces the requirement to 1975, when "one of Congress's principal objectives for the national market system was to assure the availability of information with respect to quotations for, and transactions in, securities" ([SEC, press release 2020-311, 9 December 2020](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2020-311)). In a 2012 enforcement order it quoted the House report of that year: consolidated data "would form the heart of the national market system" ([SEC, Release 34-67857, 14 September 2012](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2012/34-67857.pdf)). The rules that followed, from the national market system to the order protection rule of 2005, are told in [The rules that rewired markets](/learn/the-rules-that-rewired-markets-reg-ats-reg-nms-mifid-and-brazil); this part follows the data itself.

The two forms answer different needs. A consolidated feed is what a retail broker shows its customers and what venues use to check that they do not trade through a better price elsewhere. A direct feed is what a trading firm's machines consume when every microsecond and every order in the book matter. The second is a business. The United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit (D.C. Circuit) described exchanges that "develop and sell proprietary securities market data products" and said that "distribution of their proprietary data is a lucrative business for the exchanges" ([D.C. Circuit, The Nasdaq Stock Market LLC v. SEC, No. 21-1100, decided 24 May 2022](https://media.cadc.uscourts.gov/opinions/docs/2022/05/21-1100-1947763.pdf)). Most of the disputes in this part come from the gap between the two.

## The United States: two processors under two plans

### Who runs the tape

The consolidated feed in the United States is produced by securities information processors (SIPs). The Consolidated Tape Association (CTA) describes the processor as linking the markets "by processing and consolidating all protected bid/ask quotes and trades from every trading venue into a single, easily consumed data feed", and says it calculates and disseminates the national best bid and offer (NBBO) and the limit up, limit down (LULD) price bands, among other regulatory information such as short sale restrictions and halts ([CTA Plan, overview, read 16 November 2026](https://www.ctaplan.com/)).

There are two processors, under two plans. The CTA and the Consolidated Quotation (CQ) plans cover stocks listed on the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE), known as Network or Tape A, and stocks listed on NYSE Arca, NYSE American, Cboe and other regional exchanges, Tape B; "Since the late 1970s, all SEC-registered exchanges and market centers that trade Network A or Network B securities send their trades and quotes to a central consolidator", which produces the Consolidated Tape System (CTS) and Consolidated Quote System (CQS) streams ([CTA Plan, overview](https://www.ctaplan.com/)). The Unlisted Trading Privileges (UTP) Plan covers Nasdaq-listed stocks, Tape C, and its data includes the national best bid and offer and each member's best bid and offer with sizes ([UTP Plan, overview](https://utpplan.com/PageParts/Overview.html)). The CTA's page on odd lots spells out who does what: the NYSE is administrator of the CTA and CQ plans and the Securities Industry Automation Corporation (SIAC) is the processor, while "Nasdaq business units serve as the Administrator and Processor for the UTP SIP" ([CTA Plan, Odd Lots](https://www.ctaplan.com/odd-lots)). The CTA's participant list on 16 November 2026 named 20 exchanges and the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA), from the 24X National Exchange to the Texas Stock Exchange ([CTA Plan, overview](https://www.ctaplan.com/)).

### What the processors publish about themselves

The plans publish operating metrics. The CTA's report for the third quarter of 2026 gives, for the quote feed, a median latency of 18.0 microseconds and a 99th percentile of 24.5, with a peak of 325,400 messages in 100 milliseconds against a capacity of 950,000, a ratio of 2.9 to 1; for the trade feed, a median of 18.6 and a 99th percentile of 31.8, with a peak of 80,900 messages in 100 milliseconds against a capacity of 350,000; and 100.00% system availability against a service level of 99.98% ([CTA, Key Operating Metrics, third quarter 2026](https://www.ctaplan.com/publicdocs/ctaplan/CTA_SIP_Metrics_3Q_2026.pdf)). The same report defines the measurement: latency runs from the time stamp taken "as an inbound Participant message arrives at the network entrance" to the CTS and CQS environment until the consolidated message leaves it.

The documents are not perfectly consistent, and a reader who uses them should know where. The column headings of the 2026 report say "Latency (us)", microseconds, but the footnote on the quote page says "Latency is represented in milliseconds" while the footnote on the trade page says microseconds (same report). The CTA's overview page gives capacity as "2,700,000 for the quote feed and 425,000 for the trade feed" per 100 milliseconds and the median latency as "under 20 microseconds" ([CTA Plan, overview](https://www.ctaplan.com/)), figures that differ from the capacity columns of the quarterly report. The UTP Plan's overview gives capacity of 2,700,700 and 2,734,100 messages per 100 milliseconds and states that "Current latency at the median is about 38.2-12.8 microseconds for the UTP Data Services", a range printed with its larger number first ([UTP Plan, overview](https://utpplan.com/PageParts/Overview.html)). The history is longer than the units: the CTA's 2015 report shows the quote feed's average falling from 4.04 in the first quarter of 2010 to 0.46 in the second quarter of 2015 without printing a unit, and notes that before 2013 latency was measured only from the processor's own time stamp to the end of processing ([CTA, Key Operating Metrics, second quarter 2015](https://ctaplan.com/publicdocs/CTAPLAN_Processor_Metrics_2Q2015.pdf)).

What none of these numbers includes is the time a quote spends travelling from a venue's matching engine to the processor, and from the processor to the user. The UTP Plan says so in so many words: its statistics "represent measurements within the SIP", and it added "Realized Latency" charts because "Realized Latency is the measurement from the inception of the Participant matching engine event (e.g. order execution, top of book update) to the point of dissemination from the SIP" ([UTP Plan, Realized Latency Charting](https://utpplan.com/PageParts/LatencyCharts.html)).

### Who pays, and how much

The tape is sold, and the revenue goes back to the venues that feed it. The plans publish quarterly revenue disclosures in which "Market data revenue is allocated according to an allocation formula and rules promulgated under Regulation NMS", NMS meaning the national market system ([UTP Plan, Revenue Disclosure, second quarter 2026](https://utpplan.com/DOC/UTP_Revenue_Disclosure_Q22026.pdf)). For Tape C the figures read as follows:

| Tape C | 2015 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue distributed to participants for quoting | US$59.4 million | US$71.7 million |
| Revenue distributed for trading (trade reports) | US$59.4 million | US$71.7 million |
| Total distributed | US$118.9 million | US$143.5 million |
| Quote messages | 35.3 billion | 150.4 billion |
| Trade messages | 2.95 billion | 14.1 billion |
| Revenue per quote message (as printed) | US$0.0017 | US$0.0005 |

Source: [UTP Plan, Revenue Disclosure, second quarter 2026, page 3](https://utpplan.com/DOC/UTP_Revenue_Disclosure_Q22026.pdf). Recomputed for this article from the plan's own totals, quote revenue per quote message was US$0.00168 in 2015 and US$0.00048 in 2025: quote traffic grew 4.26 times in ten years while total revenue grew 1.21 times. The same disclosure splits revenue by fee type: in the second quarter of 2026, 44.3% came from professional subscribers, 15.1% from non-professional subscribers, 13.0% from non-display use, 20.8% from per-quote queries and 6.8% from other fees, against 46.3%, 16.2%, 15.6%, 12.3% and 9.5% in the first quarter of 2015 (same disclosure, page 2). The plan's population metrics count 6,629,603 capped non-professional subscribers and 286,779 professional subscribers at the end of 2025, against 2,731,280 and 253,034 in the first quarter of 2015 ([UTP Plan, Key Quarterly Operating Metrics, fourth quarter 2025](https://www.utpplan.com/DOC/UTP_2025_Q4_Stats_with_Processor_Stats.pdf)). The CTA's own overview argues that its fees are "Competitive" and that most non-professionals "do not pay fees because the low cost allows for brokerage firms to include real-time prices as part of their service" ([CTA Plan, overview](https://www.ctaplan.com/)).

## Direct feeds and the latency gap

### Three sources of delay

Direct feeds are the venues' own products: Nasdaq's TotalView-ITCH, described byte by byte in part 380, is one. The SEC's 2020 rule on market data infrastructure explained why they arrive first. The centralized model "suffers from three sources of latencies: (a) geographic latency, (b) aggregation latency, and (c) transmission latency", and "Geographic latency is typically the most significant component of the latencies that the exclusive SIPs experience compared to the proprietary data feeds" ([SEC, Market Data Infrastructure, Release 34-90610, 9 December 2020](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/final/2020/34-90610.pdf)). The geography is specific. "The CTA/CQ SIP is located in Mahwah, NJ, and the UTP SIP is located in Carteret, NJ. Other exchanges and broker-dealers are located in Secaucus, NJ", and the three are "typically referred to as the 'New Jersey Triangle'"; the need for data to cross it, together with the limited content of the consolidated feed, "has led many market participants to relegate SIP data to backup data" (same release). A firm colocated beside one exchange receives that exchange's direct feed in microseconds, while the consolidated quote for the same event has to travel to another building, be merged and travel back. How the physical race for that time is run, with colocation, microwave towers and clock synchronization, is the subject of [Colocation, microwaves and the race for latency](/learn/colocation-microwaves-and-the-race-for-latency).

### The fairness rule and the first penalty against an exchange

The law allows the venues to sell faster data, but not to let it leak out first. Rule 603(a) of Regulation NMS requires exchanges to distribute market data on terms that are "fair and reasonable" and "not unreasonably discriminatory", which, the SEC wrote, "prohibits an exchange from releasing data relating to quotes and trades to its customers through proprietary feeds before it sends its quotes and trade reports for inclusion in the consolidated feeds" ([SEC, Release 34-67857, 14 September 2012](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2012/34-67857.pdf)).

In September 2012 the SEC found that the NYSE had broken that rule. From June 2008 one NYSE proprietary feed sent depth-of-book data, including its best prices and executions, to subscribers before the NYSE sent the same data to the processor, and in the first half of 2010 another feed did the same during high-volume intervals; "The disparities ranged from single-digit milliseconds to, on occasion, multiple seconds" (same order). The order gave three causes, all of them network or system design: an internal architecture that gave the proprietary feed "a path to customers that was faster than the path used to send quotes to the Network Processor", a second feed built to run independently of the system that fed the processor, and a software issue that delayed that system in periods of heavy volume. It added that "NYSE's compliance department played no role in the design, implementation, or operation of the systems", and that the NYSE had not kept the files that showed when it sent each quote to the processor (same order). The NYSE and NYSE Euronext settled without admitting or denying the findings, and the SEC announced that the US$5 million penalty "marks the first-ever SEC financial penalty against an exchange" ([SEC, press release 2012-189, 14 September 2012](https://www.sec.gov/news/press/2012/2012-189.htm)).

### What the gap is worth: two studies

Whether the gap matters to an ordinary investor is disputed, and the two best-known measurements point in different directions. A study in The Financial Review, which EconPapers lists under Michael Goldstein, Shengwei Ding, John Hanna and Terrence Hendershott, compared the national best bid and offer computed from the consolidated feed with the one computed from direct feeds at the same data centre and found that "Price dislocations between the NBBOs occur several times a second in very active stocks and typically last one to two milliseconds"; the authors concluded that "The short duration of dislocations makes their costs small for investors who trade infrequently, while the frequency of the dislocations makes them costly for frequent traders" ([How Slow Is the NBBO? A Comparison with Direct Exchange Feeds, The Financial Review, vol. 49 no. 2, 2014, abstract on EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:finrev:v:49:y:2014:i:2:p:313-332)). Robert Bartlett and Justin McCrary used the processors' own new time stamps and found that "Reporting latencies average 1.13 milliseconds for quotes and 22.84 milliseconds for trades", but that orders priced at the consolidated quote gained on average US$0.0002 a share against the quote computed from direct feeds, and wrote that "These findings contradict claims that fast traders systematically exploit traders who transact at the SIP NBBO" ([Bartlett and McCrary, How Rigged Are Stock Markets? Evidence From Microsecond Timestamps, NBER Working Paper 22551, August 2016](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w22551/w22551.pdf)), NBER being the National Bureau of Economic Research. The working paper states that it was not peer reviewed by the NBER (same paper). The wider dispute over latency arbitrage, and over whether high-frequency trading helps or harms other investors, is laid out in [High-frequency trading: what it is and the dispute](/learn/high-frequency-trading-what-it-is-and-the-dispute).

### When the processor fails

A consolidated feed is also a single point of failure. On 22 August 2013 Nasdaq halted trading in all Nasdaq-listed securities "for more than three hours" after the UTP processor "was unable to process quotes from exchanges for dissemination to the public"; according to Nasdaq, as the SEC recounted, more than 20 connect and disconnect sequences and a stream of quotes for inaccurate symbols from NYSE Arca, "26 times greater than usual activity", exceeded the processor's capacity, and the events "exposed a flaw in the SIP's software code which prevented a successful failover to the backup system" ([SEC, Regulation Systems Compliance and Integrity, Release 34-73639, 19 November 2014](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/final/2014/34-73639.pdf)). The same release, which adopted Regulation Systems Compliance and Integrity (Regulation SCI), records an options halt of about 20 minutes caused by a systems issue at the Options Price Reporting Authority (OPRA), and a network hardware failure on 30 October 2014 after which SIAC switched the CTS, CQS and OPRA feeds to its secondary data centre, and concludes that "the availability of consolidated market data is central to the functioning of the securities markets" (same release). The CTA now describes a backup server running "hot/hot" and a backup site with a 10-minute recovery requirement ([CTA Plan, overview](https://www.ctaplan.com/)); the UTP Plan describes its backup site as "hot/warm" and reports 100% availability in 54 of the last 64 quarters ([UTP Plan, overview](https://utpplan.com/PageParts/Overview.html)).

## The 2020 market data infrastructure rule and the litigation

### What the rule changed

On 9 December 2020 the SEC adopted rules to modernize an infrastructure that, in its words, "has not been significantly updated since its initial implementation in the late 1970s" ([SEC, press release 2020-311](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2020-311)). The rule, known as the market data infrastructure (MDI) rule, did two things. It widened what counts as core data, and it replaced the "exclusive SIP" model with "competing consolidators", entities that register with the SEC under a new Rule 614 and are subject to standards of "promptness, accuracy, reliability, and fairness", alongside "self-aggregators", broker-dealers and others that consolidate data only for their own use (same press release).

| Data | Before the rule | Under the rule |
|---|---|---|
| Round lot | 100 shares for most stocks | By average closing price: 100 shares up to US$250.00, 40 shares from US$250.01 to US$1,000.00, 10 shares from US$1,000.01 to US$10,000.00, 1 share above |
| Odd-lot quotations | Not included | Odd lots priced at or better than the NBBO, aggregated at each price at each venue |
| Depth of book | Not included | Sizes at each venue at the next five prices below the national best bid and above the national best offer |
| Auction information | Limited | Information generated by a self-regulatory organization (SRO) leading up to and during opening, reopening and closing auctions |

Source: [SEC, press release 2020-311, fact sheet](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2020-311).

### In court: the rule upheld, the governance plan struck down

Exchanges challenged the rule. The D.C. Circuit recorded their arguments: that the rule would "exacerbate, not reduce, information asymmetries", replacing "a two-tiered system with a multi-tiered system", and that it rested on speculation. It rejected them, holding that the SEC's stated goal was to "reduce information asymmetries" and that "The Rule, however, clearly represents a reasonable balancing of the objectives Congress directed the Commission to address in a complex and technical area based on the record before the Commission"; it denied the petitions on 24 May 2022 ([D.C. Circuit, No. 21-1100, 24 May 2022](https://media.cadc.uscourts.gov/opinions/docs/2022/05/21-1100-1947763.pdf)).

A parallel case about governance went the other way in part. On 6 May 2020 the SEC had ordered the exchanges and FINRA to submit a single new plan for consolidated equity market data ([SEC, Release 34-88827, 6 May 2020](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/sro/nms/2020/34-88827.pdf)), and in August 2021 it approved a plan, the CT Plan, that gave voting seats on its operating committee to representatives of non-SROs, grouped exchanges by corporate family for voting and required an independent administrator. On 5 July 2022 the D.C. Circuit granted the exchanges' petitions on the first point only, the non-SRO votes, found that provision not severable and vacated the order approving the CT Plan in its entirety, while upholding the 2020 order in large part ([D.C. Circuit, The Nasdaq Stock Market LLC v. SEC, No. 21-1167, decided 5 July 2022](https://media.cadc.uscourts.gov/opinions/docs/2022/07/21-1167-1953361.pdf)).

### What has been implemented, and what has not

The competing consolidators have not arrived. The implementation of the MDI rule was to begin with amendments to the existing plans; the operating committees filed them on 5 November 2021 and the SEC disapproved them, so that new amendments would have to be filed ([SEC, Regulation NMS: Minimum Pricing Increments, Access Fees, and Transparency of Better Priced Orders, Release 34-101070, Federal Register, 8 October 2024](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-10-08/pdf/2024-21867.pdf)). In the same 2024 release the SEC took two of the rule's content changes and ordered them through the existing processors: the round lot definition, now recalculated twice a year instead of monthly, on the first business day of November 2025, and odd-lot information on the first business day of May 2026, with the processors to represent quotation sizes "rounded down to the nearest multiple of a round lot" (same release). The processors told the SEC that they implemented the round lot definition in November 2025, and on 15 January 2026 the SEC exempted them until the first business day of May 2028 from publishing odd-lot quotations at every price level at each venue, leaving the best odd-lot order (BOLO), the highest odd-lot bid above the national best bid and the lowest odd-lot offer below the national best offer, to start in May 2026 ([SEC, Release 34-104612, 15 January 2026](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/exorders/2026/34-104612.pdf)). The processors' own page had asked for the delay because of "an unprecedented number of concurrent obligations and enhancements", including fractional shares, a possible move to 24-hour operation and half-penny ticks ([CTA Plan, Odd Lots](https://www.ctaplan.com/odd-lots)).

The governance change went ahead in another form. The administrator's site records that the 2020 order was amended in September 2023, that CT Plan LLC was approved in November 2024 and that DataCT LLC became the independent administrator ([US Consolidated Tape, transition page, read 16 November 2026](https://consolidatedtape.com)). The SEC's order of 20 November 2024 approved, with modifications, the single plan the SROs had filed on 23 October 2023 ([SEC, Release 34-101672, 20 November 2024](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/sro/nms/2024/34-101672_0.pdf)). On 26 June 2026 it approved the CT Plan's fee schedule, with modifications that require the plan to keep publishing quarterly population and revenue figures; as the order describes the fees, non-professional use is tiered from US$0.90 down to US$0.25 a person against the old flat US$1.00, and professional use is charged US$26 for Tape A, US$23 for Tape B and US$24 for Tape C per device, against a Tape A range of US$19 to US$45 under the old plans ([SEC, Release 34-105778, 26 June 2026](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/sro/nms/2026/34-105778.pdf)). The same day it approved extending the processors' hours from 9:00 p.m. Eastern Time on Sunday to 8:00 p.m. on Friday, with a one-hour pause at 8:00 p.m. from Monday to Thursday, as exchanges moved to trade 23 hours a day ([SEC, Release 34-105779, 26 June 2026](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/sro/nms/2026/34-105779.pdf)). The new plan becomes operative on 1 April 2027, and subscribers that have not signed the new agreements by 1 March 2027 "will not be authorized to receive US Consolidated Tape data after April 1, 2027"; the administrator states that "No technology changes are required" because the processors run by SIAC and Nasdaq stay as they are ([US Consolidated Tape, transition page](https://consolidatedtape.com)).

### Worked example: one stock, two round lots

The quotes below are invented to show the arithmetic; the rules are the SEC's. A stock whose average closing price is near US$480 falls in the US$250.01 to US$1,000.00 tier, so its round lot is 40 shares instead of 100. Each of three venues displays one order on each side:

| Venue | Bid | Bid size | Offer | Offer size |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 479.90 | 200 | 480.10 | 300 |
| B | 479.95 | 60 | 480.06 | 80 |
| C | 479.98 | 30 | 480.02 | 25 |

| Rule | National best bid | National best offer | Spread | Odd lots priced better (BOLO) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Round lot of 100 shares | 479.90 x 200 (A) | 480.10 x 300 (A) | US$0.20 | Not published |
| Round lot of 40 shares | 479.95 x 40 (B, 60 rounded down) | 480.06 x 80 (B) | US$0.11 | 479.98 x 30 bid, 480.02 x 25 offer (C) |

Under the old rule only venue A's orders count, because 60, 80, 30 and 25 shares are all odd lots. Under the tiered rule venue B's orders become round lots and set the national best bid and offer; their sizes are shown rounded down to a multiple of 40, so 60 shares appear as 40, and venue A's 300-share offer would appear as 280. Venue C's orders are still odd lots, so they cannot set the national best bid and offer, but because they are priced better they now appear as the best odd-lot order, a US$0.04 market inside the US$0.11 one. Two independent programs, one in Python and one in JavaScript, computed every figure in the second table and agree. A direct-feed subscriber saw all of this before; what changed is how much of it reaches the consolidated feed.

## Europe: from no tape to three

Europe's problem was the opposite of the American one: a consolidated tape was allowed but nobody built it. The 2024 review of the Markets in Financial Instruments Regulation (MiFIR) says so in its recitals: "To date, however, no supervised entity has applied for authorisation to act as a CTP", a consolidated tape provider (CTP), and it lists the three obstacles the European Securities and Markets Authority (ESMA) had found in 2019: how a provider would obtain the data, poor harmonization of the data, and a lack of commercial incentive ([Regulation (EU) 2024/791, 28 February 2024, recital 4](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R0791)). [The rules that rewired markets](/learn/the-rules-that-rewired-markets-reg-ats-reg-nms-mifid-and-brazil) records the review itself and the first two selections; the design and the launch are the subject here.

The regulation removed the obstacles by law. Trading venues and approved publication arrangements (APAs), the "data contributors", must transmit their data to the provider's data centre "as close to real time as technically possible", in a harmonized format (Article 22a, same regulation). The provider must give the tape free of charge "to retail investors, academics, civil society organisations and competent authorities", charge other users, and disseminate the data "as a continuous electronic live data stream on non-discriminatory terms as close to real time as technically possible" (Article 27h(1)). For shares and exchange-traded funds (ETFs) the core data includes the "European best bid and offer with the corresponding volume" (Article 2(1), point 36b), but the provider "shall not publish the market identifier code" (MIC) when disseminating it in real time (Article 27h(1)), so the public sees the best European price without the name of the venue that shows it. Part of the equity tape's revenue must be redistributed to data contributors that are small regulated markets or growth markets (1% or less of Union share volume), that admitted shares or ETFs to trading on or after 27 March 2019, or whose trades came from pre-trade transparent order books, with the first criterion weighted most and the last least (Article 27h(6) to (8)). Every venue and provider must also sell its own data "on a reasonable commercial basis" and publish its data policy free of charge (Article 13).

The tapes are now arriving in turn. ESMA launched the bond selection on 3 January 2025, selected Ediphy (fairCT) on 3 July 2025 and, on 16 November 2026, still listed its authorization as ongoing; it launched the equity selection on 20 June 2025, selected EuroCTP on 19 December 2025 and authorized it on 27 July 2026, and EuroCTP launched the first EU tape for shares and ETFs on 14 September 2026; the derivatives selection, launched on 5 January 2026, chose Etrading Software (Netherlands) B.V. on 6 July 2026 ([ESMA, Consolidated tape providers, read 16 November 2026](https://www.esma.europa.eu/esmas-activities/markets-and-infrastructure/consolidated-tape-providers)). ESMA gave EuroCTP a transition period to 30 September 2026 and a five-year term from the start of operations, and its executive director, Natasha Cazenave, said that "By bringing pre-trade and post-trade data from multiple data contributors into a single stream, the CTP for shares and ETFs will give market participants a comprehensive view of trading activity" ([ESMA, press release ESMA71-545613100-2975, 27 July 2026](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2026-07/ESMA71-545613100-2975%5FESMA%5Fauthorises%5FEuroCTP%5Fas%5Fthe%5FConsolidated%5FTape%5FProvider%5Ffor%5Fshares%5Fand%5Fexchange-traded%5Ffunds%5F-%5FPress%5Frelease.pdf)). The messages between contributors and providers are new International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 20022 messages approved for the purpose ([ESMA, Consolidated tape providers](https://www.esma.europa.eu/esmas-activities/markets-and-infrastructure/consolidated-tape-providers)).

The United Kingdom (UK) went its own way after leaving the Union. The Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) decided in April 2024 that "we wouldn't require bond CTPs to make payments to data providers", launched a tender on 7 March 2025, appointed ETS Connect UK in August 2025, was delayed by a legal challenge to the award until the High Court lifted the suspension in December 2025, signed the contract in January 2026, authorized the provider in May 2026, and saw the UK bond tape launched on 22 June 2026 ([FCA, Bond consolidated tape, last updated 3 August 2026](https://www.fca.org.uk/markets/data-reporting-services-providers/bond-consolidated-tape)).

## Brazil: one exchange, one feed, priced by use

### Why there is no consolidation problem, and what replaces it

Brazil has one stock exchange, so there is no tape to consolidate; [Brazil: BM&F, Bovespa, B3 and electronic access](/learn/brazil-bmf-bovespa-b3-and-electronic-access) tells how it came to be so. The questions that remain are the ones the American dispute shares: how fast the data is, who may receive it directly, and what it costs. B3's market data platform is the UMDF, which, as part 380 showed, B3 publishes in two encodings, FIX Adapted for STreaming (FAST) and Simple Binary Encoding (SBE), FIX being the Financial Information eXchange protocol. This part takes up where part 380 stopped: how the feed is laid out on the network and how a receiver recovers what it loses.

### A channel, three streams and three feeds

B3 delivers the binary feed over User Datagram Protocol (UDP) multicast, and its guidelines explain the consequence plainly: "The UDP protocol itself trades reliability for performance and does not guarantee the datagrams delivery", packets can also arrive out of order, and even a packet that reaches the receiving machine can be lost inside it ([B3, Binary UMDF guidelines 2.3.1.2, section 6.1](https://www.b3.com.br/data/files/84/B7/1D/9E/AB611A103BBB511AAC094EA8/BinaryUMDF-MessageSpecificationGuidelines-v.2.3.1.2-enUS.pdf)). A channel, "a logical group of multicast IP addresses, UDP ports", carries one group of instruments and is broken into three streams: incrementals, instrument definitions and snapshot recovery. Feed A carries all three; Feed B "only has the incremental stream", and its purpose is to mitigate packet loss for a client "properly listening on both feeds (A and B) simultaneously" that "processes the first received packet, discarding the second one"; Feed C comes from the disaster recovery site (same guidelines). The multicast data is available "through the RCB and inside B3's datacenter for co-location customers", the RCB being the Rede de Comunicação B3, the exchange's own network (same guidelines, section 6.5).

B3 publishes the addresses. Its connectivity spreadsheet for the binary feed lists, for production, Feed A in 233.252.8.0/24, Feed B in 233.252.9.0/24 and the disaster recovery Feed C in 233.252.15.0/24, each with a rendezvous point (RP) address, which means the groups are routed with Protocol Independent Multicast in sparse mode (PIM-SM) rather than as source-specific groups; the ports follow a pattern built on the channel number ([B3, Binary UMDF, IP address and connectivity data, spreadsheet linked from the Binary UMDF page](https://www.b3.com.br/data/files/8D/60/3E/0D/630FD810066AFDD8AC094EA8/Binary%20UMDF%20-%20IP%20Address%20-%20Connectivity%20data.xlsx); [B3, Binary UMDF page](https://www.b3.com.br/en_us/solutions/platforms/puma-trading-system/for-developers-and-vendors/binary-umdf)). For equities channel 80:

| Stream | Feed A | Feed B | Feed C (disaster recovery) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Snapshot recovery | 233.252.8.106, port 30080 | none | 233.252.15.106, port 30080 |
| Incrementals | 233.252.8.107, port 20080 | 233.252.9.107, port 20080 | 233.252.15.107, port 20080 |
| Instrument definition | 233.252.8.108, port 10080 | none | 233.252.15.108, port 10080 |

Source: [B3, Binary UMDF IP address and connectivity data](https://www.b3.com.br/data/files/8D/60/3E/0D/630FD810066AFDD8AC094EA8/Binary%20UMDF%20-%20IP%20Address%20-%20Connectivity%20data.xlsx). The same spreadsheet lists the rendezvous points as 200.19.57.252 for Feed A, 200.19.58.252 for Feed B and 200.19.59.252 for Feed C. How rendezvous points and source-specific multicast differ, and what the switches and routers must hold for every group, is explained in [Multicast: what it costs to not flood](/learn/multicast-what-it-costs-to-not-flood).

### Who owns 233.252.8.0

Multicast addresses are registered, and the registry tells the history of exchange data on the internet. In the Internet Assigned Numbers Authority (IANA) registry, 233.252.8.0 to 233.252.11.255 is registered as "BVMF_MKT_DATA" on 12 August 2010 and 233.252.14.0 to 233.252.17.255 as "BVMF_MKT_DATA_2" on 27 September 2016, both in the block IANA calls AD-HOC Block III, 233.252.0.0/14; the same block holds two ranges for Pico, the trading network company, and one for Tradition, the interdealer broker; older blocks record Nasdaq groups from 1998, Options Price Reporting Authority groups from 2005 and "CME Market Data" ranges from 2007, CME being the Chicago Mercantile Exchange ([IANA, IPv4 Multicast Address Space Registry, last updated 6 November 2026](https://www.iana.org/assignments/multicast-addresses/multicast-addresses.txt)). BVMF is the abbreviation B3's older documents use for BM&FBovespa, its predecessor; the FAST specification still writes that "BVMF does not support the usage of this feed" for anything but charting ([B3, UMDF FIX/FAST specification 2.2.2](https://b3.com.br/data/files/EC/B1/9A/AD/2E86F910FC0436F9AC094EA8/UMDF_MarketDataSpecification_v2.2.2.pdf)).

The certification environment uses a different kind of address. Its feeds sit in 233.205.192.0/24 ([B3, Binary UMDF IP address and connectivity data](https://www.b3.com.br/data/files/8D/60/3E/0D/630FD810066AFDD8AC094EA8/Binary%20UMDF%20-%20IP%20Address%20-%20Connectivity%20data.xlsx)). That is GLOP addressing, under which the 16 bits after 233 are an autonomous system (AS) number, which "allows a single /24 per AS" ([IETF, RFC 3180, GLOP Addressing in 233/8, September 2001](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc3180.txt)), a Request for Comments (RFC) of the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF). The arithmetic is 205 x 256 + 192 = 52672, and the Brazilian registry lists AS 52672 with "B3 S.A. Brasil, Bolsa, Balcão" as registrant ([Registro.br, RDAP record for AS 52672, read 16 November 2026](https://rdap.registro.br/autnum/52672)), RDAP being the Registration Data Access Protocol. On an Ethernet segment, the incremental group 233.252.8.107 maps to the media access control (MAC) address 01:00:5e:7c:08:6b, the low 23 bits of the group behind the 01:00:5e prefix; because the top bit of the second octet is dropped, 32 different groups share each multicast MAC address, and the computation can be checked with this site's multicast MAC mapper.

### Losing a packet, and getting the book back

Each binary datagram starts with a 16-byte packet header: channel, a sequence version and a sequence number "Always incremented by one in the same channel and same SequenceVersion", and a sending time in nanoseconds ([B3, Binary UMDF guidelines 2.3.1.2, section 6.5.5.1](https://www.b3.com.br/data/files/84/B7/1D/9E/AB611A103BBB511AAC094EA8/BinaryUMDF-MessageSpecificationGuidelines-v.2.3.1.2-enUS.pdf)). A receiver detects loss by watching that number, and there are three remedies, in order. The first is arbitration: B3 "strongly suggest[s]" that clients take both incremental feeds and process the first datagram received (section 6.1). The second, only for the FAST feed, is retransmission over the Transmission Control Protocol (TCP): a global "TCP Replayer" lets a client request a range of messages with ApplicationMessageRequest (35=BW), receive an acknowledgement (35=BX), the resent messages wrapped in Application Raw Data Reporting messages (35=URDR) and a closing ApplicationMessageReport (35=BY), and B3 warns that "This method of recovery should only be used if few messages were lost" ([B3, UMDF FIX/FAST Market Data Messaging Specification, version 2.2.2, 9 July 2026, section 4.1.4](https://b3.com.br/data/files/EC/B1/9A/AD/2E86F910FC0436F9AC094EA8/UMDF_MarketDataSpecification_v2.2.2.pdf)). A separate historical replayer serves messages back to sequence number 1 of the week, at most 2,000 a request, and B3 says it "does not support the usage of this feed for any other purpose than offline charting" (same specification, section 4.1.5). The binary feed has no TCP recovery at all: B3 lists the "TCP Recovery System (UMDF TCP Replayer)" among the FAST features it does not carry ([B3, Binary UMDF guidelines 2.3.1.2, section 4](https://www.b3.com.br/data/files/84/B7/1D/9E/AB611A103BBB511AAC094EA8/BinaryUMDF-MessageSpecificationGuidelines-v.2.3.1.2-enUS.pdf)).

The third remedy is the snapshot. The snapshot stream loops through the channel's instruments, each snapshot carrying the book, the latest statistics and the instrument's state, valid as of a sequence number of the incremental feed given in LastMsgSeqNumProcessed (369) and LastSequenceVersion (37084); it is meant for "large-scale data recovery (i.e. major outage or late joiners)", and the client must queue incrementals while it collects the snapshots, then discard every queued message whose sequence number is at or below the snapshot's, and unjoin the snapshot group afterwards (same guidelines, sections 6.3 and 7.4). B3 adds that "Client systems must handle instruments with no snapshots as having empty order books and no statistical data before applying incremental data" (section 6.3), and that a client following only a few instruments can use RptSeq (83), a sequence number per instrument, to tell whether a lost packet touched them at all (section 7.4). Silence is also a signal: the binary feed sends a Sequence message when no data has flowed for one second, and a client that hears nothing for three intervals must treat the book as inconsistent (section 6.2); the FAST feed uses ten seconds and thirty ([B3, UMDF FIX/FAST specification 2.2.2, section 4.1.1](https://b3.com.br/data/files/EC/B1/9A/AD/2E86F910FC0436F9AC094EA8/UMDF_MarketDataSpecification_v2.2.2.pdf)).

Other venues answer the same problem with different mixes:

### Worked example: a gap on both feeds

The packet contents and numbers below are invented; the rules are B3's. Channel 80 carries two instruments, 1111 and 2222, and each packet holds one incremental message. Feed A loses packets 703, 706 and 707; Feed B loses 705 and 707.

| Step | What the receiver sees | Rule applied | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 701 to 713 on A and B, first copy kept | Arbitration (section 6.1) | 703, 705 and 706 recovered from the other feed |
| 2 | 707 missing on both | Sequence number gap (section 6.5.5.1) | Channel out of sync |
| 3 | Instrument 2222: RptSeq 4399 to 4404 with no hole | RptSeq per instrument (section 7.4) | A client following only 2222 needs no recovery |
| 4 | Instrument 1111: RptSeq jumps from 6998 to 7000 | Same rule | A client following 1111 must recover |
| 5 | Join snapshot group, queue incrementals 708 to 713 | Snapshot recovery (section 7.4) | Snapshots: 1111 valid at 711 (RptSeq 7001), 2222 valid at 710 (RptSeq 4403) |
| 6 | Oldest queued packet 708 is below 710, the lowest snapshot position | Wait condition (section 7.4, step 3) | Queue can be applied |
| 7 | 708, 709, 710 and 711 | Discard if at or below the instrument's LastMsgSeqNumProcessed | All four discarded |
| 8 | 712 (2222, RptSeq 4404) and 713 (1111, RptSeq 7002) | Apply | Both books current; unjoin the snapshot group |

The same scenario was run through two independent programs, in Python and in JavaScript, which reproduce every row, including the check that each applied message carries the next RptSeq for its instrument.

## What the dispute is about

Both sides of the market data dispute have a coherent case, and the record shows each in its own words.

**The case for the venues' model.** Exchanges produce the data, invest in the systems that publish it and compete with each other and with off-exchange venues; the D.C. Circuit recorded their argument that the 2020 rule would turn "a two-tiered system" into "a multi-tiered system" and make asymmetries worse ([D.C. Circuit, No. 21-1100](https://media.cadc.uscourts.gov/opinions/docs/2022/05/21-1100-1947763.pdf)). The processors argue that the consolidated tape is cheap enough for brokers to give real-time prices to most retail customers ([CTA Plan, overview](https://www.ctaplan.com/)), and the study by Bartlett and McCrary found little evidence that fast traders exploit orders priced at the consolidated quote ([Bartlett and McCrary, 2016](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w22551/w22551.pdf)).

**The case for consolidation and competition.** The SEC found an "information asymmetry" between users of core data and users of proprietary feeds and set out to reduce it, and the court accepted that aim ([D.C. Circuit, No. 21-1100](https://media.cadc.uscourts.gov/opinions/docs/2022/05/21-1100-1947763.pdf)); the 2014 study found dislocations several times a second that are costly to frequent traders ([The Financial Review, 2014](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:finrev:v:49:y:2014:i:2:p:313-332)); and the NYSE case showed that a venue's architecture can give its paying customers a head start ([SEC, 2012](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2012/34-67857.pdf)). Europe's answer, a single tape with free access for retail investors and revenue shared with small venues, is a choice of the same kind made by statute ([Regulation (EU) 2024/791](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R0791)).

Surveillance depends on the same data: the regulators' order book reconstructions, suspicious order reports and audit trails described in [the next part of this series](/learn/surveillance-and-regulatory-reporting-mifid-ii-cat-and-cvm) all start from the feeds and the records behind them. How firms capture and time stamp the feeds on their own networks is the subject of [Network-derived analytics: watching trading on the wire](/learn/network-derived-analytics-and-trading-monitoring).

## What to remember

- Market data comes twice where venues compete: a consolidated feed under the regulator's rules and each venue's direct feed, which is sold separately, carries more detail and usually arrives first.
- In the United States two processors, under the CTA, CQ and UTP plans, publish the national best bid and offer since the late 1970s; their published latency is measured inside the processor, not from the matching engine to the user.
- The SEC's 2020 rule on market data infrastructure was upheld in 2022, but its competing consolidators are not live; round lots by price tier took effect in November 2025, the best odd-lot order in May 2026, odd-lot depth is deferred to May 2028, and a single CT Plan with new fees starts on 1 April 2027.
- Europe went from no tape to an equity tape on 14 September 2026, with free access for retail investors, the best European price published without the venue's name and revenue shared with small venues; bond and derivatives tapes are being authorized, and the UK launched a bond tape on 22 June 2026.
- B3 needs no consolidator but sells its single feed by use and location; its binary feed recovers lost packets only by feed arbitration and snapshot loops, while the older FAST feed also offers TCP replay for small losses.
- Multicast addresses tell their own history: B3's production feeds sit in ranges IANA registered as "BVMF_MKT_DATA", and its certification feeds in the GLOP block of B3's own autonomous system.
