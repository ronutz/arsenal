A falsehood can be answered in two ways: after it spreads, by checking it and publishing the result, or before it arrives, by warning people about the tricks it will use. This part of the series opened by [the map from the small lie to the campaign](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign) covers the first: how professional fact-checkers say they work, the code that certifies them, the reading technique that sets them apart from other experts, the standards that record where a digital file came from, what the experiments find when people read a correction, and how Meta's fact-checking programme was dismantled in the United States (US) and is being tested for replacement in Latin America. The [next part](/learn/prebunking-and-inoculation) covers the second, warning people in advance. Why corrections do not erase what they correct is set out in [the human attack surface](/learn/the-human-attack-surface-why-repetition-works).

## Three activities under one name

**Fact-checking**, in the definition of the International Fact-Checking Network (IFCN), a unit of the Poynter Institute in the US, is the work of organizations that "regularly publish non-partisan reports on the accuracy of statements by public figures and prominent institutions and other widely circulated claims related to public interest issues" ([IFCN, the commitments of the Code of Principles](https://ifcncodeofprinciples.poynter.org/the-commitments)). The object is a claim, and the product is a published report with its evidence.

**Debunking** extends the method from what public figures say to what circulates anonymously. The Brazilian agency Lupa, which describes its method as built on those of the Argentine Chequeado and the American PolitiFact, says it widened its work in May 2018 "para o campo do debunking" (into debunking), which it defines as "verificação de conteúdo publicado por fontes não oficiais" (the verification of content published by unofficial sources), when it joined Facebook's third-party fact-checking project ([Lupa, Como a Lupa faz suas checagens, updated 18 November 2025](https://www.agencialupa.org/noticias/2015/10/15/como-fazemos-nossas-checagens/)).

**Verification** is the reporting discipline underneath both: establishing who made a piece of content, when and where, before saying anything about it. Aos Fatos, another Brazilian fact-checker, writes that although it is best known for checks made after the fact ("ex-post"), its investigations are not published without verification methods applied before publication ("ex-ante"), so that everything it publishes is confirmed by one or more primary sources ([Aos Fatos, Política editorial](https://www.aosfatos.org/politica-editorial/)).

The machine-readable trace of the first activity is ClaimReview, a vocabulary defined at Schema.org as "A fact-checking review of claims made (or reported) in some creative work" ([Schema.org, ClaimReview](https://schema.org/ClaimReview)); Google announced on 12 June 2025 that ClaimReview was among the structured data types that "will no longer be supported in Google Search results", because, it said, those displays were "not commonly used in Search" and "no longer providing significant additional value for users" ([Google Search Central Blog, 12 June 2025, updated 8 September 2025](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2025/06/simplifying-search-results)).

## The code that certifies fact-checkers

The IFCN Code of Principles sets five commitments, and "Applications are assessed by independent assessors for compliance with 31 criteria", with the assessments reviewed by an advisory board ([IFCN, the commitments](https://ifcncodeofprinciples.poynter.org/the-commitments)). Signatory status "may not be granted to organizations whose editorial work is controlled by the state, a political party or politician", though organizations with state or political funding may qualify if there is "clear and unambiguous separation of editorial control".

| Commitment | What the criteria require, among other things |
|---|---|
| Non-partisanship and fairness | The same standards of evidence for equivalent claims "regardless of who made the claim"; not unduly concentrating on one side; a published statement on how claims are selected; no advocacy on the issues checked |
| Standards and transparency of sources | Sources given in enough detail that readers can replicate the work; primary over secondary sources; key elements checked against more than one named source |
| Transparency of funding and organization | A page listing each source of funding of 5% or more of revenue; ownership, structure and the biographies of editorial staff |
| Standards and transparency of methodology | A published methodology; claims chosen by reach and importance; evidence for and against the claim; contacting the person who made it where possible |
| Open and honest corrections policy | A visible corrections policy, followed "scrupulously", with users told they may complain to the IFCN |

The IFCN itself says it "does not publish fact checks and is therefore not eligible to be a signatory of its own Code of Principles", and publishes a transparency report instead ([IFCN, About](https://ifcncodeofprinciples.poynter.org/about)). The code is the reason the platform programmes discussed below can say they work with "independent" fact-checkers: Facebook's 2016 programme was open to "third-party fact checking organizations that are signatories of Poynter's International Fact Checking Code of Principles" ([Facebook, News Feed FYI: Addressing Hoaxes and Fake News, 15 December 2016](https://about.fb.com/news/2016/12/news-feed-fyi-addressing-hoaxes-and-fake-news/)).

## How three Brazilian fact-checkers say they work

Three Brazilian fact-checking operations publish their methods in detail. What follows is what each says about itself.

### Comprova: a collaboration that checks across newsrooms

The Projeto Comprova describes itself as a collaborative, non-partisan, non-profit initiative led by the Associação Brasileira de Jornalismo Investigativo (Abraji, the Brazilian Association of Investigative Journalism) that brings together journalists from 42 media outlets ([Comprova, Sobre o Comprova](https://projetocomprova.com.br/about/)). It was conceived by First Draft with Abraji, Projor, the Google News Initiative and the Meta Journalism Project, and was created to operate during the 2018 election period ([Comprova, Transparência](https://projetocomprova.com.br/about/transparencia/); [Comprova, Metodologia](https://projetocomprova.com.br/about/metodologia/)).

Its method rests on what it calls "checagem cruzada" (cross-checking): an investigation is normally carried out by three journalists from different member outlets, and the report is published only when at least three other participating newsrooms have reviewed and validated the verification steps and conclusions ([Comprova, Metodologia](https://projetocomprova.com.br/about/metodologia/)). It chooses what to check by reach or potential reach, measured by monitoring tools, across elections, public policy, health, climate change and digital scams, and it does not investigate content that originates from a news outlet, on the ground that newsrooms have their own correction policies.

On money, Comprova says the Google News Initiative and the Meta Journalism Project have supported it financially since 2018, WhatsApp since 2021, the United States embassy in Brazil funded two projects, and TikTok contributed in 2023; it raised R$635,000 in 2023 and had a budget of R$1.02 million in 2024, with Google as master sponsor, and states that funders have no say in editorial decisions ([Comprova, Transparência](https://projetocomprova.com.br/about/transparencia/)).

### Lupa: seven labels and a public trail

Lupa says it was founded in November 2015 as the "primeira agência de notícias especializada em checagem do Brasil" (Brazil's first news agency specializing in fact-checking), has been in the IFCN since 2017 with an annual audit, and had revenue above R$4 million in 2024 from contracts, adding that it "não vende checagens e verificações" (does not sell fact-checks) ([Lupa, Sobre a Lupa](https://www.agencialupa.org/sobre-a-lupa/)). It selects statements by three criteria, who speaks, what is said and how much noise it makes ("quem fala", "o que fala" e "que barulho faz"); it does not check opinions or predictions; it uses Brazil's access to information law when public data are missing; it gives the person checked time to respond (a step that may be skipped in debunking); it uses no anonymous sources, on the principle that "é o leitor quem checa o checador" (the reader checks the checker); and since January 2021 it reserves the right to say a public figure is lying when the same false statement is repeated ([Lupa, Como a Lupa faz suas checagens](https://www.agencialupa.org/noticias/2015/10/15/como-fazemos-nossas-checagens/)).

Since November 2023 it has used seven labels: VERDADEIRO (true), FALSO (false), FALTA CONTEXTO (lacks context), EXAGERADO (exaggerated), SUBESTIMADO (understated), CONTRADITÓRIO (contradicts an earlier statement by the same source) and INSUSTENTÁVEL (no public data support it); it dropped "De olho", "Ainda é cedo" and "Verdadeiro, mas…" at that time, and it says no check is published without being read by at least three people ([Lupa, Entenda as etiquetas da Lupa, updated 4 February 2026](https://www.agencialupa.org/acontecendo-na-lupa/2015/10/15/entenda-nossas-etiquetas/)).

### Aos Fatos: three seals and an explicit stance on power

Aos Fatos says it first went online on 7 July 2015 and is an IFCN signatory ([Aos Fatos, Sobre o Aos Fatos](https://www.aosfatos.org/sobre-o-aos-fatos/)). It uses three seals: VERDADEIRO, when the core of the claim matches facts reported by reliable primary sources; NÃO É BEM ASSIM (not quite), for information out of context, inflated, altered or lacking sources; and FALSO, when "Ao menos duas fontes primárias, além de dados e fatos passados, apontam o oposto" (at least two primary sources, plus past data and facts, point to the opposite) ([Aos Fatos, Política editorial](https://www.aosfatos.org/politica-editorial/)). Every check passes at least two editors, and all journalists involved must agree on the seal. It chooses claims by the influence of the speaker and the potential harm of the falsehood, does not check opinions, predictions or promises, and states that while it investigates misleading information from all sides of the political spectrum, its journalists believe that "quem está no poder deve e merece estar sob escrutínio preferencial" (those in power should and deserve to be under preferential scrutiny).

| | Comprova | Lupa | Aos Fatos |
|---|---|---|---|
| Founded | 2018 (election project) | November 2015 | July 2015 |
| Who checks | Journalists from 42 outlets | Its own newsroom | Its own newsroom |
| Review before publication | At least three other newsrooms validate | At least three readers, double editing | At least two editors, consensus on the seal |
| Labels | Not stated on the pages read | Seven, since November 2023 | Three |
| What it will not check | Content from news outlets | Opinions, predictions | Opinions, predictions, promises |

## Reading laterally: what fact-checkers do that other experts do not

A study of how professionals judge online sources, the one that named lateral reading, came from the Stanford History Education Group (SHEG). Sam Wineburg and Sarah McGrew observed 45 people, "10 Ph.D. historians, 10 professional fact checkers, and 25 Stanford University undergraduates", as they evaluated live websites. Historians and students "read vertically, staying within a website to evaluate its reliability. In contrast, fact checkers read laterally, leaving a site after a quick scan and opening up new browser tabs in order to judge the credibility of the original site" ([Wineburg and McGrew, working paper, September 2017](https://www.hendrix.edu/uploadedFiles/Academics/Faculty_Resources/Teaching_and_Learning/EvaluatingDigitalInformation.pdf); published in [Teachers College Record, November 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/016146811912101102)).

One task compared the site of the 64,000-member American Academy of Pediatrics with that of the American College of Pediatricians, whose positions the fact-checkers quickly found described elsewhere as socially conservative. Fact-checkers left the College's landing page in about half a minute on average (32 seconds), historians took almost three times as long (88 seconds), and the students who left it took 100 seconds, while nine never did. "Every fact checker unreservedly viewed the Academy's site as the more reliable; historians often equivocated, expressing the belief that both sites were reliable; and students overwhelmingly judged the College's site the more reliable" ([Wineburg and McGrew, 2017](https://www.hendrix.edu/uploadedFiles/Academics/Faculty_Resources/Teaching_and_Learning/EvaluatingDigitalInformation.pdf)). On a site about the minimum wage, the fact-checkers visited an average of six other sites before concluding that it and the institute behind it "were cloaked sites that represented corporate interests".

Two habits stood out. "Paradoxically, a key feature of lateral reading is not reading": the checkers ignored most of the page in front of them. And they "showed click restraint", reading search results and their addresses before choosing what to open, because "A searcher's first click is often destiny".

Later work measured the gap at scale and tested whether the habit can be taught. An assessment of 3,446 US high school students with live internet access found that, investigating a site claiming to "disseminate factual reports" on climate science, "96% never learned about the organization's ties to the fossil fuel industry", and that "More than half believed that an anonymously posted Facebook video, shot in Russia, provided 'strong evidence' of U.S. voter fraud" ([Breakstone et al., Educational Researcher, 2021](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X211017495)). At the City University of New York (CUNY), sections of a civics course that taught the Digital Polarization Initiative (DPI) curriculum (136 students) were compared with sections taught as usual by the same instructors (94 students); at the end, the DPI students "were more likely to use lateral reading to fact-check and correctly evaluate the trustworthiness of information than controls", and the authors note that whether the gain lasts remains to be studied ([Brodsky et al., Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications, 31 March 2021](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41235-021-00291-4)).

### SIFT

The DPI curriculum drew on the work of Mike Caulfield, then at Washington State University Vancouver and a co-author of the CUNY study, whose "four moves" were framed for students as "quick skills to help you verify and contextualize web content" ([Brodsky et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41235-021-00291-4)). In June 2019 Caulfield renamed them SIFT, writing that "Since 2017, we've been teaching students with something called the Four Moves" ([Caulfield, SIFT (The Four Moves), 19 June 2019](https://hapgood.us/2019/06/19/sift-the-four-moves/)):

1. **Stop.** "Don't read it or share media until you know what it is."
2. **Investigate the source.** "you want to know what you're reading before you read it."
3. **Find better coverage.** "your best strategy may be to ignore the source that reached you".
4. **Trace claims, quotes, and media back to the original context.** "we'll have you trace the claim, quote, or media back to the source".

### Tips are not always enough

Teaching tips has measured but uneven effects. A survey experiment modelled on a set of "tips" on spotting false news that had run in 14 countries improved discernment between mainstream and false headlines "by 26.5%" in a nationally representative US sample and "by 17.5%" in a highly educated online sample in India, but found "no effects among a representative sample of respondents in a largely rural area of northern India" ([Guess et al., Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1920498117)). In a field experiment in India during the 2019 elections (N = 1,224), an hour-long in-person media literacy training "did not significantly increase respondents' ability to identify misinformation on average", and supporters of the ruling party "became significantly less able to identify pro-attitudinal stories" ([Badrinathan, American Political Science Review, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055421000459)). A 2024 review compiled the evidence from 81 papers into nine types of individual-level interventions, among them "lateral reading and verification strategies", "media-literacy tips" and "warning and fact-checking labels" ([Kozyreva et al., Nature Human Behaviour, 13 May 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-01881-0)).

## Provenance: what a Content Credential proves

Lateral reading judges a source by what others say about it. Provenance standards try to attach the history to the file itself. The Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA) publishes the specification for Content Credentials, which it describes as "a cryptographically bound structure that records an asset's provenance", made of assertions about the file; the structure is signed with the private key of the software or hardware that made or edited the file and can be embedded in the file or found through a "soft binding" such as an invisible watermark or a fingerprint lookup ([C2PA, Content Credentials explainer, version 2.3](https://spec.c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.3/explainer/Explainer.html)). The Content Credentials site says the effort, in which the British Broadcasting Corporation (BBC) takes part, has "grown to a collaboration with hundreds of companies led by Microsoft, Adobe, Intel, BBC, Truepic, Sony, Publicis Groupe, OpenAI, Google, Meta, and Amazon" ([Content Credentials](https://contentcredentials.org/)).

The C2PA is explicit about the limits:

- Content Credentials "do not provide value judgments about whether a given set of provenance data is 'true', but instead merely whether the provenance information is well-formed and free from tampering, valid and trusted".
- "provenance information alone cannot tell you whether the digital content is true, accurate or factual."
- "It is not a cure-all for misinformation"; it "complements media literacy, fact-checking, and digital forensics approaches such as deep-fake detection".
- Asked whether provenance metadata can be removed, the explainer answers "Yes it can", which is why it pairs cryptographic hashing with watermarks and fingerprints so a credential can be found again "even if it is removed from the asset".
- Adding provenance is optional, and the specification does not intend "a two-tier media ecosystem where assets without Content Credentials are universally less trusted".
- Provenance is "not always complete": a file cropped in a tool that does not support the standard leaves no record of the crop ([C2PA explainer 2.3](https://spec.c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.3/explainer/Explainer.html)).

The US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), in a November 2024 report on content generated or altered by algorithms, including artificial intelligence (AI), reached the same conclusion: digital content transparency "may contribute to trustworthiness but does not guarantee it, and in some cases may undermine it", because it "can also create a false sense of trust, such as when a piece of content appears legitimate based on technical measures but is then manipulated through non-technical means (e.g., taking a legitimate piece of content out of context)"; and metadata recorded in a file "can similarly be stripped altogether, as it often is when files are shared (e.g., via social media platforms)" ([NIST AI 100-4, Reducing Risks Posed by Synthetic Content, November 2024](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.100-4.pdf)). In short, a credential can show that a file is what its signer says it is; whether the scene in it means what a caption claims is still a question for verification. Synthetic voices and video are taken up in [voice clones and deepfakes in fraud](/learn/voice-clones-and-deepfakes-in-fraud).

## What a correction does to belief

Experiments and meta-analyses agree on the direction and disagree on the size, and the effect depends on the topic.

- **30 studies.** "Fact-checking has a significantly positive overall influence on political beliefs (d = 0.29), but the effects gradually weaken when using 'truth scales,' refuting only parts of a claim, and fact-checking campaign-related statements", and the effect is "substantially attenuated by participants' preexisting beliefs, ideology, and knowledge" ([Walter, Cohen, Holbert and Morag, Political Communication, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/10584609.2019.1668894)).
- **52 studies.** Debunking had large effects (ds = 1.14 to 1.33), but so did the persistence of misinformation after debunking (ds = 0.75 to 1.06); "A detailed debunking message correlated positively with the debunking effect. Surprisingly, however, a detailed debunking message also correlated positively with the misinformation-persistence effect" ([Chan, Jones, Hall Jamieson and Albarracín, Psychological Science, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797617714579)).
- **65 studies.** Corrections had a moderate effect (r = .35) but less in politics (r = .15) than in health (r = .27), less on real-world misinformation (r = .14) than on misinformation built for the experiment (r = .48), and "appeals to coherence (r = .55) outperform fact-checking (r = .25)" ([Walter and Murphy, Communication Monographs, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1080/03637751.2018.1467564)).
- **Four countries.** Simultaneous experiments in Argentina, Nigeria, South Africa and the United Kingdom (UK) with 22 fact-checks found that fact-checks "reduced belief in misinformation by at least 0.59 points on a 5-point scale", most effects lasting more than two weeks, while exposure to the misinformation raised false belief by less than 0.07 points ([Porter and Wood, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2104235118)).
- **Decay.** Brendan Nyhan reviews evidence that the gains "often do not last or accumulate; instead, they frequently seem to decay or be overwhelmed by cues from elites and the media promoting more congenial but less accurate claims" ([Nyhan, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1912440117)).

**Brazil is the exception on record.** A survey experiment during the 2018 Brazilian elections found that "fact-checking corrections in Brazil are ineffective at reducing misinformation. They fail even when they are most likely to work: among nonpartisans and when they confirm individuals' political predispositions"; the authors conclude that partisan motivated reasoning "is not the main driving force behind the (in)effectiveness of corrections" there ([Batista Pereira, Bueno, Nunes and Pavão, The Journal of Politics, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1086/719419)).

### Labels, and the labels that are missing

On platforms, a fact-check reaches most people as a label. Labels have a side effect: in experiments with 5,271 and 1,568 participants, warnings reduced the perceived accuracy of the false headlines they were attached to, but "the presence of warnings caused untagged headlines to be seen as more accurate than in the control", an "implied truth effect" that disappeared when some true headlines were also marked as verified ([Pennycook, Bear, Collins and Rand, Management Science, November 2020](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2019.3478)). Across 21 experiments (N = 14,133), warning labels from fact-checkers reduced belief in false headlines by 27.6% and sharing by 24.7%, and still reduced belief by 12.9% and sharing by 16.7% "even for those most distrusting of fact-checkers" ([Martel and Rand, Nature Human Behaviour, 2 September 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-01973-x)).

On spread rather than belief, a study by researchers at Sciences Po in Paris, one of whom worked as a fact-checker at Agence France-Presse (AFP) and collected 944 checked stories over 18 months, found according to Chequeado's summary that <Claim status="reported" by="by Chequeado, summarizing the study">fact-checking reduced the circulation of misinformation on Facebook by about 8%, an effect driven by stories rated false</Claim>, with posts more likely to be deleted and users who were flagged less likely to share misinformation afterwards ([Chequeado, 9 January 2026](https://chequeado.com/el-explicador/la-verificacion-de-datos-reduce-la-circulacion-de-desinformacion-en-facebook-en-aproximadamente-un-8/)). The working paper itself, by Julia Cagé, Nathan Gallo, Moritz Hengel, Emeric Henry and Yuchen Huang, covers December 2021 to June 2023 with two difference-in-differences designs, reports that fact-checking "reduces the circulation of misinformation on Facebook by approximately 8%, an effect driven entirely by stories rated as 'False'", and finds that a fact-check "more than doubles the deletion of posts in the fact-checked stories" ([Cagé et al., SSRN, December 2025](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5868423)). Chequeado discloses that it has been part of Meta's fact-checking programme since 2018 ([Chequeado, 10 September 2026](https://chequeado.com/nota/meta-comienza-a-probar-las-notas-de-la-comunidad-en-america-latina-que-dice-la-evidencia-sobre-su-efectividad)).

| Question | What the studies read here support |
|---|---|
| Do fact-checks lower belief in the claim they correct? | Yes, on average, in many countries; less in politics and on real-world claims |
| Do the effects last? | Often for weeks in experiments; they decay and are overridden by elite cues |
| Do they work everywhere? | Not in the 2018 Brazilian experiment, even among nonpartisans |
| Do labels work for people who distrust fact-checkers? | Yes, at about half the effect |
| Can labels backfire on unlabelled content? | Yes, the implied truth effect, unless true items are labelled too |

## The case against fact-checking, and the replies

The criticism is older than Meta's decision and comes from scholars as well as politicians. Joseph Uscinski and Ryden Butler argued in 2013 that fact-checking "employs a variety of objectionable methodological practices, such as treating a statement containing multiple facts as if it were a single fact and categorizing as accurate or inaccurate predictions of events yet to occur", resting on "the tacit presupposition that there cannot be genuine political debate about facts" ([Uscinski and Butler, Critical Review, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1080/08913811.2013.843872)). Chloe Lim compared two major US fact-checkers and found that "only one in 10 statements was found to be fact-checked by both", that they agreed on outright falsehoods and obvious truths but much less "for statements in the more ambiguous scoring range", and that "the strategic ambiguity of politicians may impede the fact-checking movement's goals" ([Lim, Research and Politics, July 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/2053168018786848)). A later comparison of 22,349 articles from Snopes and PolitiFact found that "Generally, the two fact checkers agreed with each other, with only one conflicting verdict among 749 matching claims after adjusting minor rating differences" ([Lee, Xiong, Seo and Lee, Harvard Kennedy School Misinformation Review, 26 October 2023](https://doi.org/10.37016/mr-2020-126)).

Perceptions split along party lines. In a 2019 Pew Research Center survey, "Seven-in-ten Republicans say fact-checkers tend to favor one side, compared with roughly three-in-ten Democrats (29%)" ([Pew Research Center, 27 June 2019](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2019/06/27/republicans-far-more-likely-than-democrats-to-say-fact-checkers-tend-to-favor-one-side/)). Selection is where the two readings meet: the IFCN code asks signatories not to "unduly" concentrate on one side and to publish how they select claims ([IFCN](https://ifcncodeofprinciples.poynter.org/the-commitments)), while Aos Fatos states openly that those in power deserve preferential scrutiny ([Aos Fatos](https://www.aosfatos.org/politica-editorial/)). Whether checking incumbents harder is accountability or imbalance is a question the codes leave to the reader.

## The platforms: from paid fact-checks to crowd notes

### The programme Facebook built

On 15 December 2016 Facebook announced that stories identified as fake by signatory fact-checkers "will get flagged as disputed" with a link to the explanation, and that "Stories that have been disputed may also appear lower in News Feed" ([Facebook, 15 December 2016](https://about.fb.com/news/2016/12/news-feed-fyi-addressing-hoaxes-and-fake-news/)). The division of labour stayed the same for nine years: in the description of the Latin American network LatamChequea, the certified organizations rate posts and "La plataforma decide luego qué medidas aplicar a los contenidos" (the platform then decides what measures to apply), and a rating does not mean that content is removed ([LatamChequea, 10 September 2026](https://latamchequea.com/2026/09/10/latamchequea-pide-a-meta-que-no-reemplace-la-verificacion-independiente-por-notas-de-la-comunidad/)). For years the company "boasted it was working with more than 100 organizations in over 60 languages", the Associated Press (AP) reported ([AP, 7 January 2025](https://www.clickondetroit.com/business/2025/01/07/meta-replaces-fact-checking-with-x-style-community-notes/)), and AFP, itself a participant, describes it as a programme in which Facebook "contrata dezenas de veículos de comunicação de todo o mundo" (hires dozens of media outlets around the world) for the purpose ([Folha de Pernambuco, with AFP, 12 September 2026](https://www.folhape.com.br/economia/conselho-de-supervisao-da-meta-pede-que-verificacao-nao-seja/512209/)).

### Meta's decision of January 2025

On 7 January 2025 Meta announced: "Starting in the US, we are ending our third party fact-checking program and moving to a Community Notes model" ([Meta, More Speech and Fewer Mistakes, 7 January 2025](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/01/meta-more-speech-fewer-mistakes/)). Its argument, in its own words:

- <Claim status="attributed" by="by Meta">"Experts, like everyone else, have their own biases and perspectives. This showed up in the choices some made about what to fact check and how."</Claim>
- "Over time we ended up with too much content being fact checked that people would understand to be legitimate political speech and debate. Our system then attached real consequences in the form of intrusive labels and reduced distribution. A program intended to inform too often became a tool to censor."
- It cited X: "We've seen this approach work on X", and said notes would "require agreement between people with a range of perspectives to help prevent biased ratings".
- It would "get rid of our fact-checking control, stop demoting fact checked content" and replace full-screen warnings with "a much less obtrusive label".

In the same announcement Meta estimated that of the millions of pieces of content it removed each day in December 2024, "one to two out of every 10 of these actions may have been mistakes", and an update on 29 May 2025 reported <Claim status="attributed" by="by Meta">"a roughly 50% reduction in enforcement mistakes on our platforms in the United States" between the fourth quarter of 2024 and the first quarter of 2025</Claim>, with the prevalence of violating content "largely" unchanged ([Meta, 7 January 2025, updated 29 May 2025](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/01/meta-more-speech-fewer-mistakes/)).

Meta began testing on 18 March 2025, using "X's open source algorithm as the basis of our rating system", in six languages including Portuguese, with notes of up to 500 characters that must link to a source; "Notes also won't have penalties associated with them the way fact checks did" ([Meta, 13 March 2025, updated 7 April 2025](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/03/testing-begins-community-notes-facebook-instagram-threads/)). On 7 April 2025 it wrote: "Starting today, we're ending our third-party fact checking program in the United States". It said it intended "ultimately to roll out this new approach to our users all over the world", and that until then "the third party fact checking program will remain in place" in other countries.

### The reactions

Supporters and critics read the same decision in opposite ways. Donald Trump, then president-elect, said "I think they've come a long way, Meta", and when asked whether Mark Zuckerberg had made the changes in response to threats Trump had made against him, answered "Probably"; Representative Jim Jordan, an Ohio Republican, called it a "huge step in the right direction" ([AP, 7 January 2025](https://www.clickondetroit.com/business/2025/01/07/meta-replaces-fact-checking-with-x-style-community-notes/); [The Hill, 7 January 2025](https://digital-release.thehill.com/?p=5071812)). Alexios Mantzarlis, a former director of the IFCN, called the change "a choice of politics, not policy", and Dan Evon of the News Literacy Project said it "provides an air of legitimacy to a popular disinformation narrative: That fact-checking is politically biased" (AP). The IFCN answered in an open letter on 9 January 2025: <Claim status="denied" by="by the IFCN">"This is false, and we want to set the record straight"</Claim>, rejecting the charge that its members censored, since Meta never let fact-checkers remove content ([IFCN, open letter to Mark Zuckerberg, 9 January 2025](https://www.poynter.org/ifcn/2025/an-open-letter-to-mark-zuckerberg-from-the-worlds-fact-checkers-nine-years-later/)).

In Brazil the federal government reacted within three days. On 10 January 2025 the Advocacia-Geral da União (AGU, the Attorney General's Office) sent Meta an extrajudicial notification giving it 72 hours to explain what it was doing about its duty of care on gender violence, children, racism, homophobia, suicide and hate speech, and whether it would publish a transparency report on checks made through community notes ([AGU, 10 January 2025](https://www.gov.br/agu/pt-br/comunicacao/noticias/agu-da-72-horas-para-meta-explicar-mudancas-na-checagem); [AGU, Notificação Extrajudicial n. 00002/2025](https://www.gov.br/agu/pt-br/comunicacao/noticias/agu-da-72-horas-para-meta-explicar-mudancas-na-checagem/NOTIF_FINAL_assinado.pdf/@@download/file)). The chief of staff, Rui Costa, said President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva had ordered it: "não vamos abrir mão da soberania do país" (we will not give up the country's sovereignty) ([Exame, 14 January 2025](https://exame.com/brasil/meta-diz-que-fim-de-checagem-de-fatos-se-aplica-apenas-para-os-eua)). Meta replied on 13 January that its independent fact-checking programme would, for now, be kept in Brazil, with the replacement restricted at first to the US, and confirmed that its new hateful conduct policy already applied in Brazil; the AGU answered that the platforms' terms and Meta's changes "não estão adequadas à legislação brasileira" (are not in line with Brazilian law) and called a public hearing ([Aos Fatos, 14 January 2025, updated 15 January 2025](https://www.aosfatos.org/noticias/agu-nova-politica-meta-desrespeita-a-lei/)). Aos Fatos, which reported the reply, is itself a partner in Meta's programme in Brazil.

### 2026: the Oversight Board and Latin America

Before expanding outside the US, Meta asked its Oversight Board, the body it funds to review its moderation decisions ([AFP, via CR Hoy, 9 September 2026](https://crhoy.com/tecnologia/meta-sustituira-la-verificacion-de-datos-por-notas-de-la-comunidad-en-latinoamerica/)), for "guidance from the Board on the specific factors it should consider when deciding whether any country should be omitted". The Board's policy advisory opinion of 26 March 2026 said community notes "could enhance users' freedom of expression" but noted Meta's own figure of "only about 6% of notes ever being published", found that "professional fact-checkers are among the most-cited sources for supporting context" and that "reducing support for fact-checkers will likely impact community notes", and recommended that notes "should not be introduced in advance of or during major elections" where risks are present and safeguards insufficient, and that countries with "repressive human rights records and weak civil societies" be left out at first. It stated that it "does not include independent recommendations about whether third-party fact-checking should be discontinued" ([Oversight Board, Assessing Meta's Plans to Expand Community Notes, 26 March 2026](https://www.oversightboard.com/decision/pao-007g5zuv/)).

On 9 September 2026 Meta began testing Community Notes in 16 Spanish-speaking Latin American countries: Argentina, Bolivia, Chile, Colombia, Costa Rica, Ecuador, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, Mexico, Nicaragua, Panama, Paraguay, Peru, Uruguay and Venezuela. Notes would not be visible during the test, independent fact-checking would continue meanwhile, and once the product worked Meta expected notes to become "el nuevo estándar de nuestras plataformas" (the new standard on our platforms), with fact-checking migrated gradually country by country ([Meta, Iniciamos los Tests de Notas de la Comunidad en Hispanoamérica, 9 September 2026](https://about.fb.com/ltam/news/2026/09/iniciamos-los-tests-de-notas-de-la-comunidad-en-hispanoamerica/)). Meta said that in the US, a year and a half after launch, notes had <Claim status="attributed" by="by Meta">more than 1.4 million contributors who had produced more than 50,000 notes on Facebook, Instagram and Threads, and that more than 200 million users saw a note in July alone</Claim>, and that their daily volume was double that of the independent fact-checks. Brazil, Portuguese-speaking and Meta's largest market in the region, was not included, AFP reported ([AFP, via CR Hoy, 9 September 2026](https://crhoy.com/tecnologia/meta-sustituira-la-verificacion-de-datos-por-notas-de-la-comunidad-en-latinoamerica/)).

The IFCN said "The community notes program alone is simply not adequate when it comes to reducing the impact of harmful falsehoods" and that professional programmes "should continue both in the region and worldwide" ([IFCN, 9 September 2026](https://www.poynter.org/fact-checking/2026/statement-from-the-international-fact-checking-network-on-metas-expansion-of-community-notes-to-latin-america/)). LatamChequea, a network of 47 organizations, which counts 14 organizations in the region taking part in Meta's programme, asked that notes complement, not replace, professional checks, arguing that "Reemplazar la verificación independiente debilitará las fuentes de las que se nutre el propio sistema" (replacing independent verification will weaken the sources the system itself draws on) ([LatamChequea, 10 September 2026](https://latamchequea.com/2026/09/10/latamchequea-pide-a-meta-que-no-reemplace-la-verificacion-independiente-por-notas-de-la-comunidad/)). The Oversight Board, in a statement AFP consulted, urged Meta "a não apresentar as notas da comunidade como uma substituição direta da verificação" (not to present community notes as a direct replacement for fact-checking), saying the decision to move from test to rollout "deve se basear em uma análise dos riscos específicos de cada país" (should rest on an analysis of each country's specific risks) ([Folha de Pernambuco, with AFP, 12 September 2026](https://www.folhape.com.br/economia/conselho-de-supervisao-da-meta-pede-que-verificacao-nao-seja/512209/)). On 12 November 2026 LatamChequea's members, in a statement from Mexico City, repeated their concern about the Meta announcement in a joint call to platforms, governments and funders ([Lupa, 12 November 2026](https://www.agencialupa.org/institucional/2026/11/12/verificadores-de-20-paises-pedem-as-plataformas-governos-e-financiadores-que-protejam-as-informacoes-confiaveis-na-america-latina/)).

## Community Notes: what the evidence shows

Most independent evidence comes from X, which publishes its notes data; Meta, as Chequeado and LatamChequea note, had not published comparable data on its own system ([Chequeado, 10 September 2026](https://chequeado.com/nota/meta-comienza-a-probar-las-notas-de-la-comunidad-en-america-latina-que-dice-la-evidencia-sobre-su-efectividad)).

- **When a note appears, sharing falls.** Using synthetic controls on 40,078 posts with proposed notes, a 2025 study estimated that notes cut reposts by 46.1% and likes by 44.1% after being attached; over the posts' entire lifespans, the reductions amounted to 11.6% fewer reposts and 13.3% fewer likes ([Slaughter, Peytavin, Ugander and Saveski, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 18 September 2025](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2503413122)); the journal published a correction to the paper in January 2026, which adds a competing-interest statement: one author "has collaborated with individuals employed by Meta" and another "with individuals employed by X/Twitter within the past 48 months", the collaborators "were not aware of the results presented in this work until they were publicly available", and the authors declare no competing financial interests ([correction, 30 January 2026](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2600635123)).
- **Few notes are shown, and few people write them.** Of more than 1.8 million notes, "only 11.5% of notes reach agreement on publication", the top 10% of contributors wrote 58% of all notes, and notes were published on average 65.7 hours after the original post, with longer delays reducing the chance of consensus ([Razuvayevskaya et al., arXiv preprint, 14 October 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.12559)).
- **Polarizing content is undermoderated by design.** Across 1.9 million notes and 13 countries, the algorithm captures each country's main polarizing dimension and surfaces notes with cross-partisan support, which "means that, by design, Community Notes systematically undermoderate polarizing content"; notes on the US, UK, French and German elections of 2024 and 2025 were undermoderated compared with other notes ([Bouchaud and Ramaciotti, Science Advances, July 2026](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aee6932)).
- **The partisan pattern did not disappear.** "2.3 times more posts by Republicans are flagged as misleading compared to posts by Democrats", which the authors say cannot be attributed to raters' bias, so that "Republicans will be sanctioned more than Democrats even if platforms transition from professional fact-checking to Community Notes" ([Renault, Mosleh and Rand, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 16 June 2025](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2502053122)).
- **In Portuguese.** A preprint that analysed 142,448 Portuguese-language notes in X's public data of 7 April 2026 found that 82% remained in the "needs more ratings" state, unevenly across topics ([Machado da Rocha, SciELO Preprints, 11 August 2026, not peer reviewed](https://doi.org/10.1590/scielopreprints.15996)).

## What defenders can do

- **Readers** can apply the moves the research describes: stop, leave the page to see what others say about the source, look for better coverage, and trace the claim to its origin ([Caulfield, 2019](https://hapgood.us/2019/06/19/sift-the-four-moves/)). Comprova's own advice adds checking the date, the author and whether other outlets report the same thing, noticing whether an image is built to provoke anger, indignation or fear, and running a reverse image search before sharing ([Comprova, Dicas para verificar conteúdos](https://projetocomprova.com.br/dicas/)).
- **Publishers and camera makers** can attach Content Credentials, knowing that a credential proves origin and integrity, not truth, and that metadata is often stripped on social platforms ([C2PA explainer 2.3](https://spec.c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.3/explainer/Explainer.html); [NIST AI 100-4](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.100-4.pdf)).
- **Platforms** that label content can label verified true items too, which removed the implied truth effect in experiments ([Pennycook et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2019.3478)), and can publish the data that would let outsiders test their systems, as the Oversight Board recommended ([Oversight Board, 2026](https://www.oversightboard.com/decision/pao-007g5zuv/)).
- **Fact-checkers** are measured against public criteria: replicable sources, published methods and funding, and corrections ([IFCN](https://ifcncodeofprinciples.poynter.org/the-commitments)).

## What to remember

- Fact-checking rates the claims of public figures, debunking extends it to anonymous viral content, and verification establishes who made content, when and where.
- The IFCN code certifies fact-checkers against 31 criteria under five commitments; state- or party-controlled outlets cannot sign.
- Comprova validates each check across at least three other newsrooms; Lupa uses seven labels; Aos Fatos uses three and says those in power deserve preferential scrutiny.
- Professional fact-checkers read laterally: they leave a site quickly to learn who is behind it, while historians and students read vertically and were misled more often.
- A Content Credential shows a file's signed history and whether it was tampered with; the C2PA itself says provenance cannot show whether content is true, and metadata can be stripped.
- Corrections lower false belief on average (d = 0.29 across 30 studies; at least 0.59 points on a 5-point scale in four countries), less in politics, with decay; a 2018 experiment in Brazil found them ineffective.
- Labels reduce belief even among people who distrust fact-checkers, but can make unlabelled false content look true.
- Meta ended US fact-checking in April 2025 for Community Notes, citing bias and censorship; fact-checkers deny censoring; Brazil's government challenged the change and Meta kept the programme in Brazil.
- In September 2026 Meta began testing notes in 16 Spanish-speaking Latin American countries, not Brazil; the Oversight Board, the IFCN and LatamChequea urged that notes complement fact-checks.
- On X, attached notes cut reposts by almost half but most come late, few reach consensus, and polarizing content is undermoderated by design.

- If a fact-checker's choice of what to check can tilt its record even when every verdict is accurate, what selection rule would a reader on either side accept as fair?
