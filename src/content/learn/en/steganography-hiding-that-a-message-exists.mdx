Encryption leaves a trace that announces a secret. Steganography tries to leave no trace at all: the message travels inside something that looks ordinary, a letter, a photograph, a sound file, the timing of network packets, so that an observer does not know there is anything to look for. This opener draws the map of the series. After reading, you will understand how steganography differs from cryptography and from watermarking, how Gustavus Simmons turned the problem into a game between two prisoners and a warden, what "secure" can mean for a hiding scheme once Kerckhoffs's principle is applied to it, why security, capacity and robustness pull against each other, how the techniques fall into physical, linguistic, technical and network families, which famous claims are established and which are folklore, and where each technique is treated in depth in the parts that follow.

## What steganography is, and what it is not

The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) of the United States defines steganography as "The art, science, and practice of communicating in a way that hides the existence of the communication", the current wording in its glossary, adapted from NIST Special Publication 800-72 ([NIST Computer Security Resource Center, glossary](https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/steganography)). Ross Anderson and Fabien Petitcolas of the University of Cambridge put the contrast in one sentence in 1998, in a journal of the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE): "While classical cryptography is about concealing the content of messages, steganography is about concealing their existence" ([Anderson and Petitcolas, IEEE Journal on Selected Areas in Communications, May 1998](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/archive/rja14/Papers/jsac98-limsteg.pdf)).

The word comes from Greek. In a 2014 review in Communications of the ACM, the magazine of the Association for Computing Machinery (ACM), Elżbieta Zielińska, Wojciech Mazurczyk and Krzysztof Szczypiorski of the Warsaw University of Technology gloss steganography as "covered writing" and cryptography as "secret writing" ([Communications of the ACM, March 2014](https://staff.elka.pw.edu.pl/~wmazurc1/art/CACM-trends.pdf)); Józef Lubacz, Mazurczyk and Szczypiorski trace the first part to steganos, meaning protected or covered ([Lubacz, Mazurczyk and Szczypiorski, arXiv:1207.0917, 2012](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1207.0917)). Johannes Trithemius used the word as the title of his Steganographia, the subject of [the next part](/learn/steganography-before-computers-wax-tablets-invisible-ink-and-microdots).

Why hide existence, when encryption already protects content? Anderson and Petitcolas answer with cases in which the mere sight of ciphertext is the leak: enciphered traffic between a soldier and a hostile government, or between a known drug smuggler and someone not yet under suspicion, and a police force in the United Kingdom that found criminals detecting and reacting to the presence of encrypted radio traffic near them ([Anderson and Petitcolas, 1998](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/archive/rja14/Papers/jsac98-limsteg.pdf)). The 1999 survey by Petitcolas, Anderson and Markus Kuhn adds the battlefield: even if the content is encrypted, detecting a signal "may lead rapidly to an attack on the signaller", which is why military radios use spread spectrum and meteor scatter transmission ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, Proceedings of the IEEE, July 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rja14/Papers/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)). For how encryption itself evolved, see [the encryption family history](/learn/encryption-family-history), and for the difference between encrypting, hashing and encoding, [hashing, encryption and encoding](/learn/hashing-encryption-encoding).

The two are often combined. The 1999 survey treats the hidden payload as data "which we can assume has been encrypted and is thus indistinguishable from random noise" ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rja14/Papers/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)). Lubacz and his co-authors make the opposite point for network carriers: encrypting first "will potentially increase the probability that the message is noticed" ([Lubacz, Mazurczyk and Szczypiorski, 2012](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1207.0917)). Both can be true: an encrypted payload looks like noise, which helps only where the carrier already contains noise of the same kind.

The table sets the three neighbouring disciplines side by side, as the Communications of the ACM review and Niels Provos and Peter Honeyman's 2003 introduction describe them ([Zielińska, Mazurczyk and Szczypiorski, 2014](https://staff.elka.pw.edu.pl/~wmazurc1/art/CACM-trends.pdf); [Provos and Honeyman, IEEE Security and Privacy, May/June 2003](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~chazelle/courses/BIB/stegano-honeyman.pdf)).

| | Cryptography | Steganography | Watermarking |
|---|---|---|---|
| Goal | Make the content unreadable | Hide that communication happens | Protect or identify the carrier |
| What an observer sees | An obviously secret message | An ordinary-looking object | An ordinary object, sometimes a visible mark |
| What the attacker wins by | Reading the content | Detecting that a message exists | Removing or forging the mark |
| Main requirement | Secrecy of the key | Statistical and perceptual invisibility | Robustness against removal |
| Typical fragility | Not compared in these sources | Hidden data often destroyed by small changes | Mark must survive compression and editing |

## The vocabulary, agreed in 1996

The first academic conference on information hiding was held in 1996, at the Isaac Newton Institute in Cambridge, England, in May, and its proceedings appeared as volume 1174 of Springer's Lecture Notes in Computer Science ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rja14/Papers/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)). Birgit Pfitzmann recorded the terminology agreed at an informal plenary meeting there, "Information hiding terminology", and the 1999 survey adopts it ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rja14/Papers/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)):

| Term | Meaning in the 1996 terminology | Toy example |
|---|---|---|
| Embedded data | The message one wishes to send secretly | *Meet at noon* |
| Cover-text, cover-image, cover-audio | The innocuous object the message is hidden in | A holiday photograph |
| Stego-text, stego-object | The cover after the message has been hidden in it | The same photograph, slightly altered |
| Stego-key | The secret that controls hiding, and restricts detection or recovery to those who know it | A password that chooses which pixels carry bits |

The survey also separates steganography from copyright marking: a steganographic attack succeeds when it detects that communication exists, while copyright marks carry the "additional requirement of robustness against possible attacks"; fragile marks break when the object is altered too much, robust marks are meant to be impossible to remove without destroying the object, and fingerprints act as hidden serial numbers while watermarks say who owns the object ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rja14/Papers/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)). Marks, fingerprints and their limits are the subject of [Watermarks and fingerprints](/learn/watermarks-and-fingerprints-marking-content-to-trace-it).

## Simmons's prisoners' problem

The modern study of steganography starts with a story. Gustavus Simmons presented "The Prisoners' Problem and the Subliminal Channel" at CRYPTO'83, the workshop on communications security held in Santa Barbara, California ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rja14/Papers/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)); it was published in Advances in Cryptology, edited by David Chaum (Plenum, 1984), and its abstract opens: "Two accomplices in a crime have been arrested and are about to be locked in widely separated cells." In Simmons's version the warden lets them exchange messages only if he can read them, hoping to deceive them with forged or altered messages; the prisoners accept the risk, want a subliminal channel hidden inside the open messages, and also want to authenticate what they receive ([Simmons, Advances in Cryptology, 1984, Springer chapter page](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4684-4730-9_5)).

The literature retells it with names that stuck. Alice and Bob are in jail and want to plan an escape; every message passes through the warden, Willie; if Willie sees any ciphertext, he puts them in solitary confinement ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rja14/Papers/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)). Anderson and Petitcolas add the rule that makes the game hard: "we assume that the mechanism in use is known to the warden, and so the security must depend solely on a secret key that Alice and Bob have somehow managed to share" ([Anderson and Petitcolas, 1998](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/archive/rja14/Papers/jsac98-limsteg.pdf)).

Simmons showed that such a channel exists inside certain digital signature schemes: the random value each signature needs can be chosen so that it carries a short message, and because it exploits randomness already present, the message "cannot even in principle be detected"; he called it the subliminal channel ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rja14/Papers/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)). The prisoners were a cover story in their own right. Anderson and Petitcolas write that the formulation "was itself an instance of information hiding": a ruse to draw academic attention to a problem from the then classified work on verifying nuclear arms control treaties, in which the United States and the Soviet Union wanted to place sensors in each other's facilities that would report some facts, such as the number of missiles, without revealing others, such as their location ([Anderson and Petitcolas, 1998](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/archive/rja14/Papers/jsac98-limsteg.pdf)).

### Passive and active wardens, worked through

The survey distinguishes a passive warden, who can only observe the traffic, from an active warden, who may modify it ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rja14/Papers/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)). The difference matters because the defences differ. A toy run of the game, with historical counterparts from the sources:

| Round | What Alice does | What Willie does | Historical counterpart |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sends "Father is dead" with a hidden meaning in the exact wording | A passive warden reads it and lets it through | Ordinary postal reading |
| 2 | Sends the same text | An active warden rewrites it as "Father is deceased", preserving the plain meaning and destroying any code in the words | A First World War censor did exactly this; the reply, "Is Father dead or deceased?", gave the game away ([Anderson and Petitcolas, 1998](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/archive/rja14/Papers/jsac98-limsteg.pdf)) |
| 3 | Hides meaning in kisses drawn on the letter, or in a shipment's watch hands | The active warden deletes the crosses and moves the hands | Postal censors "have deleted lovers' X's from letters, shifted the hands of watches in shipments, and even rephrased telegrams" ([Anderson and Petitcolas, 1998](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/archive/rja14/Papers/jsac98-limsteg.pdf)) |
| 4 | Weaves the message into the plot of a story, say the succession of towns where the action happens | The warden can only pass or suppress the whole letter, because rewriting it would change its obvious meaning | The supraliminal channel proposed to turn an active warden into a passive one ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rja14/Papers/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)) |

Round 2 is the lesson that every later defence repeats: a warden who cannot detect a hidden message can still destroy it by normalizing the carrier, rewording text, re-encoding images, delaying and reordering traffic. [Part 458](/learn/steganography-before-computers-wax-tablets-invisible-ink-and-microdots) shows how wartime censors did it to mail, cables and radio. Further work found that public key steganography is possible, with Alice and Bob sharing no secret before going to jail, though an active warden makes it harder ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rja14/Papers/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)).

## What "secure" means for hiding

In January and February 1883 Auguste Kerckhoffs set out in the Journal des sciences militaires the principles a cipher system should meet; the second, in Petitcolas's English version, is that "The system must not require secrecy and can be stolen by the enemy without causing trouble" ([Petitcolas, Kerckhoffs' principles from La cryptographie militaire](https://www.petitcolas.net/kerckhoffs/)). The site's glossary entry on [Kerckhoffs's principle](/glossary/kerckhoffs-principle) and the article [Security through obscurity: what Kerckhoffs actually said](/learn/security-through-obscurity) explain the principle for cryptography.

Applied to hiding, it gives the survey's "tentative definition" of a secure stego-system: "one where an opponent who understands the system, but does not know the key, can obtain no evidence (or even grounds for suspicion) that a communication has taken place" ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rja14/Papers/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)). The authors draw the consequence that steganographic processes intended for wide use "should be published, just like commercial cryptographic algorithms and protocols", and give a concrete form: embed in pseudo-randomly chosen pixels or sound samples, so that the key of the pseudo-random generator becomes the stego-key "and Kerckhoffs' principle is observed" ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rja14/Papers/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)). Provos and Honeyman state the same aim, "Modern steganography attempts to be detectable only if secret information is known", and add a condition cryptography does not have: the unmodified cover must also stay secret, because comparing cover and stego object reveals the changes at once ([Provos and Honeyman, 2003](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~chazelle/courses/BIB/stegano-honeyman.pdf)).

The older practice is the opposite, and the sources say so. Provos and Honeyman write that "A classical steganographic system's security relies on the encoding system's secrecy", and use the tattooed messenger as the example: once the trick is known, guards can shave the head of everyone passing by ([Provos and Honeyman, 2003](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~chazelle/courses/BIB/stegano-honeyman.pdf)). The 2014 review in Communications of the ACM still lists, in its comparison table, that steganographic security "relies on the confidentiality of the method of embedding" ([Zielińska, Mazurczyk and Szczypiorski, 2014](https://staff.elka.pw.edu.pl/~wmazurc1/art/CACM-trends.pdf)). The two statements describe different things: the table describes how many methods work in practice, the survey and Provos and Honeyman describe what a method must achieve to deserve the word secure. The survey's verdict on hidden-method systems is blunt: "That any of the above 'security-by-obscurity' systems ever worked was a matter of luck" ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rja14/Papers/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)).

Christian Cachin made the definition measurable. In his information-theoretic model, first presented at the Information Hiding Workshop of 1998, the warden's task is a hypothesis test between innocent covers and stego objects, and "The security of a steganographic system is quantified in terms of the relative entropy" between the two distributions ([Cachin, Cryptology ePrint Archive 2000/028, revised 2004](https://eprint.iacr.org/2000/028)). Provos and Honeyman summarize the ideal: "A system is perfectly secure if no decision rule exists that can perform better than random guessing" ([Provos and Honeyman, 2003](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~chazelle/courses/BIB/stegano-honeyman.pdf)). The 1999 survey adds why steganography is harder to found than cryptography: it depends on understanding the cover source, and if a source were understood completely, "steganography would either be trivial or impossible depending on the system" ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rja14/Papers/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)).

## Security, capacity and robustness

Provos and Honeyman name the three-way pull: "Three different aspects in information-hiding systems contend with each other: capacity, security, and robustness." Capacity is how much can be hidden, security is the eavesdropper's inability to detect it, and robustness is how much modification the stego object withstands before the hidden data is destroyed; watermarking favours robustness, while steganography "strives for high security and capacity", so its hidden data is often fragile ([Provos and Honeyman, 2003](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~chazelle/courses/BIB/stegano-honeyman.pdf)). A 2021 survey of deep-learning data hiding by Zihan Wang and seven co-authors draws the same triangle, with capacity, imperceptibility and robustness at the corners, and places naive steganography, secure steganography and watermarking at different points of it ([Wang and others, arXiv:2107.09287, version of 19 April 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.09287)).

| Corner favoured | What you gain | What you give up | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capacity | Many hidden bits per carrier | More changes, so easier detection | Naive tools that fill every least significant bit |
| Security (undetectability) | Statistical invisibility | Fewer bits; hidden data dies if the carrier is re-encoded | Covert communication |
| Robustness | The mark survives compression, cropping and printing | Visible or detectable redundancy, few bits | Watermarks and fingerprints |

Network steganography has the same trade under other names. Lubacz, Mazurczyk and Szczypiorski call the throughput "steganographic bandwidth" and write that "usually, the higher steganographic bandwidth, the lower the resistance to steganalysis" ([Lubacz, Mazurczyk and Szczypiorski, 2012](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1207.0917)).

Capacity does not even grow in proportion to the carrier. Andrew Ker, Tomáš Pevný, Jan Kodovský and Jessica Fridrich showed in 2008 that, under the right conditions, the secure capacity of a single cover "should be proportional to the square root of the cover size", and observed close accordance with that square root law in experiments with three embedding methods and four detectors ([Ker, Pevný, Kodovský and Fridrich, ACM Workshop on Multimedia and Security, Oxford, September 2008](https://researchconnect.suny.edu/en/publications/the-square-root-law-of-steganographic-capacity/)). Worked through: if a photograph of one million pixels can safely carry a given payload, a photograph of four million pixels carries about twice that payload at the same risk, not four times, because the square root of 4 is 2. How capacity is counted in bits per pixel is shown in [Hiding in images](/learn/hiding-in-images-lsb-jpeg-and-adaptive-steganography).

Computer security standards set numbers on the same idea for covert channels inside systems. The Trusted Computer System Evaluation Criteria (TCSEC) of the United States Department of Defense (DoD), standard DoD 5200.28-STD of 26 December 1985, called a covert channel bandwidth above 100 bits per second "high", judged channels below one bit per second acceptable in most environments, and asked for the capability to audit channels that may exceed one bit in ten seconds ([Department of Defense, TCSEC, December 1985, section 8](https://csrc.nist.gov/csrc/media/publications/conference-paper/1998/10/08/proceedings-of-the-21st-nissc-1998/documents/early-cs-papers/dod85.pdf)). Worked through: at one bit per second, a one-kibibyte file (8,192 bits) takes 8,192 seconds, about two hours and sixteen minutes, to leak; at 100 bits per second, it takes under a minute and a half. Slow channels are not harmless, they are slow.

## The families

The 1999 survey classified information hiding into covert channels, steganography, anonymity and copyright marking, and split steganography into linguistic and technical, noting in the caption of its classification figure that many ancient systems are "a form of 'technical steganography' (in the sense that messages are hidden physically)" ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rja14/Papers/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)). The same caption files most of the survey's own digital examples under linguistic steganography and copyright marking. This series sorts the techniques into four families by carrier, so the survey's "technical" corresponds to the physical family below.

| Family | Carrier | Examples | Where in this series |
|---|---|---|---|
| Physical | Objects and substances | Wax tablets, tattooed scalps, invisible inks, microdots | [Steganography before computers](/learn/steganography-before-computers-wax-tablets-invisible-ink-and-microdots) |
| Linguistic | The words themselves | Null ciphers, acrostics, open codes, semagrams, synonym substitution, generated text | [Part 458](/learn/steganography-before-computers-wax-tablets-invisible-ink-and-microdots) and [Hiding in sound, video and text](/learn/hiding-in-sound-video-and-text-from-echo-hiding-to-zero-width-characters) |
| Technical (digital media) | Redundancy in images, audio, video and file formats | Least significant bit (LSB) embedding, Joint Photographic Experts Group (JPEG) coefficient embedding, adaptive schemes, echo hiding, zero-width characters | [Hiding in images](/learn/hiding-in-images-lsb-jpeg-and-adaptive-steganography), [Steganalysis](/learn/steganalysis-finding-the-hidden-message), [Hiding in sound, video and text](/learn/hiding-in-sound-video-and-text-from-echo-hiding-to-zero-width-characters) |
| Network | Protocol fields, timing and behaviour | Header fields, packet timing, tunnels through the Domain Name System (DNS) or the Internet Control Message Protocol (ICMP) | Network covert channels and tunnels (a later part) |

The network family has its own founding text. In October 1973 Butler Lampson, then at the Xerox Palo Alto Research Center, studied how to confine a program so that it "cannot transmit information to any other program except its caller", and sorted the escape routes into storage channels, legitimate channels such as the bill for the service, and "Covert channels, i.e. those not intended for information transfer at all, such as the service program's effect on the system load" ([Lampson, Communications of the ACM 16(10), October 1973, author's page](https://www.bwlampson.site/11-Confinement/WebPage.html); [text](https://crypto.stanford.edu/cs155old/cs155-spring11/papers/confinement.pdf)). The TCSEC later defined a covert storage channel as one process writing a storage location that another reads, and a covert timing channel as one process "modulating its own use of system resources" so that another observes the change in response time ([Department of Defense, TCSEC, December 1985](https://csrc.nist.gov/csrc/media/publications/conference-paper/1998/10/08/proceedings-of-the-21st-nissc-1998/documents/early-cs-papers/dod85.pdf)). Lubacz and his co-authors define network steganography by three conditions: some protocol functions are modified, the modification touches functions that cope with imperfect channels or that shape the exchange, and the effects are made to look like the network's ordinary imperfection ([Lubacz, Mazurczyk and Szczypiorski, 2012](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1207.0917)). The site's article on [ICMP, ping and traceroute](/learn/icmp-ping-and-traceroute) already notes the Echo payload as a covert channel; zero-width and lookalike characters are explained in [Hidden and lookalike characters](/learn/hidden-and-lookalike-characters), and how text is stored as bytes in [Character encoding](/learn/character-encoding).

## Who uses it, and for what

The survey lists the demand: military and intelligence agencies that need unobtrusive communications; criminals, whose preferred tools in 1999 were prepaid and identity-changing mobile phones; law enforcement and counter-intelligence, which want to detect and trace hidden messages; people resisting limits on free speech and civilian cryptography; digital elections and digital cash; and, above all, copyright, which the survey calls the main driving force of the research ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rja14/Papers/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)). The authors observe that the ethics are "much less clear" than in cryptography: industry needs tools to hide copyright marks, "yet these tools can be abused by spies" ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rja14/Papers/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)). The 2014 review lists legitimate uses as circumventing web censorship and surveillance, computer forensics and copyright protection, and illicit ones from criminal communication to leakage from guarded systems and industrial espionage ([Zielińska, Mazurczyk and Szczypiorski, 2014](https://staff.elka.pw.edu.pl/~wmazurc1/art/CACM-trends.pdf)). Lubacz and his co-authors go further and argue that researchers who publish a new technique have "an ethical obligation" to present the methods that uncover it ([Lubacz, Mazurczyk and Szczypiorski, 2012](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1207.0917)). This series follows that rule: every technique comes with how it is found and stopped.

Tracing leaks is an old application. Anderson and Petitcolas report, introducing it with "Apparently", that in the 1980s Margaret Thatcher had government word processors encode their identity in the word spacing so that ministers who leaked cabinet documents could be traced ([Anderson and Petitcolas, 1998](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/archive/rja14/Papers/jsac98-limsteg.pdf)); the survey describes a publishing pilot in which shifting text lines by one three-hundredth of an inch survived multi-generation photocopying ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rja14/Papers/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)).

## Cases, claims and folklore

Steganography attracts stories, and the series gives each one its status.

In February 2001 the newspaper USA Today published a report by Jack Kelley, forwarded to the Information Security News mailing list on 7 February 2001, saying that <Claim status="reported" by="by USA Today, citing unnamed United States officials">Osama bin Laden and his associates were hiding maps and photographs of targets and posting instructions on sports chat rooms, pornographic bulletin boards and other websites</Claim> ([USA Today, February 2001, as forwarded to the Information Security News list](https://seclists.org/isn/2001/Feb/38)). Provos and Honeyman, at the University of Michigan's Center for Information Technology Integration (CITI), built a crawler and a statistical detector, ran dictionary attacks on a cluster of workstations, and reported: "We have analyzed two million images downloaded from eBay auctions but have not been able to find a single hidden message" ([Provos and Honeyman, CITI Technical Report 01-11, 31 August 2001](http://www.citi.umich.edu/techreports/reports/citi-tr-01-11.pdf)); the later version adds one million images from a Usenet archive, with the same result ([Provos and Honeyman, Detecting Steganographic Content on the Internet](http://niels.xtdnet.nl/papers/detecting.pdf)). Their report notes that the newspaper articles "sounded very dire" but "none substantiated these rumors" ([Provos and Honeyman, 2001](http://www.citi.umich.edu/techreports/reports/citi-tr-01-11.pdf)). In March 2004 USA Today's own investigation, as republished by the Deseret News, examined about 100 of Kelley's 720 stories and found that he had fabricated parts of major stories; the accounts it lists as contradicted do not include the February 2001 report ([Blake Morrison, USA Today, in the Deseret News, 21 March 2004](https://www.deseret.com/2004/3/21/19818659/ex-usa-today-writer-fabricated-parts-of-major-stories)). The 2014 review still repeats that <Claim status="reported" by="by Zielińska, Mazurczyk and Szczypiorski, citing press reports">"It is claimed" that the organization behind the attacks of 11 September 2001 used images to conceal instructions</Claim> ([Zielińska, Mazurczyk and Szczypiorski, 2014](https://staff.elka.pw.edu.pl/~wmazurc1/art/CACM-trends.pdf)). No published evidence read for this article shows such messages.

Court records carry a detailed allegation. On 28 June 2010 the Department of Justice (DOJ) announced the arrest of ten alleged secret agents in the United States, with eleven defendants charged ([DOJ, 28 June 2010](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/ten-alleged-secret-agents-arrested-united-states)). The sworn complaint of a Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) special agent describes steganography as hiding data inside an image, states that <Claim status="alleged" by="in the FBI complaint">Moscow Center used steganographic software that was not sold commercially</Claim>, and says that the images sat on publicly available websites and looked entirely ordinary to the naked eye ([United States v. "Christopher R. Metsos" and others, sealed complaint, Southern District of New York, June 2010](https://www.justice.gov/sites/default/files/opa/legacy/2010/06/28/062810complaint2.pdf)). The case, its outcome and other prosecutions belong to a later part on espionage and the courts. Hiding in malware, which the 2014 review traces through Duqu and Operation Shady RAT ([Zielińska, Mazurczyk and Szczypiorski, 2014](https://staff.elka.pw.edu.pl/~wmazurc1/art/CACM-trends.pdf)), is the subject of a later part built on the vendors' own reports.

## What to remember

- Cryptography hides what a message says; steganography hides that there is a message. They are often combined, and an encrypted payload helps only where the carrier already holds similar noise.
- Simmons's prisoners' problem (1983) frames the field: Alice and Bob talk under the eyes of a warden who punishes any visible secret; a passive warden watches, an active warden alters.
- Kerckhoffs's principle applies: a secure scheme stays undetectable to an opponent who knows the method but not the key; schemes whose safety rests on a secret method are, in the 1999 survey's words, lucky when they work.
- Security, capacity and robustness pull against each other; secure capacity grows only with about the square root of the cover size.
- The families are physical, linguistic, technical (digital media) and network; an active warden who normalizes the carrier can destroy all of them without detecting any.
- Court records carry detailed allegations of use, such as the FBI's 2010 complaint; many famous claims, such as hidden terrorist messages on auction sites in 2001, remain unsubstantiated reports.

<ReaderQuestions>

- If an active warden can destroy hidden messages by rewording, re-encoding and delaying everything, what does that cost the honest users of the same channel, and who should decide when the cost is worth paying?
- Kerckhoffs's principle says the method should be public and only the key secret. Should researchers who publish a new hiding method always publish its detector at the same time, as Lubacz and his co-authors argue?
- Provos and Honeyman found no hidden messages in three million images in 2001. What would a search have to look like today to show that something is absent rather than merely undetected?
- The square root law says safe capacity grows slowly with the size of the carrier. Does that make steganography a niche tool, or does the sheer volume of images and traffic online make up for it?
- When a newspaper report, a review article and a court complaint disagree about whether steganography was used, which kinds of evidence should a reader ask for before repeating the claim?

</ReaderQuestions>
