## An underground needs a press

A subculture that cannot publish is just a rumour. The computer underground had no journals, no conferences and no employers who would admit to hiring it, so it built its own press out of what it had: a bulletin board, an ASCII text file, and a distribution network of teenagers with modems.

**Phrack** began in November 1985 on a Metal Shop BBS in St. Louis, founded by Taran King and Knight Lightning. The name is *phreak* plus *hack*, and the format never really changed: numbered issues of plain-text articles, each one dense with technical detail nobody else would print. Its Pro-Philes profiled figures in the scene. Its **Phrack World News** carried the movements, arrests and gossip of a community with no other newspaper. And its technical files were the real curriculum - telephone switching internals from [Legion of Doom](/learn/lod-mod-and-operation-sundevil) members, network protocol dissections, and in issue 49, in 1996, **Aleph One's "Smashing the Stack for Fun and Profit"**, which explained the buffer overflow so completely that it became the founding text of memory-corruption exploitation and, thirty years later, is still the reference everyone reaches for.

**2600: The Hacker Quarterly** started in January 1984, edited by Eric Corley under the name **Emmanuel Goldstein** - Orwell's fictional dissident, a naming choice that tells you the editorial line before you open it. Where Phrack was a text file, 2600 was print: a physical magazine on newsstands, sold in bookshops, taken home in a backpack. That physicality mattered enormously in the pre-web years, because it meant a teenager in a town with no scene could find the underground on a shelf.

## The meetings, and what they were really for

2600's most durable invention was not editorial. In 1987 it began organising **monthly meetings on the first Friday**, in public places, in city after city, and they still run - in payphone-less malls and food courts on several continents. Anyone can show up.

The meetings did what no magazine could: they turned a mailing list into a community with faces. They also drew visible law-enforcement attention - a 1992 meeting at a Washington mall was broken up with attendees searched and detained, which the Secret Service later had to answer for - and that episode is why the meetings became, themselves, a small civil-liberties argument conducted monthly in public.

## The Manifesto

In January 1986, hours after his arrest, a Legion of Doom member named **Loyd Blankenship**, writing as **The Mentor**, published a short essay in Phrack titled *The Conscience of a Hacker*. It is barely a page. It describes a bored, bright kid dismissed by teachers, who finds a machine that responds to competence rather than social standing, and it ends by insisting that curiosity is not a crime.

It became the single most reprinted text the culture produced - quoted in *Hackers*, read aloud at conferences, argued over in courtrooms, and still handed to newcomers. Its critics have a real point: it is adolescent, it is self-flattering, and it has been used to dress up harm that had nothing to do with curiosity. Both things are true, and that is why the essay is worth teaching rather than either canonising or dismissing.

Blankenship's second encounter with the law is the more instructive one. By coincidence of employment he wrote **GURPS Cyberpunk** for Steve Jackson Games - the manuscript the Secret Service seized in 1990 as a supposed handbook for computer crime.

## What the press paid for its principles

2600 spent its own money defending the line it had argued for fifteen years. In 2000 the film industry sued the magazine for publishing and linking to **DeCSS**, the DVD decryption program written by [Jon Lech Johansen](/glossary/dvd-jon). *Universal v. Reimerdes* went badly: the court enjoined 2600 not only from hosting the code but from **linking to sites that hosted it**, one of the first rulings to treat a hyperlink as an act of distribution rather than a citation. The response was the most creative protest the field has produced - DeCSS as a haiku, a T-shirt, a song, a dramatic reading, and a prime number - all making one argument: you are being asked to ban a number.

## The line it drew, and where it went

The underground press is also where **full disclosure** was argued into existence. The position - publish the flaw, in full, because vendors fix what is public and ignore what is private - was worked out in these pages long before it had a policy name, and every modern disclosure framework is a negotiated retreat from it.

Phrack still publishes, irregularly, decades in. 2600 still prints, the meetings still happen, Off The Hook still airs on WBAI. The knowledge itself moved to conference stages, bug bounties and vendor advisories, which is a healthier arrangement in almost every way. What the magazines retain is the thing an industry cannot supply: an editorial position that the right to understand technology does not depend on permission from whoever sells it.
