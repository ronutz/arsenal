This part discusses suicide and self-harm and how reports about them spread. It describes no methods and no places. If you are struggling, in Brazil the Centro de Valorização da Vida (CVV) answers free on 188, 24 hours a day, and by chat and e-mail ([CVV](https://cvv.org.br/)); elsewhere, the International Association for Suicide Prevention (IASP), whose website the World Health Organization (WHO) points to for lists of suicide prevention services, links to a directory of verified helplines in more than 150 countries ([IASP, crisis centres and helplines](https://www.iasp.info/crisis-centres-helplines/); [WHO, Preventing suicide: a resource for media professionals, update 2023](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240076846)). Local emergency services can also help.

Contagion did not begin with social media. Dancing crowds, convent possessions and school outbreaks spread through towns and cloisters long before any network, as earlier parts of this series show. What platforms changed is the carrier. A symptom can now travel from a video creator in one country to teenagers in another who never meet; a warning about a danger that does not exist can reach more people than the danger ever could; a recommendation system can bring a vulnerable person content she did not search for, again and again. This part of the series on [mass hysteria and collective belief](/learn/mass-hysteria-how-groups-come-to-believe-anything) looks at those carriers. The clinical side of the tic-like behaviours of 2019 to 2022 (who was affected, how doctors told them from Tourette syndrome, what they were called) is in [Mass psychogenic illness: when symptoms spread](/learn/mass-psychogenic-illness-when-symptoms-spread); this part tells the platform side. The 2014 Facebook experiment on emotional contagion and the research on complex contagion in networks are in [Conformity, cascades and contagion](/learn/conformity-cascades-and-contagion-the-mechanisms); the diffusion studies of true and false news and WhatsApp's forwarding limits are in [From the lodge to the network](/learn/from-the-lodge-to-the-network-how-reach-changed). After reading, you can tell a hoax panic from a real risk, say what the research on suicide contagion and protective stories does and does not show, and recognize what platforms, courts and health authorities did about it.

## What a platform changes

The WHO's 2023 resource for journalists states the starting point: "The Internet has become an important platform for information and communication about suicide, especially among young people and others who may be vulnerable", and adds that "There is some evidence that helpful digital media are often shared more frequently than sensational content" ([WHO, Preventing suicide: a resource for media professionals, update 2023, annex 1](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/92773496-dc8b-4d42-aef3-3a5a517dfa63/content)). The cases in this part show four changes that platforms made to old patterns of contagion.

**The index case can be virtual.** In classic outbreaks of mass psychogenic illness the first case is someone the others see. German neurologists described a creator on YouTube as a "'virtual' index case", and other influencers who copied the behaviours as "'secondary virtual' index cases", spreading symptoms "without local restrictions" ([Müller-Vahl, Pisarenko, Jakubovski and Fremer, Brain 145(2), 2022, full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9014744/fullTextXML)).

**The warning can be the vector.** In hoax panics the content that circulates most is often the alarm, not the alleged danger. A British fact-checker traced how police statements based on press reports were then treated by the press as confirmation, a "feedback loop" ([Full Fact, The "Momo challenge" is an online hoax fuelled by media coverage, 1 March 2019](https://fullfact.org/online/momo-challenge-hoax/)).

**The feed chooses.** A coroner in London found in 2022 that a platform's algorithms had produced, for a 14-year-old girl, "binge periods of images, video clips and text some of which were selected and provided without Molly requesting them" ([North London Coroner's Service, Regulation 28 report to prevent future deaths, Molly Russell, 13 October 2022](https://www.judiciary.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/Molly-Russell-Prevention-of-future-deaths-report-2022-0315_Published.pdf)).

**Attention pays.** Clinicians reviewing the tic-like wave noted that some influencers sold merchandise tied to their videos and that "The videos with more views tend to be the videos portraying the most frequent, extraordinary, and violent tics, even when these are inaccurate portrayals" ([Frey and colleagues, Psychology Research and Behavior Management 15, 2022, full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9733629/fullTextXML)); in 2019 YouTube was not running advertisements on videos about the Momo hoax, even those from news organizations ([The Verge, 1 March 2019](https://www.theverge.com/2019/3/1/18244890/momo-youtube-news-hoax-demonetization-comments-kids)).

How far a message travels on networks, and what the studies of virality can and cannot show, is the subject of [From the lodge to the network](/learn/from-the-lodge-to-the-network-how-reach-changed); what platforms say about their recommendation systems is in [Recommendation and profiling](/learn/recommendation-and-profiling-what-platforms-disclose).

## Symptoms on screens: the tic-like wave from the platform side

### A channel in Germany

The German case can be dated to the day. On 21 February 2019, according to Kirsten Müller-Vahl and colleagues at Hannover Medical School, a 22-year-old man launched the YouTube channel Gewitter im Kopf (in their rendering, "thunderstorm in the brain"); the channel reached a million subscribers in less than three months, and by 2 January 2022 it had 2.20 million subscribers and 336 videos "viewed 315 826 001 times in total", with merchandise and a mobile app reproducing its most popular exclamations ([Müller-Vahl and colleagues, Brain, 2022](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9014744/fullTextXML)). The first patient of what the authors called "mass social media-induced illness" came to their clinic in May 2019, three months after the launch, and all their patients said they had watched the videos before, or in some cases during, the appearance of similar or identical symptoms (same). On 4 June 2019 the two main German Tourette advocacy groups distanced themselves from the channel, according to the authors because of its misrepresentation of and disrespect to people with Tourette syndrome (same). The authors judged most of the behaviours shown in the videos to be functional rather than tics. Their label was contested in the same journal by researchers and a community member writing under the title "A call for caution: 'stop that' sentiments threaten tic research, healthcare and advocacy" ([Conelea and colleagues, Brain 145(4), May 2022, PubMed record](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=35213691)), a naming dispute the [opener of this series](/learn/mass-hysteria-how-groups-come-to-believe-anything) describes.

### TikTok in the pandemic

On TikTok the same pattern ran through many creators at once. Clinicians in Chicago reviewed videos tagged "tic", "Tourette" or "tourettes" from the most popular creators between 11 March 2020 and 30 March 2021: the creators' mean age was 18.8, most were women, arm movements were the most frequent behaviour, they averaged 29 tic-like movements a minute, and features rare in typical tic disorders were present in the overwhelming majority; the authors concluded: "We believe this to be an example of mass sociogenic illness" ([Olvera, Stebbins, Goetz and Kompoliti, Movement Disorders Clinical Practice 8(8), November 2021, PubMed abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=34765687)). A team of paediatric neurologists rated the 100 most-viewed videos under "#tourettes" and concluded that "TS symptom portrayals on highly viewed TikTok videos are predominantly not representative or typical of TS" (TS: Tourette syndrome) ([Zea Vera and colleagues, Pediatric Neurology 130, May 2022, PubMed abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=35303587)). In a series of six teenage girls with explosive onset of tic-like movements, "All patients reported exposure to a specific social media personality before symptom onset", which the authors read as spread "in a way previously requiring physical proximity" ([Hull and Parnes, Movement Disorders Clinical Practice 8(8), November 2021, PubMed abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=34765689)). A 2022 review counted "#tourette" as viewed "nearly 5 billion times on the TikTok platform alone" during the pandemic, and also recorded that users said social media portrayals of tics had brought "peer support and a sense of belonging" ([Frey and colleagues, 2022](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9733629/fullTextXML)).

TikTok's answer, given to NBC 6 South Florida, was: "We consult with experts who have cautioned that correlation does not mean causation. People living with Tourette syndrome use TikTok to express themselves authentically, find community, and fight stigma, and we continue our wholehearted commitment to supporting a safe and welcoming environment for our diverse community of viewers and creators" ([NBC 6 South Florida, page dated 3 May 2022](https://nbcmiami.com/news/health/can-tiktok-trigger-tics-doctors-see-rise-in-teen-girls-experiencing-them/2751779)). The point is fair as far as it goes: the studies above describe videos and patients' exposure, and none is a controlled test of causation.

### How the wave ended for the patients

Follow-up studies suggest the wave receded as patients recovered rather than as platforms intervened. At Boston Children's Hospital, of 56 patients aged 10 to 18 with new functional tic-like behaviours from March 2020, 96% were female-assigned at birth, and "up to 79% of patients improved independent of comorbid diagnosis or treatment" ([Tomczak and colleagues, Brain and Behavior 14(3), March 2024, PubMed abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=38468457)). The Hannover group followed 30 of its patients for a mean of 26.3 months: 63% reported improvement and 23% complete remission; better prognosis went with younger age, male sex, early diagnosis and absence of depression, "while diagnostic acceptance or changes in daily social media time had no influence on prognosis", and 36.7% said that simply knowing the correct diagnosis helped ([Hartung and colleagues, Movement Disorders Clinical Practice 13(3), March 2026, PubMed abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=41059647)). That last finding cuts against the simplest platform story: reducing screen time did not, in this small sample, predict recovery.

### Beyond tics

Psychiatrists have proposed similar readings for other conditions presented on platforms. One paper suggested that "many of those self-diagnosed with DID may be manifesting a similar, technologically-facilitated conversion phenomenon" (DID: dissociative identity disorder), or something closer to factitious disorder, and proposed an umbrella term, "Social Media Associated Abnormal Illness Behavior" ([Giedinghagen, Clinical Child Psychology and Psychiatry 28(1), January 2023, PubMed abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=35473358)). Another noted that online mental-health disclosure "can have significant benefits, including reducing mental health stigma, providing peer and social support", while also offering "opportunities for monetary and other forms of secondary gain" ([Porter and colleagues, Journal of the American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry 63(2), February 2024, PubMed abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=37271332)). Of the 100 most popular TikTok videos about attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) in one study, 52 were classified as misleading, 27 as personal experience and 21 as useful, and non-healthcare providers uploaded most of the misleading ones ([Yeung, Ng and Abi-Jaoude, Canadian Journal of Psychiatry 67(12), December 2022, full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9659797/fullTextXML)). <Claim status="disputed" by="by clinicians who see social modelling of symptoms, against patients and advocates who stress real suffering and the support found online">How much of these presentations the platforms cause</Claim> is not settled by any of these studies, which describe content and clinic samples.

## Viral challenges and hoax panics

### Challenges, hoaxes and the warning as vector

Teenagers describe most online challenges as safe. A survey of more than 10,000 teenagers, parents and teachers in ten countries, Brazil among them, commissioned by TikTok in 2021, found that 48% of teenagers described a recent challenge they had seen as safe and 32% as involving some risk but still safe, while 3% described it as very dangerous, and 0.3% said they had taken part in one they considered very dangerous ([TikTok, Helping our community stay safe while having fun on TikTok, 17 November 2021](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/helping-our-community-stay-safe-while-having-fun-on-tiktok?lang=en)). The same research described self-harm hoaxes, which "attempt to make people believe something frightening that isn't true", and which "largely spread through warning messages encouraging others to alert as many people as possible"; it reported that "31 percent of teens exposed to these hoaxes had experienced a negative impact", 63% of them on their mental health, and that 56% of parents would not mention a self-harm hoax unless their teenager did first (same). The research was commissioned by the company whose platform it concerns.

The UK's safer internet charity SWGFL calls these stories "digital ghost stories" and warns that "Warnings and content about digital ghost stories merely goes to raise curiosity and drive traffic to the very content that is of concern" ([SWGFL, Digital Ghost Stories: Impact, Risks and Reasons](https://www.swgfl.org.uk/research/digital-ghost-stories-impact-risks-and-reasons/)). The technology journalist Larry Magid, citing David Finkelhor, director of the University of New Hampshire's Crimes Against Children Research Center, calls the reaction "juvenoia", an "exaggerated fear about the influence of social change on children and youth" ([Larry Magid, Parenting for a Digital Future, London School of Economics, 21 September 2018](http://blogs.lse.ac.uk/parenting4digitalfuture/2018/09/21/dire-warnings-about-children-dying-because-of-apps-and-games-are-a-form-of-juvenoia/)). Writing in the Skeptical Inquirer, Benjamin Radford adds that police, teachers and others "often end up legitimizing the stories and making them more credible" by addressing them ([Benjamin Radford, Skeptical Inquirer, 27 February 2019](https://www.csicop.org/specialarticles/show/the_momo_challenge_and_the_blue_whale_game_online_suicide_game_conspiracies)). How a rumour spreads by being repeated, even to deny it, is treated in [Rumour, panic and lynching](/learn/rumour-panic-and-lynching); the concept of moral panic, and the critiques of it, in [Witch hunts and moral panics](/learn/witch-hunts-and-moral-panics).

### The Blue Whale, 2016 to 2017

The "Blue Whale" story began with a newspaper article. In May 2016 Galina Mursalieva wrote in Novaya Gazeta, an investigative newspaper, that inside certain online groups "curators" set players 50 tasks over 50 days, the last of them an instruction to take their own life; the report "estimated that 130 children might have killed themselves between November 2015 and April 2016" because of their participation in these groups, and was viewed over one and a half million times, according to the paper ([Ant Adeane, BBC, Blue Whale: What is the truth behind an online 'suicide challenge'?, 13 January 2019](https://bbc.in/2TIYti3); [Novaya Gazeta, 16 May 2016](https://novayagazeta.ru/articles/2016/05/16/68604-gruppy-smerti-18)). The investigative journalist Evgeny Berg of Meduza, an independent Russian-language outlet, traced the figure of 130 to the count made by the father of a girl who had died at the end of 2015, whose organization produced a brochure that implied foreign intelligence might be to blame; Mursalieva rejected that account, telling the BBC that the number "was based on the conclusions of over 200 parents who lost their children" ([BBC, 2019](https://bbc.in/2TIYti3)). The BBC's conclusion, after re-examining the story, was that "not a single suicide has so far been proved to be linked to these groups, neither by journalists nor police" (same).

Researchers who entered the groups found an audience waiting for a game. Alexandra Arkhipova, a professor of folklore studies at the Russian State University for the Humanities, told the BBC that the "curators" her team met "turned out to be children aged 12 to 14", copycats acting out a game they had read about in the press, and that "In all these groups people, mainly young people, were waiting for this game"; "This game never starts" (same). Philipp Budeikin, arrested in November 2016 and presented as the game's creator, faced 15 charges, and by the following month "all but one had collapsed", according to the BBC; friends told Berg he had filled groups with shock content to gather followers and then advertise his music (same). On 17 July 2017 the district court in Tobolsk sentenced him to three years and four months in a penal colony settlement, finding him guilty, in the words of the judge's aide quoted by The Moscow Times, "of inciting two girls, 16 and 17, to commit suicide"; according to the investigators' account reported by Zona Media, one was talked out of it and the other was saved by doctors ([Zona Media, 18 July 2017](https://zona.media/news/2017/07/18/lis); [The Moscow Times, 19 July 2017](https://www.themoscowtimes.com/all/58446?amp=1)). The BBC's 2019 account gives a different date and term for his sentence (10 May 2017, three years); the two contemporary reports say he pleaded guilty in May and was sentenced in July ([BBC, 2019](https://bbc.in/2TIYti3); [The Moscow Times, 2017](https://www.themoscowtimes.com/all/58446?amp=1)). A governor in Russia compared the game on television to the Islamic State group (same), and an expert at the Bulgarian Safer Internet Centre, quoted by Anne Collier and then by Larry Magid, said that Russian politicians had named "'Western intelligence services' and 'Ukrainian nationalists'" as its creators ([Magid, 2018](http://blogs.lse.ac.uk/parenting4digitalfuture/2018/09/21/dire-warnings-about-children-dying-because-of-apps-and-games-are-a-form-of-juvenoia/)).

### Brazil, April 2017

The story reached Brazil in April 2017, at the same moment as the Netflix series 13 Reasons Why (released on 31 March). At a public hearing of the Chamber of Deputies' science and communication committee on 9 May 2017, the president of SaferNet Brasil, Thiago Tavares, called Blue Whale a classic example of a hoax, which had arrived on 1 April through a national television news programme using images from a report broadcast in Russia in March 2016; he said: "Não encontramos nenhuma evidência que comprove a existência de estrutura centralizada de comando e controle que criaria grupos em aplicativos de mensagens ou fóruns em redes sociais" ("We found no evidence of any centralized command-and-control structure creating groups in messaging apps or social-media forums"), and that the evidence pointed to decentralized groups created by individuals, "na maioria adolescentes, em situação de vulnerabilidade" ("mostly vulnerable adolescents"), to practise cyberbullying and, in isolated cases, to induce others to suicide ([Conselho Federal de Psicologia, Jogo Baleia Azul: a internet estimula o suicídio?, 10 May 2017](https://site.cfp.org.br/jogo-baleia-azul-internet-estimula-o-suicidio/)). The president of the Federal Council of Psychology, Rogério Giannini, told the same hearing: "não podemos fomentar o pânico social de que desafios induzidos pela internet sejam a principal causa de suicídios entre os jovens brasileiros" ("we cannot feed the social panic that challenges induced by the internet are the main cause of suicides among young Brazilians") (same).

Local authorities faced real cases whose link to the game they could not establish. On 18 April 2017 Curitiba's municipal health department issued an alert to parents and schools after five suicide attempts by adolescents in a single night; its mental-health coordinator said: "Não temos como confirmar a relação, mas nos chama a atenção o padrão similar dos casos" ("We cannot confirm the link, but the similar pattern of the cases catches our attention") ([Gazeta do Povo, 18 April 2017](https://www.gazetadopovo.com.br/curitiba/jogo-baleia-azul-e-seriado-do-netflix-fazem-curitiba-emitir-alerta-apos-tentativas-de-suicidio-de-adolescentes-0ztc5o326cvy4jthu9kc65h2t)). At least eight states and the Federal District had opened investigations ([Jornal de Brasília, 21 April 2017](https://jornaldebrasilia.com.br/brasilia/oito-estados-e-o-df-investigam-tentativas-de-suicidio-supostamente-motivadas-pelo-baleia-azul/)). Help-seeking rose too: the CVV received 1,840 e-mails between 1 and 10 April 2017, against 635 in the same days of March, its daily average of 55 e-mails passed 300, and unique visitors to its site rose from an average of 2,500 to 6,770 a day ([IstoÉ Dinheiro, from O Estado de S. Paulo, 11 April 2017](https://istoedinheiro.com.br/serie-faz-crescer-em-445-busca-pelo-cvv)). Netflix's communications manager in Brazil, Amanda Vidigal, said the company had taken "total cuidado" ("total care"), with health consultants, a 30-minute after-show and a website listing support groups; the three hardest episodes carried warnings (same).

The law followed two years later. In April 2019 Law 13,819 created the National Policy for the Prevention of Self-Mutilation and Suicide, with a free and confidential telephone service for people in psychological distress and the compulsory notification of suspected or confirmed self-inflicted violence by health services (to health authorities) and by schools (to the child protection councils) ([Senado Federal, Lei nº 13.819, de 26 de abril de 2019](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/30920297/publicacao/30920455)). In October 2019 the Chamber approved a Senate bill making it a crime to induce or instigate self-harm; until then, as the Chamber's news agency explained, the crime of inducing suicide was consummated only if death or serious injury followed, and the rapporteur, deputy Caroline de Toni, said the change aimed at the so-called deadly challenges, naming "o jogo da baleia azul" and "o desafio da Momo" among others ([Agência Câmara, 29 October 2019](https://www.camara.leg.br/noticias/606247-camara-aprova-punicao-para-quem-induzir-pessoas-a-automutilacao/)). Law 13,968 of 26 December 2019 rewrote article 122 of the Penal Code as "Induzimento, instigação ou auxílio a suicídio ou a automutilação" ("Inducing, instigating or assisting suicide or self-mutilation"), with the penalty increased up to double when the conduct takes place through a computer network, a social network or a live broadcast, and by half "se o agente é líder ou coordenador de grupo ou de rede virtual" ("if the agent is the leader or coordinator of a group or virtual network") ([Presidência da República, Lei nº 13.968, de 26 de dezembro de 2019](https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/_ato2019-2022/2019/lei/l13968.htm)).

### Momo, 2018 to 2019

The image behind "Momo" was a sculpture of a bird-woman exhibited in 2016 at a gallery in Ginza, Tokyo; in July 2018 BBC News Brasil described how it spread on WhatsApp with warnings from police in Mexico and Spain, and quoted Rodrigo Nejm of SaferNet, who said: "É mais uma isca usada por criminosos pra roubar dados e extorquir pessoas na internet" ("It is one more bait used by criminals to steal data and extort people on the internet"), and that the organization had been contacted by worried parents and educators but "ainda não recebeu nenhuma denúncia específica" ("has not yet received any specific report") ([BBC News Brasil, 26 July 2018](https://www.bbc.com/portuguese/salasocial-44961410)). Its maker, the artist Keisuke Aiso, told reporters in 2019 that he had thrown the rubber sculpture away the year before, "It doesn't exist anymore, it was never meant to last", and that "I do feel a little responsible for it" ([Rolling Stone, 4 March 2019](https://www.rollingstone.com/culture/culture-news/momo-sculpture-destroyed-keisuke-aiso-802627/)).

In the United Kingdom the scare of February 2019 shows the feedback loop step by step. Full Fact found "no clear evidence that anyone has actually been subject to the Momo challenge as described"; national coverage, minimal after a small flurry in August 2018, "began spiking dramatically on 26 February, the day after the Police Service of Northern Ireland (PSNI) put out a" public statement that itself noted no official reports had been made to police and cited "media reports"; the police and a company called National Online Safety "seem to have largely relied on press reports for their information", and "their public comments were then treated by the press as additional confirmation that the claims were true"; schools emailed warnings to parents, and some held assemblies on the subject ([Full Fact, 1 March 2019](https://fullfact.org/online/momo-challenge-hoax/)). YouTube said no one at the company had seen "recent evidence of videos promoting the Momo Challenge on YouTube" and that "videos encouraging harmful and dangerous challenges are against our policies" ([The Verge, 1 March 2019](https://www.theverge.com/2019/3/1/18244890/momo-youtube-news-hoax-demonetization-comments-kids)). SWGFL concluded that "it was quickly established that there was no evidence of children coming to harm" ([SWGFL](https://www.swgfl.org.uk/research/digital-ghost-stories-impact-risks-and-reasons/)).

### The school-threat rumour, December 2021

A hoax need not involve self-harm to show the same mechanism. In December 2021 warnings of violence at schools across the United States on 17 December spread on social media, after a school shooting in Michigan that left four students dead; some districts closed and police presence increased. The Department of Homeland Security wrote that it "does not have any information indicating any specific, credible threats to schools but recommends communities remain alert". TikTok, where the rumours were discussed, wrote that it had "exhaustively searched for content that promotes violence at schools today, but have still found nothing", that "What we find are videos discussing this rumor and warning others to stay safe", and that it was working "to remove alarmist warnings that violate our misinformation policy" ([Good Morning America, ABC News, 17 December 2021](https://www.goodmorningamerica.com/news/story/dhs-recommends-schools-remain-alert-amid-unfounded-threats-81811521)). The warnings were the content.

### When a challenge is real: a court and an algorithm

Some dangerous challenges are real, and courts have begun to ask what recommendation systems owe their users. In Anderson v. TikTok, brought by the mother of a ten-year-old girl who died after attempting a dangerous challenge shown to her on the platform, the US Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit held on 27 August 2024 that when TikTok's algorithm curates and recommends content to a user, the company "makes choices about the content recommended and promoted to specific users, and by doing so, is engaged in its own first-party speech", so that the immunity of section 230 of the Communications Decency Act, which protects platforms from liability for third-party content, did not bar the claims; it reversed in part, vacated in part and remanded ([US Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit, Anderson v. TikTok, Inc., No. 22-3061, 27 August 2024, GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-ca3-22-03061/pdf/USCOURTS-ca3-22-03061-0.pdf)). Eric Goldman, on his Technology and Marketing Law Blog, called it a "Bonkers Opinion" that "Repeals Section 230 In the Third Circuit" ([Eric Goldman, Technology and Marketing Law Blog, 29 August 2024](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2024/08/bonkers-opinion-repeals-section-230-in-the-third-circuit-anderson-v-tiktok.htm)). The ruling decided only that the claims could proceed, not whether TikTok is liable. The wider legal framework, from section 230 to Brazil's Marco Civil, is in [Platform law](/learn/platform-law-section-230-the-dsa-and-the-marco-civil).

## Suicide contagion: what the evidence shows

### Werther: a name with a sourcing problem

The WHO's resource explains the term: the effect of media reports on increasing suicides is called "suicide contagion" and the "Werther effect", after the hero of Goethe's novel The Sorrows of Young Werther (1774), and "The publication of Goethe's novel was followed by a number of suicides" ([WHO, 2023](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/92773496-dc8b-4d42-aef3-3a5a517dfa63/content)). The same resource adds that the book was banned in several European countries, and that evidence of imitation remained anecdotal until the 1970s, when David Phillips compared the number of suicides in the United States in months with a front-page article on suicide against months without one, finding a significant increase in 26 of 33 such months; his 1974 article in the American Sociological Review, listed in its references, carries the phrase "Werther effect" in its title (same). The history behind the name is thinner than its fame. A 2024 study notes that "evidence for a suicide 'epidemic' after the publication of Goethe's Werther is limited to unsystematically documented cases"; searching German-language newspapers since 1774, it confirmed most previously reported cases and found eight more attributed to the novel, but observed that "newspapers reported on the Werther-epidemic itself and the moral implications of glamorizing suicide much more than on specific imitative suicides", and concluded that "Whether the reports identified by this study substantiate a 'Werther epidemic' remains up to interpretation" ([Mestas, Health Communication 39(7), June 2024, PubMed abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=37170580)). Like the "meowing nuns" traced in [Dancing plagues and possessed convents](/learn/dancing-plagues-and-possessed-convents), the founding anecdote was itself a story that spread.

### The meta-analysis

The modern evidence is stronger than the anecdote. A systematic review and meta-analysis identified 31 studies and included 20, at moderate risk of bias, in its main analyses; it found that "The risk of suicide increased by 13% in the period after the media reported a death of a celebrity by suicide" (rate ratio 1.13, 95% confidence interval 1.08 to 1.18; 14 studies); when the method used by the celebrity was reported, there was an associated 30% increase in deaths by the same method; general reporting of suicide, measured article by article, was not associated with suicide (rate ratio 1.002 per article); heterogeneity was large and there were signs of publication bias; and "The best available intervention at the population level to deal with the harmful effects of media reports is guidelines for responsible reporting" ([Niederkrotenthaler and colleagues, BMJ 368:m575, 18 March 2020, full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7190013/fullTextXML)).

Social media add a layer of reaction. A study of almost one million messages on Twitter about the suicides of 18 prominent people in Japan from 2010 to 2014 found that "'surprised' reactions" correlated positively with subsequent national suicide counts, "while a high proportion of polite messages of condolence were negatively correlated" ([Fahey, Matsubayashi and Ueda, Social Science and Medicine 219, December 2018, PubMed abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=30342383)). A systematic review of 25 studies on social media concluded: "Suicidal content on social media can be both contagious and protective" ([Calvo and colleagues, Spanish Journal of Psychiatry and Mental Health 19(1), 2026, PubMed abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=38848950)).

### Papageno: stories that protect

The opposite effect has its own name, after the character in Mozart's The Magic Flute who is talked out of suicide. In a content analysis of 497 Austrian press reports from the first half of 2005, "Repetitive reporting of the same suicide and the reporting of suicide myths were positively associated with suicide rates", while articles about people who adopted coping strategies in adverse circumstances were negatively associated with suicide ([Niederkrotenthaler and colleagues, British Journal of Psychiatry 197(3), September 2010, PubMed abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=20807970)). Pooling individual data from randomized trials, a 2022 meta-analysis found that stories of hope and recovery produced a small reduction in suicidal ideation among people with some vulnerability (standardized mean difference -0.22) and no evidence of a difference in help-seeking attitudes ([Niederkrotenthaler and colleagues, Lancet Public Health 7(2), February 2022, PubMed abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=35122759)).

Guidelines have been tested as a natural experiment. In Vienna, after a working group of the Austrian Association for Suicide Prevention developed media guidelines in 1987 and the press agreed to abstain from reporting such cases, the suicides that had drawn the most dramatic coverage in the city fell by 75% in the second half of 1987, and the fall was sustained for five years ([Sonneck, Etzersdorfer and Nagel-Kuess, Social Science and Medicine 38(3), 1994, PubMed abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=8153751)). A later time-series analysis estimated a nationwide reduction of 81 suicides a year (95% confidence interval 13 to 149), concentrated where the collaborating newspapers were most read ([Niederkrotenthaler and Sonneck, Australian and New Zealand Journal of Psychiatry 41(5), May 2007, PubMed abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=17464734)).

### 13 Reasons Why: signals and their critics

Netflix released 13 Reasons Why on 31 March 2017, with a graphic depiction of a teenager's death that suicide prevention organizations criticized at once. The studies that followed disagree, and the disagreement is a lesson in what such data can show.

Netflix's response came in stages. In 2017 the series carried warnings on its three hardest episodes and a 30-minute after-show, according to O Estado de S. Paulo ([IstoÉ Dinheiro, 2017](https://istoedinheiro.com.br/serie-faz-crescer-em-445-busca-pelo-cvv)). In July 2019 it removed the depiction of the death from the first season, explaining: "So on the advice of medical experts, including Dr. Christine Moutier, chief medical officer at the American Foundation for Suicide Prevention, we've decided with creator Brian Yorkey and the producers to edit the scene in which Hannah takes her own life from season one" ([TechCrunch, 16 July 2019](https://techcrunch.com/?p=1856058)).

## What platforms, courts and health authorities did

### Guidelines

The WHO's 2023 update lists what responsible reporting does and avoids: it provides accurate information about where to seek help, at the end of a story "or, even better, at the beginning", with "Only a limited number of services" because a long list can overwhelm; it educates the public with facts; it reports stories of coping; it applies particular caution with celebrities; and it does not describe the method, name the site, oversimplify the reason "to a single factor", place suicide as the top story or repeat it unduly ([WHO, 2023](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/92773496-dc8b-4d42-aef3-3a5a517dfa63/content)). Its annex on online media adds that "Spreading and sharing stories of suicide should be actively discouraged", that stories of people who coped "should be actively encouraged", and points to "#chatsafe" guidelines written to help young people talk about suicide safely (same). The Blue Whale coverage broke several of these rules at once: a single cause, a dramatic count, repetition.

### Platforms

After the Momo scare, YouTube treated videos about it, including news segments, as unsuitable for advertising ([The Verge, 2019](https://www.theverge.com/2019/3/1/18244890/momo-youtube-news-hoax-demonetization-comments-kids)). In November 2021 TikTok said it would "start to remove alarmist warnings" about self-harm hoaxes "as they could cause harm by treating the self-harm hoax as real", show additional resources to people searching for such hoaxes, and alert its safety teams to sudden increases of violating content under a hashtag ([TikTok, 17 November 2021](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/helping-our-community-stay-safe-while-having-fun-on-tiktok?lang=en)). The changes followed the evidence that warnings themselves cause harm; the announcement gave no measure of their effect.

### A coroner, a company's answer, and a statute

In London, the inquest into the death of 14-year-old Molly Russell concluded on 30 September 2022 that she "died from an act of self-harm whilst suffering from depression and the negative effects of on-line content". The coroner's report to prevent future deaths, of 13 October 2022, found that some sites she used "were not safe as they allowed access to adult content that should not have been available for a 14-year-old child to see", that algorithms had produced binge periods of content she did not request, and that the material "affected her mental health in a negative way and contributed to her death in a more than minimal way"; among his concerns were the lack of separation between adult and child users, the lack of age verification, and "That algorithms were used to provide content together with adverts" ([North London Coroner's Service, 13 October 2022](https://www.judiciary.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/Molly-Russell-Prevention-of-future-deaths-report-2022-0315_Published.pdf)). Meta, which operates Instagram, answered on 6 December 2022 that it was "committed to providing a positive experience on Instagram, especially for teenagers", that it supported the government's focus on suicide and self-harm content in the Online Safety Bill, and that, while it did not provide separate platforms for adults and teenagers in the UK, under-18s were defaulted into private accounts ([Meta Platforms Ireland, response to the Regulation 28 report, 6 December 2022](https://www.judiciary.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/2022-0315-Response-from-META.pdf)). The Online Safety Act 2023 made it an offence in the UK to do an act, including sending or publishing a communication electronically, intended to encourage or assist the serious self-harm of another person, "whether or not serious self-harm occurs" ([Online Safety Act 2023, section 184, legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/section/184)).

### Health authorities

The US Surgeon General's advisory on social media and youth mental health states: "We cannot conclude that social media is sufficiently safe for children and adolescents", reports that up to 95% of 13- to 17-year-olds use a social media platform, and calls on platforms to commission independent assessments and share data with researchers and on policymakers to set age-appropriate safety standards ([US Department of Health and Human Services, Social Media and Youth Mental Health, page last reviewed 19 February 2025](https://www.hhs.gov/surgeongeneral/reports-and-publications/youth-mental-health/social-media/index.html)). It also names, as an open question, which kinds of content are most harmful (same).

In Brazil, the CVV completed the national roll-out of 188 on 1 July 2018, after an agreement with the Ministério da Saúde (Ministry of Health), making it the first free emotional-support number available 24 hours a day in every state; more than 2,000 volunteers handled about two million contacts in 2025 ([CVV, CVV celebra os oito anos do 188, 2 July 2026](https://cvv.org.br/cvv-celebra-os-oito-anos-do-188-que-aproximou-a-escuta-de-milhoes-de-brasileiros/)). The CVV's timeline dates the pilot of 188 in Rio Grande do Sul to 2015 and the agreement with the ministry to 2017, and says the national service arrived almost two years ahead of schedule ([CVV, Nossa história](https://cvv.org.br/nossa-historia/)); its anniversary note instead places the agreement "quase dois anos antes" ("almost two years before") July 2018. The Ministry's epidemiological bulletin of February 2024 records 15,507 suicides in Brazil in 2021 and a 42% rise in the mortality rate between 2010 and 2021, from 5.2 to 7.5 per 100,000 inhabitants ([Ministério da Saúde, Boletim Epidemiológico 55(4), 6 February 2024](https://www.gov.br/saude/pt-br/centrais-de-conteudo/publicacoes/boletins/epidemiologicos/edicoes/2024/boletim-epidemiologico-volume-55-no-04.pdf)).

### Brazilian courts and the new statute for children online

On 26 June 2025 the Supreme Federal Court (Supremo Tribunal Federal, STF) held article 19 of the Marco Civil da Internet partly unconstitutional and set out a duty of care ("dever de cuidado"): internet application providers are liable when they fail to make immediately unavailable content that amounts to a closed list of serious crimes, including "crimes de induzimento, instigação ou auxílio a suicídio ou a automutilação, nos termos do art. 122 do Código Penal" ("crimes of inducing, instigating or assisting suicide or self-mutilation, under article 122 of the Penal Code"), a liability that "diz respeito à configuração de falha sistêmica" ("concerns the occurrence of a systemic failure") ([STF, Responsabilidade de plataformas digitais por conteúdo de terceiros, RE 1.037.396 and RE 1.057.258, judgment of 26 June 2025, information sheet](https://sipla.ip.mpg.de/fileadmin/Files/Files/STF_Responsabilidade_de_plataformas_digitais_por_conteudo_de_terceiros.pdf); [Agência Brasil, 26 June 2025](https://agenciabrasil.ebc.com.br/justica/noticia/2025-06/stf-decide-que-redes-devem-ser-responsabilizadas-por-conteudos-ilegais)). In June 2026 the court added a safeguard to the final thesis, proposed by its president, Edson Fachin: platforms are not punished for keeping content up if they show there was reasonable doubt about its illegality after qualified analysis ([Olhar Digital, 17 June 2026](https://olhardigital.com.br/2026/06/17/internet-e-redes-sociais/stf-cria-protecao-para-redes-sociais-em-casos-de-conteudo-ilicito/)). The Statute of the Child and Adolescent in the Digital Environment (Law 15,211 of 2025, known as the "ECA Digital" after the Portuguese initials of the Estatuto da Criança e do Adolescente) came into force on 17 March 2026 and obliges technology companies to remove content including publications related to "automutilação e suicídio" ("self-mutilation and suicide"), alongside age verification and parental supervision tools ([Agência Senado, 17 March 2026](https://www12.senado.leg.br/noticias/materias/2026/03/17/eca-digital-para-protecao-on-line-de-criancas-e-adolescentes-entra-em-vigor); [Agência Câmara, 18 September 2025](https://www.camara.leg.br/noticias/1201525-NOVA-LEI-PROTEGE-CRIANCAS-CONTRA-ADULTIZACAO-NA-INTERNET)). How content moderation works at that scale, and where it fails, is in [Content moderation at scale](/learn/content-moderation-at-scale).

## Who steered, and who did not

In most of these episodes nobody steered. The tic-like wave spread through imitation and attention, not through any plan; the Momo panic was built by worried parents, schools, police and newspapers repeating one another; the December 2021 rumour spread through warnings. That is the pattern of [the human attack surface](/learn/the-human-attack-surface-why-repetition-works): repetition by trusted voices makes a claim feel true, and correction rarely catches up, as [why misbeliefs persist](/learn/why-misbeliefs-persist) shows.

Some people did profit or exploit. Influencers sold merchandise around their symptoms ([Frey and colleagues, 2022](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9733629/fullTextXML)); a young man in Russia, by his friends' account, filled groups with shock content to gain followers for his music, and was convicted for pushing two girls towards suicide ([BBC, 2019](https://bbc.in/2TIYti3); [Zona Media, 2017](https://zona.media/news/2017/07/18/lis)); SaferNet warned that criminals used Momo as bait for data theft and extortion ([BBC News Brasil, 2018](https://www.bbc.com/portuguese/salasocial-44961410)). Others turned the panic to their own ends: a bereaved father's organization implied that foreign intelligence was behind the deaths, and Russian politicians, by a safer-internet expert's account, blamed Western services and Ukrainian nationalists ([BBC, 2019](https://bbc.in/2TIYti3); [Magid, 2018](http://blogs.lse.ac.uk/parenting4digitalfuture/2018/09/21/dire-warnings-about-children-dying-because-of-apps-and-games-are-a-form-of-juvenoia/)). How states and manipulators exploit fear is the subject of [How manipulators harness collective panic](/learn/how-manipulators-harness-collective-panic) and of [the psychological operations series](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign).

The systems themselves are a third kind of actor. A recommendation algorithm has no intent, but a coroner found that it served a child content she did not request, and a US appeals court held that its choices are the platform's own speech ([North London Coroner's Service, 2022](https://www.judiciary.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/Molly-Russell-Prevention-of-future-deaths-report-2022-0315_Published.pdf); [Third Circuit, 2024](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-ca3-22-03061/pdf/USCOURTS-ca3-22-03061-0.pdf)). Defences against hoax panics before they start, by explaining the technique rather than repeating the claim, are discussed in [prebunking and inoculation](/learn/prebunking-and-inoculation), and checking a viral warning is a task for [verification and fact-checking](/learn/verification-and-fact-checking).

If this subject affects you, in Brazil the CVV answers free on 188 at any hour, and by chat at cvv.org.br; elsewhere, the IASP's directory lists crisis lines by country ([CVV](https://cvv.org.br/); [IASP](https://www.iasp.info/crisis-centres-helplines/)).

## What to remember

- Platforms changed the carriers of contagion: a virtual index case, warnings that spread a hoax, feeds that choose for the user, and attention that pays.
- The tic-like wave of 2019 to 2022 followed specific channels and creators; most patients improved, and in one follow-up changes in screen time did not predict recovery.
- No suicide was proved linked to the Blue Whale groups, and Momo had no clear victims; in both, warnings by police, schools and newspapers fed the panic. Brazil answered with Law 13,819 (2019) and Law 13,968 (2019), which punishes inducing self-harm, more severely online.
- Reports of celebrity suicides are followed by a 13% rise in suicides on average; stories of coping and recovery have small protective effects; guidelines for the press lowered suicides in Austria.
- The 13 Reasons Why studies found signals consistent with contagion that reanalyses dispute; protective effects for some viewers are better supported.
- Responses now include the WHO's guidelines, platform policies against alarmist hoax warnings, a UK coroner's findings and the Online Safety Act, and in Brazil the 188 line, the STF's duty of care for article 122 crimes and the ECA Digital.
