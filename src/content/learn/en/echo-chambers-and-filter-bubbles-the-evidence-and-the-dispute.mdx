Two images dominate the public debate about social media and division. In the echo chamber, people surround themselves with voices that repeat their own views back to them. In the filter bubble, software does the surrounding: ranking systems learn what each person likes and quietly hide the rest. Both images predict that people will see less of the other side and grow more extreme. Both have been tested, with results that surprised many who used the terms, and the largest tests, run with Meta during the 2020 United States (US) election, became a dispute in their own right between the company, the journal that published them and outside researchers. This part of the series opened by [the map from the small lie to the campaign](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign) sets out the definitions, the main studies and what each changed and measured, the critics of the concepts and the critics of the critics, and the Brazilian research on WhatsApp groups and YouTube. How messages travel through networks is in [the previous part, on reach](/learn/from-the-lodge-to-the-network-how-reach-changed); how much misinformation people actually see is in [the human attack surface](/learn/the-human-attack-surface-why-repetition-works); what platforms disclose about their recommendation systems is the subject of the part on [recommendation and profiling](/learn/recommendation-and-profiling-what-platforms-disclose).

## Two images, two mechanisms

### The filter bubble

The filter bubble is Eli Pariser's phrase. TED's page for the talk he gave in March 2011 summarizes it: "As web companies strive to tailor their services (including news and search results) to our personal tastes, there's a dangerous unintended consequence: We get trapped in a 'filter bubble' and don't get exposed to information that could challenge or broaden our worldview" ([TED, Eli Pariser, Beware online "filter bubbles", recorded 3 March 2011, published 2 May 2011](https://www.ted.com/talks/eli_pariser_beware_online_filter_bubbles)). In his book of the same year, as the Reuters Institute's review quotes it, personalization would create "a unique universe of information for each of us" ([Arguedas, Robertson, Fletcher and Nielsen, Echo chambers, filter bubbles, and polarisation: a literature review, Reuters Institute, 19 January 2022](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/echo-chambers-filter-bubbles-and-polarisation-literature-review)). The transcript on TED's site did not load for automated reading when this part was researched and is listed for reading in a browser.

Axel Bruns, of the Queensland University of Technology's Digital Media Research Centre, notes that Pariser opened the book with an anecdote about two friends who searched for "BP" in the spring of 2010 and saw very different results, and argues that "Pariser largely fails to provide a clear definition for the 'filter bubble' concept; it remains vague and founded in anecdotes" ([Bruns, Filter bubble, Internet Policy Review 8(4), 29 November 2019](https://policyreview.info/concepts/filter-bubble)). He adds that the term, first about search results, has since moved to social media, where it is often used interchangeably with the older image of the echo chamber.

### The echo chamber

Social scientists often use the definition from Jamieson and Cappella's 2008 book Echo Chamber, as the Reuters Institute review gives it: "a bounded, enclosed media space that has the potential to both magnify the messages delivered within it and insulate them from rebuttal" ([Arguedas et al., 19 January 2022](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/echo-chambers-filter-bubbles-and-polarisation-literature-review)). The review draws the distinction that matters for everything below. An echo chamber "does not prejudge why some people might live in such bubbles", since people may choose them; a filter bubble is "an echo chamber primarily produced by ranking algorithms engaged in passive personalisation without any active choice on our part". The first is a question about people's choices and the whole of their media diet; the second is a question about software.

Bruns proposes minimal definitions for network data: an echo chamber "emerges when a group of participants choose to preferentially connect with each other, to the exclusion of outsiders", and a filter bubble when they "choose to preferentially communicate with each other, to the exclusion of outsiders" ([Bruns, 29 November 2019](https://policyreview.info/concepts/filter-bubble)). The two sets of definitions do not match, which is part of the dispute: Bruns writes that empirical studies "have generally been forced to introduce their own definitions, which reduces their comparability".

Two other terms recur. Selective exposure is the choice of like-minded sources, and the review distinguishes it from selective avoidance of cross-cutting ones, finding that "while many people do engage in some degree of selective exposure, they do not necessarily engage in selective avoidance". Affective polarization "refers to how much opposing partisans dislike one another", which the review finds "clearly seems to be on the rise" in the US, in contrast to ideological polarization ([Arguedas et al., 2022](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/echo-chambers-filter-bubbles-and-polarisation-literature-review)).

## A first test inside Facebook, 2015

In May 2015 three Facebook researchers, Eytan Bakshy, Solomon Messing and Lada Adamic, published in Science an analysis of how 10.1 million US Facebook users interacted with socially shared news, separating what friends shared, what the News Feed ranking showed and what users clicked; they concluded that <Claim status="disputed" by="by critics of the sample">"Compared with algorithmic ranking, individuals' choices played a stronger role in limiting exposure to cross-cutting content"</Claim> ([Bakshy, Messing and Adamic, Exposure to ideologically diverse news and opinion on Facebook, Science, 7 May 2015, abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:%2210.1126/science.aaa1160%22&format=json&resultType=core)). As quoted by Journalist's Resource, the paper found that after ranking "conservatives see approximately 5% less cross-cutting content compared to what friends share, while liberals see about 8% less ideologically diverse content", that individual choice lowered the likelihood of clicking a cross-cutting article by 17% for conservatives and 6% for liberals, and that "on average more than 20 percent of an individual's Facebook friends who report an ideological affiliation are from the opposing party" ([Journalist's Resource, Harvard Kennedy School Shorenstein Center, May 2015](https://journalistsresource.org/?p=44819)).

The study drew criticism on the day it appeared. Writing on the blog Crooked Timber, Eszter Hargittai pointed to a line in the paper's supplementary materials, that only 9% of US adult users self-report a political affiliation and only 46% of those in a form that could be mapped to an ideological scale, and to the requirement that users log in at least four days a week; she concluded that "only a tiny percentage (single digits) of Facebook users were eligible to make it into the sample in the first place", people who "probably have a different relationship to politics than the average user" ([Hargittai, Crooked Timber, 7 May 2015](https://crookedtimber.org/2015/05/07/why-doesnt-science-publish-important-methods-info-prominently/)). David Lazer, writing a commentary in Science on the study, as Journalist's Resource quotes him, credited Facebook "for building a talented research group and for conducting this research in a public way", and added that "there is a broader need for scientists to study these systems in a manner that is independent of the Facebooks of the world" ([Journalist's Resource, May 2015](https://journalistsresource.org/?p=44819)). Pariser answered that the study had not killed his thesis, recalling that he had warned of the filter bubble, "the personalized universe of information that makes it into our feed" ([Pariser, Backchannel, 7 May 2015](https://medium.com/backchannel/facebook-published-a-big-new-study-on-the-filter-bubble-here-s-what-it-says-ef31a292da95)); Christian Sandvig and Zeynep Tufekci also published replies.

## The 2020 election studies with Meta

### How the project was set up

Ahead of the 2020 election Meta and a group of outside academics set up the US 2020 Facebook and Instagram Election Study, the academics led by Talia Jomini Stroud of the University of Texas at Austin and Joshua A. Tucker of New York University; Meta's president of global affairs, Nick Clegg, wrote that the external researchers "were not paid by Meta and they don't answer to us either" and that neither Meta's researchers nor the company "have the authority to restrict their findings" ([Meta, Nick Clegg, 27 July 2023](https://about.fb.com/news/2023/07/research-social-media-impact-elections/)). The authors of one of the papers describe the terms: "the lead academic authors had final say on the analysis plan", "Meta could not block any results from being published", "The academics were not financially compensated and the analysis plan was preregistered prior to data availability"; they also disclose that several co-authors are or were Meta employees and that several academic authors had other financial relationships with the company, such as consulting or grants ([Nyhan et al., Like-minded sources on Facebook are prevalent but not polarizing, Nature, 27 July 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06297-w)). An independent rapporteur, Michael Wagner of the University of Wisconsin, observed the process.

### What the experiments changed, and what they found

Four papers appeared on 27 July 2023, one in Nature and three in Science.

**Like-minded sources.** Brendan Nyhan and colleagues found, for all active adult US Facebook users, that "the median Facebook user received a majority of their content from like-minded sources", 50.4% against 14.7% from cross-cutting sources, but that "Just 20.6% of Facebook users get over 75% of their exposures from like-minded sources" ([Nyhan et al., Nature, 27 July 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06297-w)). In an experiment with 23,377 consenting users from 24 September to 23 December 2020, they cut exposure to like-minded sources by about a third, from 53.7% in the control group to 36.2%, and found <Claim status="reported" by="by its authors">"no measurable effects on 8 preregistered attitudinal measures, such as ideological extremity and consistency, party-congenial attitudes and evaluations, and affective polarization"</Claim>, ruling out effects of plus or minus 0.12 standard deviations. Their reading: "These results are not consistent with the worst fears about echo chambers", although "Facebook users are much more likely to see content from like-minded sources than they are to see content from cross-cutting sources". They list the limits themselves: three months cannot capture years of cumulative use; participants used Facebook more than the average user; "only a minority of Facebook users occupy echo chambers yet the reach of the platform means that the group in question is large in absolute terms"; and replications in other countries "will be essential". A correction to the paper was published on 1 November 2023.

**The chronological feed.** Andrew Guess and colleagues moved consenting users to reverse-chronological feeds for three months. "Moving users out of algorithmic feeds substantially decreased the time they spent on the platforms and their activity"; the amount of political and untrustworthy content they saw increased on both platforms; uncivil content decreased on Facebook; and yet <Claim status="reported" by="by its authors">"the chronological feed did not significantly alter levels of issue polarization, affective polarization, political knowledge, or other key attitudes during the 3-month study period"</Claim> ([Guess et al., How do social media feed algorithms affect attitudes and behavior in an election campaign?, Science, 27 July 2023, abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:%2210.1126/science.abp9364%22&format=json&resultType=core)).

**No reshares.** In a second experiment by Guess and colleagues, removing reshared content from feeds "substantially decreases the amount of political news, including content from untrustworthy sources", reduced partisan news clicks and "produces clear decreases in news knowledge", but "does not significantly affect political polarization or any measure of individual-level political attitudes" ([Guess et al., Reshares on social media amplify political news but do not detectably affect beliefs or opinions, Science, 27 July 2023, abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:%2210.1126/science.add8424%22&format=json&resultType=core)).

**Segregation in news exposure.** Sandra González-Bailón and colleagues, with aggregated data for 208 million US users, compared the political news users could have seen, what they saw after ranking and what they engaged with. They found that "ideological segregation is high and increases as we shift from potential exposure to actual exposure to engagement", that "there is an asymmetry between conservative and liberal audiences, with a substantial corner of the news ecosystem consumed exclusively by conservatives", and that <Claim status="reported" by="by its authors">"most misinformation, as identified by Meta's Third-Party Fact-Checking Program, exists within this homogeneously conservative corner, which has no equivalent on the liberal side"</Claim> ([González-Bailón et al., Asymmetric ideological segregation in exposure to political news on Facebook, Science, 27 July 2023, abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:%2210.1126/science.ade7138%22&format=json&resultType=core)).

**Deactivation.** A later paper from the same project randomly assigned 19,857 Facebook users and 15,585 Instagram users to deactivate their accounts for six weeks before the election and found the effects on "affective and issue polarization, perceived legitimacy of the election, candidate favorability, and voter turnout" all "precisely estimated and close to zero"; Facebook deactivation "may have reduced self-reported net votes for Trump, though this effect does not meet our preregistered significance threshold" ([Allcott et al., The effects of Facebook and Instagram on the 2020 election: A deactivation experiment, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 13 May 2024, abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:%2210.1073/pnas.2321584121%22&format=json&resultType=core)). An earlier, independent experiment had found otherwise: deactivating Facebook for the four weeks before the 2018 midterm election "reduced both factual news knowledge and political polarization", in a study funded by the Sloan and Knight foundations and first circulated as a National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) working paper ([Allcott, Braghieri, Eichmeyer and Gentzkow, The Welfare Effects of Social Media, NBER Working Paper 25514, January 2019, published in the American Economic Review 110(3)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25514)).

| Study | What was changed or measured | Main result on attitudes |
|---|---|---|
| Nyhan et al., 2023 | Like-minded exposure cut by about a third for three months | No measurable effect on eight attitude measures |
| Guess et al., 2023 (feeds) | Chronological instead of ranked feed | Less time on platform, more untrustworthy content, no significant effect on polarization |
| Guess et al., 2023 (reshares) | Reshared posts removed | Less political news and knowledge, no significant effect on polarization |
| González-Bailón et al., 2023 | Observational: potential, seen and engaged news | High, asymmetric segregation, rising from what could be seen to what was engaged with |
| Allcott et al., 2024 | Six weeks without Facebook or Instagram | Polarization effects close to zero |
| Allcott et al., 2020 | Four weeks without Facebook, 2018 | Polarization and news knowledge reduced |

## The dispute over what the studies mean

### Meta's reading

On the day of publication Clegg wrote that <Claim status="disputed" by="by Science's editor-in-chief">the experimental findings "add to a growing body of research showing there is little evidence that key features of Meta's platforms alone cause harmful 'affective' polarization, or have meaningful effects on key political attitudes, beliefs or behaviors"</Claim>, while acknowledging that "questions about social media's impact on key political attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors are not fully settled" ([Meta, 27 July 2023](https://about.fb.com/news/2023/07/research-social-media-impact-elections/)).

### Science's reading

Holden Thorp, editor-in-chief of Science, later set out the journal's view. The papers, he wrote, "concluded that the default algorithm did not contribute to polarization as extensively as had been assumed"; in the issue's cover and his editorial, "we suggested that these findings obtained because users were already so segregated by ideology on the site that the default algorithm could not cause further polarization"; and "Facebook took issue with this", repeating claims "which we disputed as overstating the evidence", a dispute in which, he wrote, "so did the academic authors of the study" ([Holden Thorp, More doubts for Facebook on polarization, 27 September 2024](https://holdenthorp.substack.com/p/more-doubts-for-facebook-on-polarization)). He described Wagner's commentary in the issue, titled "independence by permission": "while Dr. Wagner concluded that the researchers had independence in analyzing the data, they were only analyzing data that Facebook permitted them to examine". Wagner's commentary is behind the journal's paywall; its abstract says he concluded "that the team conducted rigorous, carefully checked, transparent, ethical, and path-breaking studies" ([Wagner, Science, 27 July 2023, abstract](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adi2430)). The journal's 2024 editorial, by Thorp and Valda Vinson, reported Bagchi et al.'s point that Facebook had applied "a set of 63 emergency measures designed to counter any political instability" during the experiment, wrote that the authors "insist that the conclusions of the paper still stand", and explained that "Science is not correcting the paper but is posting a link to this editorial on the paper to alert readers who may cite the work that the default algorithm was in flux during the experiment"; Meta, it added, "disagrees" and stood behind Clegg's statements ([Thorp and Vinson, Science, 26 September 2024](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adt2983)).

### The "break glass" measures

In 2024 a group of researchers including Chhandak Bagchi, a graduate student, and Przemek Grabowicz, the senior author, who did the work at the University of Massachusetts Amherst, with Filippo Menczer among the co-authors, raised a different objection in an eLetter to Science. Their university's account states that "Beginning around the start of November 2020, Meta introduced 63 'break glass' changes to Facebook's news feed which were expressly designed to diminish the visibility of untrustworthy news", that the changes cut views of misinformation "by at least 24%", that they reverted in March 2021, and that the chronological-feed experiment ran from 24 September to 23 December 2020, substantially overlapping that window ([University College Dublin, 27 September 2024](https://www.ucd.ie/newsandopinion/news/2024/september/27/neweletterinsciencedebunksmeta-fundedstudysuggestingitsnews-feedalgorithmsarenotmajordriversofmisinformation/)). <Claim status="attributed" by="by the eLetter's authors">"Their paper gives the impression that the standard Facebook algorithm is good at stopping misinformation, which is questionable"</Claim>, Grabowicz said; the authors also warned that "social media companies could conceivably change their algorithms to improve their public image if they know they are being studied".

Thorp and the executive editor, Valda Vinson, posted the letter and the authors' reply as eLetters and wrote an editorial placed at the top of the paper, because "the way the paper was written did not require a correction to anything in the paper"; he disagreed with a university press release that called the letter a debunking: "If the paper were debunked, we would correct it" ([Thorp, 27 September 2024](https://holdenthorp.substack.com/p/more-doubts-for-facebook-on-polarization)). Meta's answer, as he reports it, was that "it was known publicly before the papers were published that measures had been implemented", and that the company "stood behind Nick Clegg's original statements"; Wagner told him he "did not recall discussions about the emergency measures" when the authors and Facebook met.

### Testing without the platform

Every study above depended on Meta's cooperation. A 2025 study set out to remove that dependence. Piccardi, Saveski, Jia, Hancock, Tsai and Bernstein, of Stanford University and two other universities, noted that "Today, social media platforms hold the sole power to study the effects of feed-ranking algorithms", built a browser-based method that reranked participants' X feeds in real time, and ran a ten-day experiment with 1,256 participants during the 2024 campaign, using a language model to move posts expressing antidemocratic attitudes and partisan animosity (AAPA) up or down ([Piccardi et al., Reranking partisan animosity in algorithmic social media feeds alters affective polarization, Science, 27 November 2025, PubMed abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=41308156&rettype=abstract&retmode=text)). <Claim status="reported" by="by its authors">"Decreasing or increasing AAPA exposure shifted out-party partisan animosity by more than 2 points on a 100-point feeling thermometer, with no detectable differences across party lines"</Claim>, which the authors present as "causal evidence that exposure to AAPA content alters affective polarization". Their manipulation targeted a specific kind of content, not the like-minded sources of the Meta experiments, so the two sets of results answer different questions.

## Beyond one platform: what people's media diets show

An echo chamber, on the Reuters Institute's definition, is about a person's whole media space, so several studies look beyond any single feed.

- **Online versus offline.** Matthew Gentzkow and Jesse Shapiro found "that ideological segregation of online news consumption is low in absolute terms, higher than the segregation of most offline news consumption, and significantly lower than the segregation of face-to-face interactions with neighbors, co-workers, or family members", and "no evidence that the Internet is becoming more segregated over time" ([Gentzkow and Shapiro, Ideological Segregation Online and Offline, NBER Working Paper 15916, April 2010, published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics, November 2011](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15916); [The Quarterly Journal of Economics 126, 2011](https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/echo_chambers.pdf)).
- **Browsing histories.** Seth Flaxman, Sharad Goel and Justin Rao, with the web-browsing histories of 50,000 US users, found that social networks and search engines "are associated with an increase in the mean ideological distance between individuals" and, "somewhat counterintuitively", with "an increase in an individual's exposure to material from his or her less preferred side of the political spectrum"; most online news reading was people "simply visiting the home pages of their favorite, typically mainstream, news outlets", and "the magnitude of the effects is relatively modest" ([Flaxman, Goel and Rao, Filter Bubbles, Echo Chambers, and Online News Consumption, Public Opinion Quarterly 80, special issue, published online 22 March 2016](https://5harad.com/papers/bubbles.pdf)).
- **A national survey.** Elizabeth Dubois and Grant Blank surveyed 2,000 adult internet users in the United Kingdom (UK) in January 2017 and found "that those who are interested in politics and those with diverse media diets tend to avoid echo chambers"; in their data, 148 respondents, "about 8%", combined low media diversity with no interest in politics, the group most at risk ([Dubois and Blank, The echo chamber is overstated, Information, Communication & Society, 2018, accepted manuscript](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A34c4d33f-7cee-4fc0-aaad-0ce5311ac0de/files/m988c6e898fd3b881e39fb291545f004e)). The manuscript states that "This work was supported by Google as part of the Quello Search Project".
- **A review.** The Reuters Institute's literature review estimated that "between six and eight percent of the public inhabit politically partisan online news echo chambers" in the UK, found that "algorithmic selection generally leads to slightly more diverse news use", and concluded that <Claim status="disputed" by="by researchers who report segregation at the margins">"echo chambers are much less widespread than is commonly assumed", with "no support for the filter bubble hypothesis"</Claim>, while noting "limited empirical work done outside the United States" ([Arguedas et al., 19 January 2022](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/echo-chambers-filter-bubbles-and-polarisation-literature-review)).
- **Interaction networks.** Matteo Cinelli and colleagues compared more than 100 million pieces of content on Gab, Facebook, Reddit and Twitter about controversial topics such as gun control, vaccination and abortion, and found that "the aggregation in homophilic clusters of users dominates online dynamics", that platforms "organized around social networks and news feed algorithms, such as Facebook and Twitter, favor the emergence of echo chambers", and that news consumption showed "higher segregation on Facebook" than on Reddit ([Cinelli et al., The echo chamber effect on social media, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 118(9), 23 February 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7936330/)).

The studies measure different things. Diet studies look at all the sources a person uses and mostly find diversity; network studies look at who interacts with whom on a platform and often find clustering. Both can be true at once.

## Moral panic, or a problem in the tails?

### The critique of the concepts

Bruns argues that, for all the research they prompted, there is "a pronounced absence of evidence that genuine filter bubbles or echo chambers are real, outside of highly specific and unusual contexts", and that "This disconnect between the public understanding of and the scientific evidence on these concepts has all the hallmarks of a moral panic" ([Bruns, 29 November 2019](https://policyreview.info/concepts/filter-bubble)). He notes that both terms were coined by authors outside media research, Pariser an activist and entrepreneur and Cass Sunstein a legal scholar, and that hyperpartisans often monitor mainstream media, citing Gentzkow and Shapiro's finding on readers of extremist sites. The more important filter, he concludes, is not in the algorithm but "in our heads": the stances people take towards information they do see. Budak, Nyhan, Rothschild, Thorson and Watts list three "common misperceptions": "that average exposure to problematic content is high, that algorithms are largely responsible for this exposure and that social media is a primary cause of broader social problems such as polarization" ([Budak et al., Misunderstanding the harms of online misinformation, Nature, 5 June 2024, abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:%2210.1038/s41586-024-07417-w%22&format=json&resultType=core)).

### The case that segregation still matters

The same body of work contains the strongest reply. The averages hide the edges: Nyhan and colleagues stress that the minority in echo chambers "is large in absolute terms" on a platform of Facebook's size; González-Bailón and colleagues found segregation rising with each step from what could be seen to what was engaged with, and a conservative corner where most flagged misinformation sat; Cinelli and colleagues found clustering dominant on feed-driven platforms; and Piccardi and colleagues found that changing a specific kind of content in a feed moved animosity. Budak and colleagues themselves recommend "holding platforms accountable for facilitating exposure to false and extreme content in the tails of the distribution, where consumption is highest and the risk of real-world harm is greatest", and call for "increased platform transparency, including collaborations with outside researchers", "especially important outside the USA and Western Europe, where research and data are scant and harms may be more severe" ([Budak et al., 5 June 2024](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:%2210.1038/s41586-024-07417-w%22&format=json&resultType=core)). <Claim status="disputed">Whether echo chambers and filter bubbles are a widespread condition or a concentrated one</Claim> is, on the evidence read here, the live form of the dispute.

## Brazil: groups, channels and what can be seen

Most of the studies above are from the US and the UK. The Brazilian research uses different platforms and different methods, and runs into the same limit: the most important channel, WhatsApp, is encrypted.

### Partisan WhatsApp groups

Victor Bursztyn and Larry Birnbaum, of Northwestern University, followed 232 public partisan WhatsApp groups (175 right-wing and 57 left-wing) during the 2018 campaign and collected more than 2.8 million messages from over 45,000 users ([Bursztyn and Birnbaum, Thousands of Small, Constant Rallies: A Large-Scale Analysis of Partisan WhatsApp Groups, 2019, preprint](https://cj2021.northeastern.edu/files/2020/02/CJ_2020_paper_21.pdf)). Their university reported that in the sample right-wing groups "shared 5.5 times as many messages as left-wing users", that 46.5% of right-wing messages were multimedia against 30% of left-wing ones, and that "the most-shared news by WhatsApp groups during the 2018 presidential election campaign came from websites that spread disinformation, as identified by several fact-checking agencies", four such sites on the right and one on the left ([Northwestern Engineering, 28 August 2019](https://mccormick.northwestern.edu/news/articles/2019/08/right-wing-whatsapp-users-in-brazil-are-louder-more-active-more-effective-birnbaum.html)). The groups were chosen because they were partisan, so the study describes activity inside partisan spaces, not how many Brazilians live in them.

Rafael Evangelista and Fernanda Bruno record that the anthropologist Cesarino "discusses the formation of a polarised digital bubble largely anchored on WhatsApp chat groups", and describe groups formed after the election as direct channels for the president's supporters, in which "only a few members are responsible for almost all the content" and "A frequently repeated claim is that one should not believe in the legacy media" ([Evangelista and Bruno, WhatsApp and political instability in Brazil, Internet Policy Review 8(4), 31 December 2019](https://policyreview.info/articles/analysis/whatsapp-and-political-instability-brazil-targeted-messages-and-political)). Their account of the 2018 campaign and the debate it drew are set out in [the part on Brazil's courts and Congress](/learn/brazil-mass-messaging-the-courts-and-the-cpmi).

### Bubbles that leak: 8 January 2023

The groups were not sealed. Joao Ozawa and Josephine Lukito, of the University of Texas at Austin, with two colleagues from Palver, a company that monitors public WhatsApp groups in Brazil, analysed more than 15,000 public groups around 8 January 2023, when, in their words, thousands of Bolsonaro's supporters "ransacked official buildings in the country's capital intending to promote a military coup d'état", and found that supporters of the former president "tried to manufacture consensus in preparation for and after the attack", that "the spread of content on Twitter predicted the spread of content on WhatsApp", with Twitter activity tending to predict WhatsApp activity "between 2–4 days in advance", and that Twitter "also predicted news coverage of Bolsonaro's supporters, suggesting a propaganda feedback loop" ([Ozawa, Lukito, Bailez and Fakhouri, Brazilian Capitol attack, Harvard Kennedy School Misinformation Review, 9 April 2024](https://misinforeview.hks.harvard.edu/?p=12157)). They cite the Reuters Institute's figure that 41% of Brazilians receive news through WhatsApp. On this evidence, content moved from an open platform into closed groups and from there into the press, rather than circulating only inside a bubble.

### YouTube's recommendations

In August 2019 The New York Times reported research by Jonas Kaiser and Yasodara Córdova, of Harvard's Berkman Klein Center, with Adrian Rauchfleisch of National Taiwan University, who "programmed a Brazil-based server to enter a popular channel or search term, then open YouTube's top recommendations, then follow the recommendations on each of those, and so on", thousands of times; they found that "after users watched a video about politics or even entertainment, YouTube's recommendations often favoured right-wing, conspiracy-filled channels" ([The New York Times, Max Fisher and Amanda Taub, How YouTube radicalised Brazil, republished by the New Zealand Herald, 14 August 2019](https://nzherald.co.nz/world/how-youtube-radicalised-brazil/CWHFDFP42724DLKTEYYVBHUE44)). <Claim status="disputed" by="by YouTube">YouTube "challenged the researchers' methodology and said its internal data contradicted their findings"</Claim>, but "declined the Times' requests for that data"; company representatives "said that the platform's systems do not privilege any one viewpoint or direct users toward extremism", conceded some findings, and a spokesman, Farshad Shadloo, said "we've seen that authoritative content is thriving in Brazil and is some of the most recommended content on the site". On health searches, the paper reported, a spokesman "confirmed the Times' findings, calling them unintended", and said YouTube would change how its search surfaced videos about Zika.

During the 2022 election, researchers at NetLab, a laboratory of the Universidade Federal do Rio de Janeiro (UFRJ), ran a different audit: "we performed 205 tests on YouTube's recommendation system, simulating the behavior of new Brazilian users with no historical data on the platform looking for reliable news throughout the elections", and report that YouTube "systematically prioritized Jovem Pan", a conservative media group, and "magnified the imbalance between candidates' portrayals" ([NetLab UFRJ, summary of Santini, Salles and Mattos, Policy & Internet, 22 November 2023](https://netlab.eco.ufrj.br/en/post/recommending-instead-of-taking-down-youtube-hyperpartisan-content-promotion-amid-the-br-elections)). No reply from YouTube or Jovem Pan to this study was found in the sources read for this part. The general research on YouTube's recommendations and its critics belongs to the part on [recommendation and profiling](/learn/recommendation-and-profiling-what-platforms-disclose).

### What Brazilian data cannot show

The Reuters Institute's 2025 report on Brazil records that trust in news, now 42%, stabilized "after trust fell by 20pp between 2015 and 2023, a period characterised by growing political polarisation" ([Reuters Institute, Digital News Report 2025, Brazil, Rodrigo Carro](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2025/brazil)). None of the Brazilian sources read here measures, with individual-level data, how much of any Brazilian's information diet comes from one side, as the US and UK studies do; group studies see only public groups, and YouTube audits simulate new users rather than follow real ones.

## What the evidence supports, and what remains disputed

| Question | What the sources read here support |
|---|---|
| Do most people live in echo chambers? | No, on diet and survey data: a small minority in the UK and other countries studied (Reuters Institute; Dubois and Blank) |
| Do ranking algorithms narrow what people see? | Not on average in most studies: ranking was associated with slightly more diverse news use; Bakshy et al. found choice mattered more, a finding criticized for its sample |
| Is like-minded content common on Facebook? | Yes: half of what the median user saw (Nyhan et al.) |
| Did cutting like-minded content or ranking reduce polarization in 2020? | No measurable effect over three months (Nyhan et al.; Guess et al.), with disputed context for the feed experiment (Bagchi et al.) |
| Can feed content change animosity? | Yes, in a ten-day experiment targeting hostile content on X (Piccardi et al.) |
| Is segregation symmetric? | No, on Facebook news in 2020: a conservative-only corner holding most flagged misinformation (González-Bailón et al.) |
| Is the evidence settled outside the US and Western Europe? | No: research is thin there, by the reviewers' own account (Reuters Institute; Budak et al.) |

## What defenders can do

- **Platforms** hold the data and the switches. The Meta studies show what cooperation can produce and what it constrains ("independence by permission"); the eLetter shows why disclosing ranking changes made during a study matters; Budak and colleagues ask for transparency and outside access, and Piccardi and colleagues show one way to test without it.
- **Researchers** can state which concept they measure, a whole media diet or a single platform's network (Reuters Institute; Bruns), report the tails as well as the median (Nyhan et al.; Budak et al.), and disclose funding and data terms, as Dubois and Blank and the Meta project authors did.
- **Journals** can add context without correcting a paper that is not wrong, as Science did with an editorial and eLetters, and say when they disagree with a funder's reading of the work.
- **Readers** can check their own diet rather than their feed: the studies find that people with diverse sources and an interest in politics are the least likely to be enclosed (Dubois and Blank), and that a chronological feed is not automatically cleaner (Guess et al.).

## What to remember

- An echo chamber is an enclosed media space people may choose; a filter bubble is one produced by personalization without their choice. The terms are often used as synonyms and studies define them differently.
- Facebook's own 2015 study found individual choice limited cross-cutting news more than ranking did; critics said its sample was a small, politically engaged minority.
- In the 2020 Meta studies, half of what the median US user saw came from like-minded sources, but cutting it by a third, switching to a chronological feed or removing reshares produced no measurable change in polarization over three months.
- Meta said this showed little evidence its platforms cause harmful polarization; Science's editor-in-chief said the journal disputed that as overstating the evidence, and outside researchers pointed to 63 emergency ranking changes in force during the feed experiment.
- A 2025 experiment that reranked hostile posts on X without the platform's help shifted partisan animosity by more than 2 points on a 100-point scale.
- Diet studies find most people's news use diverse, with a few per cent in partisan echo chambers; network studies find strong clustering; critics call the concepts a moral panic, others point to the large absolute numbers and the asymmetry at the edges.
- In Brazil, partisan WhatsApp groups were dominated by right-wing activity in 2018, content moved from Twitter into WhatsApp groups and the press around 8 January 2023, and audits of YouTube found recommendations favouring right-wing channels, which YouTube disputed.

<ReaderQuestions>

- If only a small share of people live in echo chambers but that share is millions of people and holds most of the flagged misinformation, is the problem small or large, and who decides which number counts?
- When a company supplies the data, the switches and some of the authors, what would make a study's independence enough: preregistration, an observer, open data, or something else?
- Should a platform be required to disclose changes it makes to its ranking while outside researchers are studying it?
- If people choose like-minded sources more than algorithms impose them, does responsibility for enclosure lie with the people, the platforms that make choosing easy, or neither?
- With encrypted messaging at the centre of Brazilian politics and only public groups visible to researchers, what kind of evidence about Brazilian echo chambers could ever be convincing, and at what cost to privacy?

</ReaderQuestions>
