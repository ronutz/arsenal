A terminal that showed dealers' prices for unlisted American shares went live in February 1971; Toronto launched a computer-assisted trading system in 1977; in June 1992 the Chicago Mercantile Exchange (CME) opened an overnight electronic market for currency futures; and in 2013 Brazil's equities moved to a matching engine built with CME. This part of the series opened by [Electronic trading: from the pit to the microsecond](/learn/electronic-trading-from-the-pit-to-the-microsecond) follows the screens that replaced the floors described in [Open outcry: trading floors and the pregão](/learn/open-outcry-trading-floors-and-the-pregao); the next part, [The rules that rewired markets](/learn/the-rules-that-rewired-markets-reg-ats-reg-nms-mifid-and-brazil), covers the regulation that shaped them. After reading, you can tell a quotation screen from a matching engine, work through an order book under price-time priority, date the main systems from their operators' own records and explain where the Brazilian platforms came from.

## Three kinds of screen

The word "electronic" covered three different machines, and the history makes sense only once they are kept apart.

**Quotation networks.** The first kind showed prices and left the deal to the telephone. Nasdaq describes its own beginning as "the world's first fully electronic quotation system" ([Nasdaq, 8 February 2021](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2021/02/08/2171228/0/en/Nasdaq-Celebrates-50-Years-of-Innovation.html)). When London's floor emptied in 1986, the Bank of England recorded that business moved to dealing rooms "where transactions are made by telephone on the basis of information carried on screens" ([Bank of England, Quarterly Bulletin, February 1987](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1987/change-in-the-stock-exchange-and-regulation-of-the-city.pdf)).

**Hit-and-take systems.** The second kind displayed firm orders that a trader could select and accept by hand. Deutsche Börse describes IBIS, introduced by the Frankfurt Stock Exchange in April 1991, as "a hit-and-take market. Orders are not automatically executed against each other; instead, the traders select orders available in the system" ([Deutsche Börse, company history](https://deutsche-boerse.com/dbg-en/about-us/deutsche-boerse-group/company-history)).

**Matching engines.** The third kind keeps a central book of orders and executes them automatically by fixed rules. The United States Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) described one such engine at the Bolsa de Mercadorias e Futuros (BM&F), Brazil's futures exchange, in 2008: orders reach a "Trading Engine" in São Paulo and are "matched by price/time priority" ([CFTC Letter 08-18, 26 September 2008](https://cftc.gov/csl/08-18/download)). In the European Union's rules on business clocks, the matching engine is the stage between an order's arrival at a venue's outer gateway and the acknowledgement sent back ([Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2017/574, 7 June 2016](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32017R0574)).

Every system in this part sits somewhere on that line, and several moved along it: Nasdaq began as a quotation network, added execution systems from 1984 and bought the INET matching platform in 2005, and Frankfurt replaced IBIS with the fully electronic Xetra in November 1997.

## Inside a matching engine

A matching engine holds a **central limit order book**: every resting order to buy (a bid) or sell (an offer) at a stated limit price, sorted so that the best price comes first. When a new order arrives that can trade against the book, the engine decides which resting orders it fills, in what quantity, and at what price.

### Price first, then time

The most common rule is price-time priority. The CFTC's description of BM&F's system in 2008 is a clear statement of it: "Lower offers take precedence over higher offers and higher bids take precedence over lower bids. In the case of multiple bids or offers at the same price, earlier bids and offers take precedence over later bids and offers respectively" ([CFTC Letter 08-18, 26 September 2008](https://cftc.gov/csl/08-18/download)). The same letter adds that "The time assigned to an order is the time that it arrives at the Trading Engine", which is why the distance between a trader's computer and the engine came to matter.

Brazil writes the rule into regulation. The Comissão de Valores Mobiliários (CVM), Brazil's securities regulator, requires that in a centralized multilateral exchange system prices form by the interaction of offers, "em que seja dada precedência sempre à oferta que represente o melhor preço, respeitada a ordem cronológica de entrada das ofertas" (precedence always to the offer with the best price, respecting the chronological order in which offers were entered), unless a CVM-approved procedure says otherwise ([Resolução CVM 135, art. 119, § 1º, 10 June 2022, consolidated text](https://conteudo.cvm.gov.br/export/sites/cvm/legislacao/resolucoes/anexos/100/resol135consolid.pdf)).

### Other allocation rules

Price-time is not the only rule. CME's Globex lists several algorithms, among them FIFO (first in, first out), which "uses price and time as the only criteria for filling an order", and pro-rata, which shares an incoming order among resting orders at the best price in proportion to their size, with a minimum allocation and FIFO for leftover lots ([CME Group, Supported Matching Algorithms](https://cmegroupclientsite.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/EPICSANDBOX/pages/457218479/Supported+Matching+Algorithms)). Exchanges also rank visibility: in United States (US) equities, displayed orders generally have priority at a given price over fully undisplayed orders and the hidden part of reserve orders, according to the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) ([SEC, Concept Release on Equity Market Structure, 21 January 2010](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-01-21/html/2010-1045.htm)).

### A worked example

The book below is invented for illustration. It shows the offers (sell orders) resting in a stock at the moment a buy order arrives; the times are when each order reached the engine.

| Offer | Price (R$) | Quantity | Arrival time |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 10.01 | 300 | 10:00:00.000150 |
| B | 10.01 | 500 | 10:00:00.000420 |
| C | 10.02 | 1,000 | 09:59:59.998000 |
| D | 10.03 | 200 | 10:00:00.000010 |

A limit order to buy 1,000 shares at up to R$10.02 arrives. Under price-time priority the engine fills it like this:

| Step | Matched against | Why this one | Quantity | Price (R$) | Still to fill |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A | Best price (10.01), earliest at that price | 300 | 10.01 | 700 |
| 2 | B | Same price, next in time | 500 | 10.01 | 200 |
| 3 | C | Next best price, within the buyer's limit | 200 | 10.02 | 0 |

Offer C arrived earliest of all, but it waits behind A and B because its price is worse; time only breaks ties within a price. Offer D is never reached because R$10.03 is above the buyer's limit. Under a pro-rata rule, steps 1 and 2 would change: the 800 shares available at R$10.01 would be shared between A and B in proportion to their size, so the larger order B would receive more even though A was first.

### Filters before the engine

Orders are checked before they reach the book. The CFTC recorded in 2008 that credit and size limits set by BM&F's trading members filtered direct-access orders "before they reach the Trading Engine", and that a limit set to zero systemically kept a participant's orders out of the matching engine ([CFTC Letter 08-18, 26 September 2008](https://cftc.gov/csl/08-18/download)). When Brazil's exchange moved its equities to a new platform in 2013, it said it offered "seven market protection mechanisms", six price fluctuation tunnels and one volume tunnel, together with pre-trade risk control ([CME Group, 13 May 2013](https://investor.cmegroup.com/news-releases/news-release-details/puma-trading-system-bmfbovespa-celebrates-month-bovespa-segment)). Pre-trade risk controls and market access rules have a later part of their own.

### How fast the engines became

The response times operators published show the compression. When Globex launched in 1992, an announcement said response times for trades would be within three seconds; CME cut the average time for a message to go in and out of its match engine from 1.2 seconds at the start of 2001 to 0.45 seconds at the end of that year and 0.16 seconds by 31 March 2002 ([CME, 25 June 2002](https://mondovisione.com/news/cme-celebrates-10th-anniversary-of-globexsup-sup-electronic-trading-on-heels-of--2011124)). In 2010 the SEC cited published averages of 320 microseconds at the BATS exchange and 294 microseconds at Nasdaq, and wrote that some exchanges' average response times were below 1 millisecond ([SEC, 21 January 2010](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-01-21/html/2010-1045.htm)). B3 (Brasil, Bolsa, Balcão), Brazil's exchange, said in April 2025 that the time between an order's submission and its execution had fallen from 1.2 milliseconds in 2023 to about 350 microseconds, helped by an international binary protocol, and that its target was below 300 microseconds by 2026 ([B3, 4 April 2025](https://www.b3.com.br/pt_br/noticias/avanco-em-tecnologia.htm)).

## Nasdaq, 1971: a network of quotes

The over-the-counter market in American shares had no floor. The SEC Historical Society describes it as "an invisible network of broker-dealers linked by the telephone and the pink sheets", printed price lists that were always stale; the SEC recommended a computerized, real-time substitute, and the National Association of Securities Dealers (NASD) built a processing centre in Trumbull, Connecticut ([SEC Historical Society, Nasdaq](https://sechistorical.org/exhibition/transformation-regulation-equities-market-structure-1934-to-2018-2/automating-the-exchanges-1976-1995/nasdaq/)).

The NASD Automated Quotations system (NASDAQ) went live in February 1971, linking about 1,000 market makers and listing some 3,000 securities, with no retail interface and no stocks listed on the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE), according to the same exhibition ([SEC Historical Society, Nasdaq](https://sechistorical.org/exhibition/transformation-regulation-equities-market-structure-1934-to-2018-2/automating-the-exchanges-1976-1995/nasdaq/)). It offered three service levels: Level I showed representative bids, Level II the dealers' wholesale quotes, and Level III allowed users to place limit orders. Nasdaq itself calls the 1971 system "the world's first fully electronic quotation system" ([Nasdaq, 8 February 2021](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2021/02/08/2171228/0/en/Nasdaq-Celebrates-50-Years-of-Innovation.html)).

Execution came later. The exhibition records that Nasdaq was handling 63 percent of the NYSE's volume by 1980, that its Small Order Execution System (SOES) began electronic execution of small orders in 1984, that market makers pulled out of the then voluntary SOES when prices plunged in October 1987 and its use became mandatory for all of them in 1988, and that SelectNet followed in 1990 ([SEC Historical Society, Nasdaq](https://sechistorical.org/exhibition/transformation-regulation-equities-market-structure-1934-to-2018-2/automating-the-exchanges-1976-1995/nasdaq/)). In 2005 Nasdaq bought the INET electronic communication network and its matching platform, as described below.

## Instinet, 1969: institutions trade without a floor

Instinet started before Nasdaq. The SEC Historical Society writes that "from its founding in 1969", the Institutional Networks Corporation system let banks, insurers and mutual funds trade shares with each other anonymously and without brokers, but that it was slow to take off because "in the 1970s people still wanted to talk to a broker" ([SEC Historical Society, Instinet](https://sechistorical.org/exhibition/transformation-regulation-equities-market-structure-1934-to-2018-2/automating-the-exchanges-1976-1995/instinet/)). Instinet's own timeline calls its 1969 system "the market's first ECN", an electronic communication network ([Instinet, history](https://instinet.com/history)).

Two regulatory choices let it grow. In a 1986 no-action letter the SEC agreed to regulate Instinet as a broker-dealer rather than as an exchange, which the exhibition credits with letting it spend on innovation rather than self-regulation ([SEC Historical Society, Increments and ECNs](https://sechistorical.org/exhibition/transformation-regulation-equities-market-structure-1934-to-2018-2/rulemaking-and-linkages-1975-2005/increments-and-ecns/)). During the October 1987 crash Instinet kept functioning while market makers and specialists failed to provide liquidity, and by 1990 it was doing about 13 percent of the NYSE's volume ([SEC Historical Society, Instinet](https://sechistorical.org/exhibition/transformation-regulation-equities-market-structure-1934-to-2018-2/automating-the-exchanges-1976-1995/instinet/)). By January 1996, the SEC recorded, Instinet alone carried about 15 percent of total Nasdaq volume ([SEC, Order Execution Obligations, 12 September 1996](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1996-09-12/html/96-23210.htm)).

Its ownership changed several times. In December 2005 Nasdaq completed its acquisition of Instinet Group, kept the INET trading platform and sold Instinet's institutional brokerage to Silver Lake Partners ([Nasdaq, 8 December 2005](https://mondovisione.com/news/nasdaq-completes-acquisition-of-inet-2011124)); on 2 February 2007 Nomura Holdings announced that it had closed its acquisition of Instinet for US$1.2 billion ([Nomura, February 2007](https://mondovisione.com/news/nomura-closes-instinet-acquisition-announces-new-board-of-directors-2011124)). Instinet's timeline describes it since 2012 as the global execution arm of Nomura ([Instinet, history](https://instinet.com/history)).

## Toronto, 1977: CATS

Toronto claims a first of its own. TMX Group's history records that in 1977 the Toronto Stock Exchange "launched the world's first Computer Assisted Trading System (CATS)", and that in 1997 it became "the largest stock exchange in North America to choose a floorless, electronic (or virtual trading) environment when its trading floor closed" ([TMX Group, History at a Glance](https://www.courthouselibrary.ca/sites/default/files/inline-files/TMX_GroupHistoryAtAGlance.pdf)). The same timeline places Toronto's move to decimal prices in 1996, five years before the American markets.

CATS travelled. B3's regulatory filing records that early in the 1990s the Bolsa de Valores de São Paulo (Bovespa) introduced CATS, "desenvolvido pela bolsa de Toronto", in parallel with its open-outcry floor ([B3, Formulário de Referência 2024, item 1.1](https://api.mziq.com/mzfilemanager/v2/d/5fd7b7d8-54a1-472d-8426-eb896ad8a3c4/8d8a1c1b-991e-904f-e699-a9cb7cc6d9e5?origin=1)). The Brazilian story continues below.

## London, 1986: Big Bang and SEAQ

The London Stock Exchange's reforms took effect on 27 October 1986, "Big Bang" day. The Bank of England summarized them as the end of the compulsory separation between brokers and jobbers ("single capacity"), the move from fixed to negotiable commissions, a new dual-capacity system in which market makers could deal both as principals and as agents, and a price dissemination system, SEAQ (Stock Exchange Automated Quotations) ([Bank of England, Quarterly Bulletin, February 1987](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1987/change-in-the-stock-exchange-and-regulation-of-the-city.pdf)). The operator, the London Stock Exchange Group (LSEG), marks 1986 in its own history as the year the British market was deregulated in the Big Bang ([LSEG, The history of LSEG](https://www.lseg.com/en/about-us/history)).

SEAQ was a quotation screen in the Nasdaq mould, and the Bank said so: the experience of NASDAQ, "which SEAQ resembles", had pointed the way. What surprised the Bank was the speed: "the speed with which the floor has declined in importance was unexpected." Within the first few weeks, as much as three quarters of exchange business other than traded options was estimated to be done off the floor, and by December perhaps 95%, as market makers moved their resources to dealing rooms ([Bank of England, Quarterly Bulletin, February 1987](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/1987/change-in-the-stock-exchange-and-regulation-of-the-city.pdf)). Ahead of Big Bang, twenty-eight firms planning to make markets had decided to keep a presence on the floor, against nineteen jobbers there before; by December most market makers had decided to divert resources to their dealing rooms.

## Switzerland and Germany: SOFFEX, DTB and Eurex

The new Swiss and German derivatives exchanges were built electronic from the start. SIX, the Swiss exchange operator, records that the Swiss Market Index was created in 1988 "in the context of the SOFFEX derivatives exchange, known today as EUREX", and that the first options on the index became tradable on SOFFEX on 7 December 1988 ([SIX, 30 years of the SMI](https://six-group.com/en/site/smi-30years/time-journey.html)). In 2004 a member of the Eurex executive board, Daniel Gisler, told swissinfo that "The birth of our system goes back to 1987 when SOFFEX [Swiss Options and Financial Futures Exchange] was founded and was basically the first electronic exchange and integrated clearing house" ([swissinfo, 6 February 2004](https://swissinfo.ch/eng/banking-fintech/swiss-technology-takes-on-chicago-s-pit-bulls/3755884)); that is the company's own claim.

In Germany, the Deutsche Terminbörse (DTB) opened on 26 January 1990, and futures were traded on a German exchange for the first time ([Deutsche Börse, company history](https://deutsche-boerse.com/dbg-en/about-us/deutsche-boerse-group/company-history)). In 1996 DTB received the CFTC's first no-action letter allowing a foreign board of trade to install trading screens in the United States without registering there ([Eurex, application for registration as a foreign board of trade, Exhibit A-1(2)](https://www.cftc.gov/ucm/groups/public/@otherif/documents/ifdocs/eub18formfbotexhibita-12-eur.pdf)). In 1998 Deutsche Börse and the Swiss exchange merged DTB and SOFFEX into Eurex, which Deutsche Börse calls "the world's first cross-border futures exchange"; Deutsche Börse became Eurex's sole owner in 2012 ([Deutsche Börse, company history](https://deutsche-boerse.com/dbg-en/about-us/deutsche-boerse-group/company-history)).

Cash equities followed. Frankfurt's Xetra replaced IBIS on 28 November 1997 with an open order book while floor trading continued ([Deutsche Börse, company history](https://deutsche-boerse.com/dbg-en/about-us/deutsche-boerse-group/company-history)), and SIX Swiss Exchange moved to fully electronic trading from 8 December 1995, which it describes as the first switch worldwide "from floor trading to electronic trading with fully integrated transaction processing and settlement" ([SIX, 8 December 2015](https://www.six-group.com/dam/download/media/press-releases/2015/1208-e-20_years_electronic_trading.pdf)).

## Chicago, 1992: Globex

CME designed Globex to trade while its pits were closed. Its 2012 retrospective dates the decision to 1987 ("Globex concept approved for after-hours trading") and notes that "It took until 1992 when the technologies could actually become operational" ([CME Group, Globex retrospective, June 2012](https://www.cmegroup.com/content/dam/cmegroup/education/files/globex-retrospective-2012-06-12.pdf)).

CME's own account of the launch, issued for the tenth anniversary, gives the details ([CME, 25 June 2002](https://mondovisione.com/news/cme-celebrates-10th-anniversary-of-globexsup-sup-electronic-trading-on-heels-of--2011124)):

- Globex began trading at 6 p.m. Chicago time on 25 June 1992, and overnight volume for CME products on the first day totalled 1,893 Deutsche mark and Japanese yen futures and options on futures.
- It "was originally based on Reuters technology, and terminals were hard-wired to Reuters' global network anchored to a mainframe computer in Hauppauge, New York."
- In 1998 CME implemented a second generation using a modified version of the NSC trading system developed by the Paris Bourse for MATIF, the Paris futures market (now part of Euronext).
- In September 1997 CME launched E-mini S&P 500 futures, one fifth the size of the pit-traded contract; it was the first time CME offered electronic trading during the pit's regular hours, nearly 23 hours a day, five days a week.

The screen overtook the pit within twelve years. CME's retrospective records 2004 as the year "CME Globex volume exceeds pit volume for first time", and calls the open-access policy implemented in late 2000 "the most important milestone in the history of the CME Globex system" ([CME Group, June 2012](https://www.cmegroup.com/content/dam/cmegroup/education/files/globex-retrospective-2012-06-12.pdf)). Its chart of Globex's share of CME Group volume rises from about zero in 1992 to 84% in 2011.

## The ECN years: Island, Archipelago and the exchanges that bought them

In the United States the push came from regulation. In 1996 the SEC required market makers to display customer limit orders that improved their quotes and to make public any better prices they quoted privately on certain electronic communications networks (ECNs) ([SEC, Order Execution Obligations, 12 September 1996](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1996-09-12/html/96-23210.htm)). The SEC Historical Society records that these Order Handling Rules spurred new ECNs, Island, BRUT, Archipelago and REDIBook, which competed by innovating ([SEC Historical Society, Increments and ECNs](https://sechistorical.org/exhibition/transformation-regulation-equities-market-structure-1934-to-2018-2/rulemaking-and-linkages-1975-2005/increments-and-ecns/)).

The numbers grew fast. By 1999, according to the exhibition, ECNs did about a third of Nasdaq's volume, and Island, "with only 19 employees and no market makers (trades were conducted by algorithms)", did 10 percent ([SEC Historical Society, Increments and ECNs](https://sechistorical.org/exhibition/transformation-regulation-equities-market-structure-1934-to-2018-2/rulemaking-and-linkages-1975-2005/increments-and-ecns/)). The SEC wrote in December 1998 that alternative trading systems handled "more than twenty percent of the orders in securities listed on The Nasdaq Stock Market" and almost four percent in exchange-listed securities ([SEC, Regulation of Exchanges and Alternative Trading Systems, 22 December 1998](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1998-12-22/pdf/98-33299.pdf)). Island is also credited with a pricing model that had spread industry-wide by the mid-2000s: in 1997 it began paying a rebate to the "maker" who posted a resting order and charging a fee to the "taker" who traded against it ([SEC Historical Society, Electric light](https://sechistorical.org/exhibition/transformation-regulation-equities-market-structure-1934-to-2018-2/emergence-of-the-national-market-2003-2018/electric-light/)).

Consolidation followed within a decade:

- On 20 September 2002 Instinet Group and Island ECN completed their merger; Island described itself as handling about one in five trades in Nasdaq securities ([Instinet and Island, 20 September 2002](https://mondovisione.com/news/instinet-and-island-ecn-close-historic-merger-2011124)).
- On 8 December 2005 Nasdaq completed its purchase of the resulting INET platform and said it expected to move to INET's technology within twelve months ([Nasdaq, 8 December 2005](https://mondovisione.com/news/nasdaq-completes-acquisition-of-inet-2011124)).
- On 7 March 2006 the NYSE and Archipelago Holdings completed their merger; the new NYSE Group's shares began trading the next day, and its market NYSE Arca was formerly known as the Archipelago Exchange (ArcaEx) and the Pacific Exchange ([NYSE Group, 7 March 2006](https://mondovisione.com/news/new-york-stock-exchange-archipelago-holdings-merger-complete-merger-agreement-si-2011124); [NYSE Group, 8 March 2006](https://mondovisione.com/media-and-resources/news/stock-of-new-public-nyse-group-inc-begins-trading-inyse-group-is-well-positioned-2011124/)).

The floor-based NYSE changed with it. The SEC wrote in 2010 that NYSE-listed stocks "were traded primarily on the floor of the NYSE in a manual fashion until October 2006", when the NYSE began to offer fully automated access to its displayed quotations ([SEC, 21 January 2010](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-01-21/html/2010-1045.htm)). Why that change came when it did, and what it did to the NYSE's market share, belongs to the next part.

## Brazil: from CATS to PUMA

### Bovespa

B3's regulatory filing, the Formulário de Referência it files with the CVM, sets out the predecessor exchanges' path ([B3, Formulário de Referência 2024, item 1.1](https://api.mziq.com/mzfilemanager/v2/d/5fd7b7d8-54a1-472d-8426-eb896ad8a3c4/8d8a1c1b-991e-904f-e699-a9cb7cc6d9e5?origin=1)):

- In the early 1970s the recording of trades on Bovespa's floor was automated, and quotes began to be disseminated electronically.
- Early in the 1990s Bovespa introduced CATS, developed by the Toronto exchange, in parallel with the open-outcry floor; in the middle of the same decade it was replaced "por um sistema adquirido da então bolsa de Paris" (by a system acquired from the then Paris exchange).
- In 2000 Bovespa led the integration of the eight other Brazilian stock exchanges, concentrating equity trading in a single exchange.
- On 30 September 2005 Bovespa closed its open-outcry floor and became a fully electronic market.

The system bought from Paris was Mega Bolsa: a CME Group announcement of 2013 calls it "the old equities trading platform used since 1997" ([CME Group, 13 May 2013](https://investor.cmegroup.com/news-releases/news-release-details/puma-trading-system-bmfbovespa-celebrates-month-bovespa-segment)). The screen and the floor coexisted for years. André Demarco, who worked as a post controller on the open-outcry floor in 1994, recalled in 2024 that in July 1994 floor traders dealt in person in the shares of about ten companies, which made up 95% of trading, while "As ações com baixa liquidez já eram negociadas por meio de sistemas eletrônicos chamados CATS e WINCATS" (low-liquidity shares were already traded through electronic systems called CATS and WINCATS) ([B3, 5 July 2024](https://www.b3.com.br/pt_br/noticias/como-era-a-bolsa-de-valores-em-1994.htm)). On the first day of the Real, 1 July 1994, Bovespa recorded 4,204 trades; on 1 July 2024 B3 recorded 4,445,811 in its equities market.

B3 marked the twentieth anniversary of the floor's closing in September 2025. It wrote that the open-outcry floor, "Em uma história iniciada em 1895", closed on 30 September 2005, that floor trading meant 10,000 to 15,000 trades a day, and that the average had since reached 8.4 million a day ([B3 Bora Investir, 30 September 2025](https://borainvestir.b3.com.br/noticias/fim-do-pregao-viva-voz-faz-20-anos-e-b3-celebra-historia-com-exposicao/)).

### BM&F

BM&F was incorporated in July 1985 and began operating in January 1986, according to the CFTC's 2008 review ([CFTC Letter 08-18, 26 September 2008](https://cftc.gov/csl/08-18/download)). The same review records that "BM&F's original electronic trading system, launched in 2000, was based on the ATOS/Euronext trading platform", and that a new, internally developed system, also referred to as GTS, "became operational in late 2007"; the exchange called it the Global Trading System ([BM&FBOVESPA, 21 May 2009](https://mondovisione.com/media-and-resources/news/bmandfbovespa-authorizes-derivative-trading-via-colocation-new-direct-market-acc-2011124/)). Users reached it through a Financial Information Exchange (FIX) protocol interface called BELL, "BM&F Electronic Link", over the brokers' Financial Community Communication Network (RCCF), leased lines, the web or, under an order-routing agreement with CME, from Globex terminals.

The switch was gradual. BM&FBOVESPA (Bolsa de Valores, Mercadorias e Futuros), the exchange formed by the 2008 merger described below, said in June 2009 that electronic trading accounted for just over 3% of contracts in the BM&F segment in 2000, overtook the floor in 2006 with 57.59%, reached 72.95% in 2007 once products could trade on the screen and the floor side by side, and accounted for over 98% of the 22 million contracts traded in June 2009 ([BM&FBOVESPA, 30 June 2009](https://mondovisione.com/news/trading-in-the-bmandf-segment-at-bmandfbovespa-to-become-fully-electronic-as-of--2011124)). The last open-outcry session closed that day, and trading became exclusively electronic on 1 July 2009; B3's filing records the end of open outcry at BM&F on 30 June 2009 ([B3, Formulário de Referência 2024, item 1.1](https://api.mziq.com/mzfilemanager/v2/d/5fd7b7d8-54a1-472d-8426-eb896ad8a3c4/8d8a1c1b-991e-904f-e699-a9cb7cc6d9e5?origin=1)). A B3 news item of April 2025 puts the end of BM&F's open outcry "em meados de 2006" ([B3, 4 April 2025](https://www.b3.com.br/pt_br/noticias/avanco-em-tecnologia.htm)); 2006 is the year in which, by the exchange's 2009 figures, the screen overtook the floor.

Access widened in the same months. BM&FBOVESPA announced in May 2009 that from 15 June it would offer direct market access by co-location (colocation), letting brokers and investors install their own servers in the exchange's data centre, first for the derivatives segment on GTS and later for equities on Mega Bolsa, subject to CVM approval ([BM&FBOVESPA, 21 May 2009](https://mondovisione.com/media-and-resources/news/bmandfbovespa-authorizes-derivative-trading-via-colocation-new-direct-market-acc-2011124/)).

### PUMA, built with CME

The merger of BM&F and Bovespa Holding into BM&FBOVESPA took effect on 8 May 2008 ([B3, Formulário de Referência 2024, item 1.1](https://api.mziq.com/mzfilemanager/v2/d/5fd7b7d8-54a1-472d-8426-eb896ad8a3c4/8d8a1c1b-991e-904f-e699-a9cb7cc6d9e5?origin=1)). CME was already close: B3's filing records an October 2007 partnership between BM&F and CME Group involving cross-shareholdings and an electronic link for routing orders ([B3, Formulário de Referência 2024, item 1.1](https://api.mziq.com/mzfilemanager/v2/d/5fd7b7d8-54a1-472d-8426-eb896ad8a3c4/8d8a1c1b-991e-904f-e699-a9cb7cc6d9e5?origin=1)), and the CFTC noted that BM&F shareholders approved on 26 February 2008 an agreement that made CME Group BM&F's largest shareholder, with 10% ([CFTC Letter 08-18, 26 September 2008](https://cftc.gov/csl/08-18/download)).

On 12 February 2010 the two exchanges announced that they would jointly develop a multi-asset trading platform for BM&FBOVESPA's equities and derivatives, which BM&FBOVESPA's chief executive, Edemir Pinto, said would be "based on technology derived from the CME Globex trading system, as well as on new technology to be jointly created by the parties"; that BM&FBOVESPA would raise its stake in CME Group to 5%, and that the platform was expected in early 2011 ([CME Group, 12 February 2010](https://www.cmegroup.com/media-room/press-releases/2010/2/12/cme_group_and_bmfbovespatobecomeglobalpreferredstrategicpartners.html)). B3's filing records that the definitive contracts, for an initial 15 years, were signed on 22 June 2010 and that the stake rose from 1.8% to 5% in July 2010 ([B3, Formulário de Referência 2024, item 1.1](https://api.mziq.com/mzfilemanager/v2/d/5fd7b7d8-54a1-472d-8426-eb896ad8a3c4/8d8a1c1b-991e-904f-e699-a9cb7cc6d9e5?origin=1)).

The new platform was named the PUMA Trading System and replaced four systems in stages: GTS, Mega Bolsa, BOVESPA FIX for corporate bonds and SISBEX for government securities; its first stage went live in spot foreign exchange on 29 August 2011 ([BM&FBOVESPA, 25 August 2011](https://mondovisione.com/news/launch-of-the-puma-trading-system-new-trading-platform-on-august-29-201-2011825)). B3's filing dates PUMA's entry into the BM&F segment to the second half of 2011, the Bovespa segment to the first half of 2013 and corporate fixed income to July 2014, and records that its infrastructure was entirely renewed in 2017 ([B3, Formulário de Referência 2024, item 1.4](https://api.mziq.com/mzfilemanager/v2/d/5fd7b7d8-54a1-472d-8426-eb896ad8a3c4/8d8a1c1b-991e-904f-e699-a9cb7cc6d9e5?origin=1)).

The equities migration came with numbers. In May 2013 BM&FBOVESPA said that Mega Bolsa's peak had been 284,348 messages and 10,350 trades per minute on 14 September 2012, that PUMA handled 385,374 messages and 16,550 trades per minute on 12 April 2013 and 427,479 messages per minute on 23 April, and that the platform had reduced latency significantly while "increasing order processing time as much as thirtyfold", in the release's words ([CME Group, 13 May 2013](https://investor.cmegroup.com/news-releases/news-release-details/puma-trading-system-bmfbovespa-celebrates-month-bovespa-segment)).

The partnership did not last its fifteen years. B3's filing records that the exchange sold 20% of its CME Group shares in September 2015 and the rest under a material fact notice of 7 April 2016, to fund its combination with Cetip ([B3, Formulário de Referência 2024, item 1.1](https://api.mziq.com/mzfilemanager/v2/d/5fd7b7d8-54a1-472d-8426-eb896ad8a3c4/8d8a1c1b-991e-904f-e699-a9cb7cc6d9e5?origin=1)), and its investor-relations history lists 2016 as the year the CME partnership ended ([B3, Histórico](https://ri.b3.com.br/pt-br/b3/historico/)). Today B3 says that high-frequency traders account for about 40% of trades in the Brazilian equities market and that it offers colocation next to its data centre in São Paulo state ([B3, 4 April 2025](https://www.b3.com.br/pt_br/noticias/avanco-em-tecnologia.htm)); [Brazil: BM&F, Bovespa, B3 and electronic access](/learn/brazil-bmf-bovespa-b3-and-electronic-access) and [Colocation, microwaves and the race for latency](/learn/colocation-microwaves-and-the-race-for-latency) go further.

## What to remember

- "Electronic" meant three machines: quotation networks (Nasdaq in 1971, SEAQ in 1986), hit-and-take screens (IBIS in 1991) and matching engines that execute automatically (CATS in 1977, SOFFEX, DTB, Globex, Xetra, GTS, PUMA).
- Most matching engines use price-time priority: the best price first, and the earliest order at that price first; Brazil writes the rule into Resolução CVM 135, and CME also uses pro-rata allocation for some products.
- London's floor emptied within weeks of Big Bang on 27 October 1986; Globex, launched on 25 June 1992 with 1,893 contracts, overtook CME's pits in 2004.
- In the United States the 1996 Order Handling Rules spurred ECNs such as Island and Archipelago; by 2006 Nasdaq had bought INET and the NYSE had merged with Archipelago.
- Bovespa used Toronto's CATS from the early 1990s and Paris's Mega Bolsa from 1997, and closed its floor on 30 September 2005; BM&F went electronic from 2000 and closed its floor on 30 June 2009; PUMA, built with CME on Globex technology, carried derivatives from 2011, equities from 2013 and fixed income from 2014.
