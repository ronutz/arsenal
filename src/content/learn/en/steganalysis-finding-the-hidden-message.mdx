Steganography wins if nobody can tell that a message exists. Steganalysis is the other side's work: deciding, from the file alone, whether it carries a hidden message, and if possible how long that message is. The previous part, [Hiding in images](/learn/hiding-in-images-lsb-jpeg-and-adaptive-steganography), described the embedding methods from least-significant-bit tools to content-adaptive schemes; this part, in the series opened by [Steganography: hiding that a message exists](/learn/steganography-hiding-that-a-message-exists), follows the detectors that answered each of them. It begins with the attacks of 1999 that anyone can compute by hand, moves through the statistical methods of the early 2000s and the machine-learning detectors of the 2010s, and ends with the two public competitions that measured how detection fares outside the laboratory, and with the tools used by forensic examiners and capture-the-flag players. After reading, you can compute a chi-square attack on a small histogram, sort a pixel group into the categories of RS analysis (named after its regular and singular groups), explain how calibration broke F5, read error rates and base rates critically, and say what the competitions showed.

## What a steganalyst has to decide

Neil Johnson and Sushil Jajodia of George Mason University wrote in 1998 that "discovering and rendering useless such covert messages is a new art form known as steganalysis" ([Johnson and Jajodia, Steganalysis: The Investigation of Hidden Information, IEEE Information Technology Conference 1998](https://www.cse.buffalo.edu/courses/cse725/peter/Johnson_1997.pdf)); Andreas Westfeld and Andreas Pfitzmann recorded that Johnson had given an introduction to steganalysis at the Information Hiding Workshop of 1998 ([Westfeld and Pfitzmann, Attacks on Steganographic Systems, 1999](https://users.ece.cmu.edu/~adrian/487-s06/westfeld-pfitzmann-ihw99.pdf)). Jessica Fridrich, Miroslav Goljan and Dorin Hogea of Binghamton University stated the rules of the game in 2002: "As in cryptanalysis, we assume that the steganographic method is publicly known with the exception of a secret key", and "if there exists an algorithm that can guess whether or not a given image contains a secret message with a success rate better than random guessing, the steganographic system is considered broken" ([Fridrich, Goljan and Hogea, Steganalysis of JPEG Images: Breaking the F5 Algorithm, Information Hiding Workshop 2002](http://ws2.binghamton.edu/fridrich/Research/f5.pdf)). That definition is strict on purpose: a scheme is broken long before a detector is useful in practice.

Four distinctions organize the field, and every method below sits somewhere on them.

| Distinction | One side | Other side |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What the detector targets | Targeted: built for one embedding method (the chi-square attack for least significant bit (LSB) replacement, calibration for F5) | Universal or blind: features and a classifier trained on examples, meant to catch several methods |
| What it outputs | Detection: cover or stego | Quantitative: an estimate of the message length (RS analysis, sample pair analysis, the F5 attack) |
| How errors are counted | False alarm: a clean image called stego | Missed detection: a stego image called clean |
| What is known about the source | The detector was trained on images like the ones it tests | Cover-source mismatch: it was not |

Research papers often report a single error, PE, the smallest average of the false-alarm and missed-detection rates over all thresholds, under equal numbers of cover and stego images; 50% is a coin toss ([Holub, Fridrich and Denemark, Universal Distortion Function for Steganography in an Arbitrary Domain, 2014](http://dde.binghamton.edu/vholub/pdf/EURASIP14_Universal_Distortion_Function_for_Steganography_in_an_Arbitrary_Domain.pdf)). Competitions use measures closer to practice, such as the missed-detection rate at a fixed 5% false-alarm rate in ALASKA#1 ([ALASKA](https://alaska.utt.fr)). A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve plots the whole trade-off between detecting stego images and raising false alarms.

## Signatures and visual attacks

The first steganalysis was close inspection. Johnson and Jajodia compared cover and stego images made with many tools and found that "patterns begin to emerge as possible signatures of steganography software", such as "unusual sorting of color palettes" and exaggerated noise, signatures that could identify not only the presence of a message but "even the tools used in embedding the messages" ([Johnson and Jajodia, 1998](https://www.cse.buffalo.edu/courses/cse725/peter/Johnson_1997.pdf)).

Westfeld and Pfitzmann turned the eye into a filter. Their visual attack removes the image content and keeps only what the extraction function would read: for EzStego, a replacement palette paints every colour whose position in the sorted palette is even black and every odd one white, so the picture shows the hidden bit of each pixel ([Westfeld and Pfitzmann, 1999](https://users.ece.cmu.edu/~adrian/487-s06/westfeld-pfitzmann-ihw99.pdf)). In a clean photograph, the least-significant-bit plane still shows the outlines of the scene, because, as the authors showed, the tools of the time "suffer from the misassumption that least significant bits of image data are uncorrelated noise". Where a message was embedded, the structure gives way to noise: with EzStego's sequential embedding at half the capacity, the filtered image shows plainly where the message is; S-Tools spread its changes over the whole picture; Steganos filled the carrier completely even for a one-byte message ([Westfeld and Pfitzmann, 1999](https://users.ece.cmu.edu/~adrian/487-s06/westfeld-pfitzmann-ihw99.pdf)). The method's limits appear in the same paper: on an image of a flooring tile the filtered picture looked like noise with nothing embedded, and embedding in JPEG (Joint Photographic Experts Group) files offers nothing to look at, since one coefficient affects a whole block of pixels.

Visual inspection still finds careless embedding twelve years later. During the 2010 BOSS (Break Our Steganographic System!) competition, the Binghamton team saw the changes made by HUGO (Highly Undetectable steGO), a sophisticated adaptive scheme, in seven of the 1,000 test images: each contained a region saturated at 0 or 255, where forced embedding left salt-and-pepper noise in the least-significant-bit plane ([Fridrich, Kodovský, Holub and Goljan, Breaking HUGO: the Process Discovery, Information Hiding 2011](http://ws2.binghamton.edu/fridrich/Research/hugobreakers05.pdf)).

## The chi-square attack

Westfeld and Pfitzmann's statistical attack of 1999 is the classic targeted detector, and it can be computed by hand. It rests on the asymmetry shown in the previous part's worked example: LSB replacement only ever swaps a value with its partner in a fixed pair (2i, 2i + 1). Replacing least significant bits with random-looking message bits pushes the two counts of each pair towards equality, and, crucially, it never changes their sum ([Westfeld and Pfitzmann, 1999](https://users.ece.cmu.edu/~adrian/487-s06/westfeld-pfitzmann-ihw99.pdf)). The authors draw the consequence: the arithmetic mean of each pair is the same in the cover and in any stego version, so "we don't need the original carrier medium for the attack".

The test, in their notation, uses k categories, one per pair of values:

- the expected count in category i after full embedding is the mean of the pair, n*ᵢ = (n₂ᵢ + n₂ᵢ₊₁) / 2;
- the observed count is the even member, nᵢ = n₂ᵢ;
- the statistic is χ² = Σ (nᵢ − n*ᵢ)² / n*ᵢ, with k − 1 degrees of freedom;
- p, the upper-tail probability of that statistic, is read as the probability of embedding; each expected count must exceed 4, merging categories if needed ([Westfeld and Pfitzmann, 1999](https://users.ece.cmu.edu/~adrian/487-s06/westfeld-pfitzmann-ihw99.pdf)).

A small statistic means the pairs look equalized, so p is close to 1; a large statistic means the pairs are as unequal as in a natural image, so p is close to 0.

### Worked example: a histogram of 300 pixels

Take a toy image of 300 pixels whose values run from 0 to 7, four pairs of values (0,1), (2,3), (4,5) and (6,7). The left histogram is the cover; the right one is the same image after every least significant bit has been replaced with message bits. (Numbers chosen and computed for this article; each pair's total is the same in both, as the method requires.)

| Value | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cover count | 40 | 20 | 30 | 50 | 60 | 30 | 25 | 45 |
| Stego count | 31 | 29 | 41 | 39 | 44 | 46 | 36 | 34 |

| Pair | Pair total | Expected n*ᵢ | Cover nᵢ | Cover term | Stego nᵢ | Stego term |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| (0,1) | 60 | 30 | 40 | (40 − 30)² / 30 = 3.333 | 31 | (31 − 30)² / 30 = 0.033 |
| (2,3) | 80 | 40 | 30 | (30 − 40)² / 40 = 2.500 | 41 | (41 − 40)² / 40 = 0.025 |
| (4,5) | 90 | 45 | 60 | (60 − 45)² / 45 = 5.000 | 44 | (44 − 45)² / 45 = 0.022 |
| (6,7) | 70 | 35 | 25 | (25 − 35)² / 35 = 2.857 | 36 | (36 − 35)² / 35 = 0.029 |
| **χ²** | | | | **13.690** | | **0.109** |

With k = 4 pairs there are 3 degrees of freedom. For the cover, χ² = 13.690 gives p ≈ 0.0034: the pairs are far too unequal for an image whose least significant bits were all replaced, so embedding is very unlikely. For the stego image, χ² = 0.109 gives p ≈ 0.991: the pairs are almost perfectly equalized, which is what full LSB replacement produces. (For 3 degrees of freedom the upper tail has a closed form, erfc(√(x/2)) + √(2x/π)·e^(−x/2), which gives both values.)

### What the attack found, and what it missed

Westfeld and Pfitzmann applied the test to growing samples starting at the top of the image. On EzStego's flooring-tile image with 3,600 bytes embedded in its upper half, the probability of embedding was 0.8826 with 1% of the pixels, 0.9808 with 2%, never below 0.77 while the sample stayed in the upper half, and essentially 0 once the sample reached 52% of the pixels; with S-Tools, images with 99.5% of the capacity used gave p = 1 − ε, but an image with 50% used gave p = 0 + ε, "too weak to detect spreaded changes"; with Steganos, even a one-byte message was detected, because the tool padded the carrier to full capacity ([Westfeld and Pfitzmann, 1999](https://users.ece.cmu.edu/~adrian/487-s06/westfeld-pfitzmann-ihw99.pdf)). On a JSteg image with 50% of the capacity (7,680 bytes) used, Westfeld's later test gave a probability of 1.00 for samples up to 54% of the coefficients and 0.00 from 59% on ([Westfeld, F5: ein steganographischer Algorithmus, 2001](https://www2.htw-dresden.de/~westfeld/publikationen/westfeld.vis01.pdf)).

Two refinements followed. Niels Provos replaced the growing sample with a constant one of 3.2% of the coefficients slid across the image in steps of 1%, an "extended" test that finds partial distortions anywhere in the file; he also showed that it could not detect OutGuess's histogram-correcting embedding ([Provos, Defending Against Statistical Steganalysis, USENIX Security Symposium, August 2001](https://www.usenix.org/legacy/events/sec01/full_papers/provos/provos.pdf)). And Fridrich, Goljan and Rui Du summarized the attack's limit: it is very reliable when the message placement is known, but randomly scattered messages are detected "only when the message length becomes comparable with the number of pixels in the image" ([Fridrich, Goljan and Du, Reliable Detection of LSB Steganography in Color and Grayscale Images, 2001](https://www.cs.uaf.edu/2015/spring/cs463/lecture/04_06_stego/stego_detection_2001.pdf)). The attack also never touches LSB matching, which does not equalize pairs.

## RS analysis: measuring how flipping changes smoothness

Fridrich, Goljan and Du's RS analysis of 2001 detects scattered LSB replacement and estimates the message length ([Fridrich, Goljan and Du, 2001](https://www.cs.uaf.edu/2015/spring/cs463/lecture/04_06_stego/stego_detection_2001.pdf)). It needs three ingredients.

1. **A smoothness measure.** Divide the image into small groups of pixels, for example four consecutive pixels in a row, and compute a discrimination function, the variation f(x₁, …, xₙ) = Σ |xᵢ₊₁ − xᵢ|. Noisier groups give larger values.
2. **Two flipping operations.** F₁ swaps 0 and 1, 2 and 3, … 254 and 255, which is exactly what LSB replacement does. The shifted flip F₋₁ swaps −1 and 0, 1 and 2, 3 and 4, … 255 and 256, defined as F₋₁(x) = F₁(x + 1) − 1. A mask says which pixels of the group are flipped.
3. **Three categories.** A group is regular (R) if flipping increases f, singular (S) if it decreases f, unusable (U) if f is unchanged.

The statistical hypothesis is that in a typical image, the proportions of regular and singular groups are about the same whether the flip is F₁ or F₋₁. Randomizing the least significant bits breaks that symmetry in a specific way: as the message grows, the regular and singular counts under F₁ converge, while under F₋₁ they move apart ([Fridrich, Goljan and Du, 2001](https://www.cs.uaf.edu/2015/spring/cs463/lecture/04_06_stego/stego_detection_2001.pdf)). Fitting lines and parabolas through the measured points of this "RS diagram" and finding their intersection gives the estimated share of pixels carrying the message.

**Worked example** with one group and the mask [0 1 1 0], which flips the two middle pixels (computed for this article):

| Group | Pixels | Variation f | Compared with the original |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Original G | 100, 101, 103, 102 | 1 + 2 + 1 = 4 | |
| F₁ on the middle pixels | 100, 100, 102, 102 | 0 + 2 + 0 = 2 | f decreased: singular under F₁ |
| F₋₁ on the middle pixels | 100, 102, 104, 102 | 2 + 2 + 2 = 6 | f increased: regular under F₋₁ |

(F₁(101) = 100 and F₁(103) = 102; F₋₁(101) = F₁(102) − 1 = 102 and F₋₁(103) = F₁(104) − 1 = 104.) A real analysis counts tens of thousands of such groups under both flips and both mask polarities; the asymmetry between the two flips is the signal.

From their experiments the authors derived the bound quoted in the previous part, that "an upper bound of 0.005 bits/pixel was experimentally determined for safe LSB embedding" ([Fridrich, Goljan and Du, 2001](https://www.cs.uaf.edu/2015/spring/cs463/lecture/04_06_stego/stego_detection_2001.pdf)). The method was patented: United States patent 6,831,991, filed on 22 June 2001 by Fridrich and Goljan, assigned to the Research Foundation of the State University of New York and granted on 14 December 2004; its abstract says the invention "reliably detects messages as short as 1% of the total number of pixels (assuming 1 bit per sample)", and its statement of government interest names support from the United States Air Force under grants F30602-00-1-0521 and F49620-01-1-0123 ([Google Patents, US6831991B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6831991)). Google Patents lists the patent and its reissue as expired.

## Sample pair analysis and the structural family

Sorina Dumitrescu, Xiaolin Wu and Zhe Wang of McMaster University put the same intuition on a formal footing in "Detection of LSB Steganography via Sample Pair Analysis", published in 2003 in the IEEE (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers) Transactions on Signal Processing. They model how random LSB flipping moves pairs of neighbouring samples between "trace multisets" defined by the difference of their values, as a finite-state machine, and derive "a simple quadratic function that can estimate the length of embedded message with high precision" ([Dumitrescu, Wu and Wang, 2003](https://www.ece.mcmaster.ca/~sorina/papers/LSBfinalTSP.pdf)). On their set of 29 continuous-tone test images, the false-alarm rate with no message was 13.79% and the missing rate at a message of 3% of the samples was 11.03%, dropping quickly to 0 for longer messages; they also proved some of the key observations on which RS analysis had been built ([Dumitrescu, Wu and Wang, 2003](https://www.ece.mcmaster.ca/~sorina/papers/LSBfinalTSP.pdf)). Andrew Ker of the University of Oxford extended this line in a series of papers, among them "Improved detection of LSB steganography in grayscale images" (Information Hiding 2004) and "A general framework for the structural steganalysis of LSB replacement" (Information Hiding 2005) ([Ker, publications](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/andrew.ker/publications.html)).

These structural detectors are precise because LSB replacement is so regular. They are the reason the previous part calls replacement the most detectable method in common use.

## Breaking JPEG schemes: calibration and compatibility

**Calibration.** F5 decremented discrete cosine transform (DCT) coefficients instead of overwriting them and so escaped the chi-square test. Fridrich, Goljan and Hogea broke it in 2002 by estimating the cover's histogram from the stego image itself: "decompressing the stego-image, cropping it by four pixels in both directions to remove the quantization in the frequency domain, and recompressing it using the same quality factor as the stego-image" ([Fridrich, Goljan and Hogea, 2002](http://ws2.binghamton.edu/fridrich/Research/f5.pdf)). Shifting the 8 × 8 grid by four pixels destroys the block structure in which the embedding happened, so the recompressed crop has statistics close to those of the original cover. Since F5 changes the histogram in a known way (each modified coefficient drops by one in absolute value, so the expected stego count at value d is 1 − β times the cover count at d plus β times the cover count at d + 1), the share β of modified coefficients is the value that best explains the difference between the estimate and the stego image, found by least squares ([Fridrich, Goljan and Hogea, 2002](http://ws2.binghamton.edu/fridrich/Research/f5.pdf)). Their experiments indicated "that relative modifications as small as 10% of the usable DCT coefficients can be reliably detected". Calibration became a standard ingredient of JPEG feature sets for a decade.

**Attacking OutGuess.** The same authors detected OutGuess, the tool that repaired its histogram, at the Association for Computing Machinery (ACM) Workshop on Multimedia and Security in December 2002; OutGuess's author records the result on his own page as "capable of reliably detecting images with content embedded by OutGuess" ([Fridrich, publications](http://ws2.binghamton.edu/fridrich/publications.html); [Provos, Outguess 0.2](https://www.provos.org/p/outguess/)).

**JPEG compatibility.** An image that was once a JPEG keeps the fingerprint of its quantization even after it is saved in a lossless format. Fridrich, Goljan and Du noted that "the quantization introduced by JPEG compression can serve as a 'watermark' or a unique fingerprint, and you can detect even very small modifications of the cover image by inspecting the compatibility of the stego-image with the JPEG format", which makes such images "a very poor choice for cover images" ([Fridrich, Goljan and Du, 2001](https://www.cs.uaf.edu/2015/spring/cs463/lecture/04_06_stego/stego_detection_2001.pdf)). The idea returned in 2019 as a "reverse JPEG compatibility attack" against images compressed at quality 99 and 100, developed during the ALASKA competition ([Yousfi, Butora, Fridrich and Giboulot, Breaking ALASKA: Color Separation for Steganalysis in JPEG Domain, ACM IH&MMSec 2019](http://ws2.binghamton.edu/fridrich/Research/ALASKA-preprint1.pdf)).

## Steganalysis at Internet scale, and base rates

Provos wrote Stegdetect, "an automated tool for detecting steganographic content in images", able to detect JSteg, JPHide, Invisible Secrets, OutGuess 0.13b, F5 (by header analysis) and data appended to JPEG files, with a companion, Stegbreak, for dictionary attacks against the passwords of JSteg-Shell, JPHide and OutGuess 0.13b ([Provos, Steganography Detection with Stegdetect, 2004](https://www.provos.org/p/detection-with-stegdetect/)). His news page dates the first release to 14 April 2001 ([Provos, Outguess 0.2](https://www.provos.org/p/outguess/)), and his release notes date version 0.6, which added linear discriminant analysis "to detect any stego system", to 6 September 2004 ([Provos, Outguess and Stegdetect Downloads](https://www.provos.org/p/outguess-and-stegdetect-downloads/)).

With Peter Honeyman, Provos then tested a public claim. The 2003 article "Hide and Seek: An Introduction to Steganography" describes how they built a crawler, downloaded "more than two million images linked to eBay auctions" and "an additional one million images from a Usenet archive", ran Stegdetect, and found "that over 1 percent of all images seemed to contain hidden content"; dictionary attacks run on about 60 nodes at the University of Michigan then tried to recover the messages ([Provos and Honeyman, Hide and Seek: An Introduction to Steganography, IEEE Security and Privacy, May/June 2003](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~chazelle/courses/BIB/stegano-honeyman.pdf)). The result: "From our eBay and Usenet research, we so far have not found a single hidden message." They offered four explanations, from strong passwords to tools they could not detect, and concluded that "either we are looking in the wrong place or there is no widespread use of steganography on the Internet" ([Provos and Honeyman, 2003](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~chazelle/courses/BIB/stegano-honeyman.pdf)). They chose eBay because of news reports; those reports, and what was and was not established about them, are examined in a later part of this series on steganography in espionage and the courts.

**Worked example: why "1% flagged" is not "1% guilty".** Provos and Honeyman cite Stefan Axelsson's application of the base-rate fallacy to intrusion detection ([Provos and Honeyman, 2003](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~chazelle/courses/BIB/stegano-honeyman.pdf)). The arithmetic below is for this article. Suppose a detector flags 1% of clean images by mistake and catches 90% of stego images, and suppose 1 image in 100,000 really carries a message. Among 2,000,000 images there are then 20 stego images, of which 18 are caught, and 1,999,980 clean ones, of which about 20,000 are flagged. Of roughly 20,018 alarms, 18 are real: fewer than one in a thousand. A detector good enough for a research paper can be nearly useless for screening unless the base rate is high or the false-alarm rate is tiny, which is why competitions began to score detectors at low false-alarm rates.

## Universal steganalysis: features and machine learning

**Higher-order statistics.** Hany Farid of Dartmouth College argued in 2002 that detectors examining "first-order statistical distributions of intensity or transform coefficients" are easily foiled by schemes that preserve those distributions, and proposed instead a wavelet-like decomposition to build higher-order statistical models of natural images; "a Fisher linear discriminant analysis is then used to discriminate between untouched and adulterated images" ([Farid, Detecting Hidden Messages Using Higher-Order Statistical Models, ICIP 2002](https://farid.berkeley.edu/downloads/publications/icip02.pdf)). This is the template of universal steganalysis: describe the image with features sensitive to embedding, then learn the boundary between covers and stego images from examples.

**From SPAM to rich models.** Feature sets grew with the schemes they chased. The subtractive pixel adjacency matrix (SPAM) features, built from differences of neighbouring pixels modelled as a Markov chain, were tuned on ±1 embedding; HUGO was designed against them ([Fridrich and Kodovský, Rich Models for Steganalysis of Digital Images, IEEE Transactions on Information Forensics and Security, 2012](http://dde.binghamton.edu/kodovsky/pdf/TIFS2012-SRM.pdf)). Fridrich and Jan Kodovský's spatial rich model (SRM) of 2012 assembles "a rich model of the noise component as a union of many diverse submodels formed by joint distributions of neighboring samples from quantized image noise residuals obtained using linear and non-linear high-pass filters": 39 submodels, 106 when quantized versions are counted separately, for a total of 34,671 features, with the extractor published by Binghamton's Digital Data Embedding (DDE) Laboratory ([Fridrich and Kodovský, 2012](http://dde.binghamton.edu/kodovsky/pdf/TIFS2012-SRM.pdf); [DDE Lab, Feature Extractors](https://dde.binghamton.edu/download/feature_extractors)). Their conclusion became the field's programme: "It appears that any substantial progress is only possible when steganalysts incorporate more complex models that capture a large number of dependencies among pixels."

**Ensemble classifiers.** Support vector machines struggled with tens of thousands of features and training sets of tens of thousands of images. Kodovský, Fridrich and Vojtěch Holub proposed "ensemble classifiers implemented as random forrests" (sic) of simple Fisher linear discriminants, each trained on a random subset of the features, and argued that they are "ideally suited for steganalysis" ([Kodovský, Fridrich and Holub, Ensemble Classifiers for Steganalysis of Digital Media, IEEE Transactions on Information Forensics and Security, 2012](http://dde.binghamton.edu/kodovsky/pdf/TIFS-2011-ensemble.pdf)). The trade-off is measurable: on BOSSbase version 0.92 (9,074 images from seven cameras, 512 × 512 pixels), the ensemble with a 12,753-feature rich model reached a median detection error of 7.85% on ±1 embedding and 13.55% on HUGO at 0.4 bits per pixel, while a Gaussian support vector machine on a 3,300-feature model did slightly better, 6.83% and 13.10%, but took 4 days 22 hours instead of 1 hour 20 minutes for ±1 embedding on the authors' processor ([Fridrich and Kodovský, 2012](http://dde.binghamton.edu/kodovsky/pdf/TIFS2012-SRM.pdf)). The DDE Laboratory's list of extractors traces the later refinements: JPEG rich models, the selection-channel-aware maxSRM and further JPEG feature sets ([DDE Lab, Feature Extractors](https://dde.binghamton.edu/download/feature_extractors)).

## BOSS, 2010 to 2011: the first open competition

"Break Our Steganographic System!" (BOSS) was the first international steganalysis challenge ([Springer, Information Hiding 2011](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/978-3-642-24178-9_5)). Its site gives a warming phase from 28 June to 9 September 2010 and a ranking phase from 9 September 2010 to 10 January 2011; participants received 512 × 512 greyscale training covers in the raw PGM (Portable GrayMap) format, matching stego images with the same fixed payload, the embedding program and an implementation of SPAM features, and were ranked on a set of 1,000 images, BOSSrank, with one trial allowed every three days ([BOSS, rules](http://agents.fel.cvut.cz/boss/index.php?mode=VIEW&tmpl=rules)). The algorithm was HUGO at 0.4 bits per pixel in every image ([Fridrich, Kodovský, Holub and Goljan, 2011](http://ws2.binghamton.edu/fridrich/Research/hugobreakers05.pdf)). The organizers, Patrick Bas, Tomáš Filler and Tomáš Pevný, published "Break Our Steganographic System: The Ins and Outs of Organizing BOSS" at Information Hiding 2011 (Lecture Notes in Computer Science volume 6958, pages 59 to 70); according to its abstract, the paper analyses the submitted results and highlights the cover-source mismatch problem, in which participants had to train their detectors on one source of images and test them on another ([Springer, Information Hiding 2011](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/978-3-642-24178-9_5)). The final scores on BOSSrank were 80.3% for the HugoBreakers (Binghamton University), 76.8% for Gül and Kurugollu (listed in the hall of fame as Queens University) and 67.0% for Andreas Westfeld ([BOSS, home page](http://agents.fel.cvut.cz/boss/index.php?mode=VIEW&tmpl=home)).

The winners published their whole journey, and it is the best account of what detection outside the laboratory involves ([Fridrich, Kodovský, Holub and Goljan, 2011](http://ws2.binghamton.edu/fridrich/Research/hugobreakers05.pdf)):

- **Forensics on the test set.** On 14 October 2010 they extracted the sensor fingerprint of each camera in the training base and tested the 1,000 BOSSrank images: only one camera matched, a Leica M9, in about 490 images. Other clues (licence plates from Oregon and Washington, an address) placed some images in the Pacific Northwest, and a reflection in a window identified another camera as belonging to a member of the organizing team; the authors note that they did not use that last finding in the competition, because it relied on information other competitors did not have.
- **The cover source is more than a camera.** They rented a Leica M9 for a week (23 to 30 October), took 7,301 images in Binghamton in the autumn with a 50 mm lens, embedded them at 0.4 bits per pixel and retrained: the score was "a measly 74%". Their lesson: "The cover source is a very complex entity that is affected by the lens, the environment in which pictures are taken and even the photographer's habits."
- **Scale won.** Low-complexity ensemble classifiers let them keep adding feature types and training images: 77% on 15 November with 5,330 features, 78% on 29 November with 9,288 features and 2 × 15,574 training images, and a final feature set of 24,993 dimensions. Under cover-source mismatch they also saw a peculiar optimum, best results when the number of training images per class was 20% to 50% larger than the feature dimension. Their submission scored 80.3%; after the ground truth was released they found their best prediction file would have scored 80.5%.

## Deep learning, from 2014

Marc Chaumont of the University of Montpellier dates the first attempt to apply deep learning to steganalysis to auto-encoders by Shunquan Tan and Bin Li at the end of 2014, followed at the beginning of 2015 by the convolutional neural network (CNN) of Yinlong Qian, Jing Dong, Wei Wang and Tieniu Tan: "In 2015, the first study using a convolutional neural network (CNN) obtained the first results of steganalysis by Deep Learning approaching the performances of the two-step approach (EC + RM)", the ensemble classifier with rich models ([Chaumont, Deep Learning in Steganography and Steganalysis from 2015 to 2018, arXiv 1904.01444, 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01444)). His chronology continues: in 2016, results close to the state of the art with an ensemble of CNNs built on Xu-Net (Guanshuo Xu, Han-Zhou Wu and Yun Q. Shi, IEEE Signal Processing Letters, May 2016); then Ye-Net (Jian Ye, Jiangqun Ni and Y. Yi, November 2017), Yedroudj-Net (January 2018), ReST-Net (May 2018) and SRNet (May 2019, online since September 2018), which "clearly surpass the 'old' two-step machine learning paradigm" ([Chaumont, 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01444)).

The early networks borrowed from the rich models: fixed high-pass filters in the first layer, or kernels initialized with SRM filters. Mehdi Boroumand, Mo Chen and Fridrich designed SRNet to "minimize the use of heuristics and externally enforced elements"; its key part is "a significantly expanded front part of the detector that 'computes noise residuals' in which pooling has been disabled to prevent suppression of the stego signal", and it works in both the spatial and the JPEG domains ([Boroumand, Chen and Fridrich, Deep Residual Network for Steganalysis of Digital Images, IEEE Transactions on Information Forensics and Security, 2019](http://ws2.binghamton.edu/fridrich/Research/SRNet.pdf)). A sense of scale from Chaumont: SRNet, one of the most successful detectors at the end of 2018, detected the adaptive scheme HILL at 0.1 bits per pixel on 256 × 256 BOSS images with a probability of error of 31.3%, "note that a Pe of 50% is equivalent to a random detector" ([Chaumont, 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01444)). Adaptive embedding at low payload remains genuinely hard to detect even for the best networks.

Fridrich's group has since asked how far such detectors can still improve. Working with artificial images whose statistical model is known, Edgar Kaziakhmedov, Eli Dworetzky and Fridrich found in 2023 that SRNet loses little against the optimal detector on smooth images but can lose a lot on textured ones, and that a network trained on many realizations of one fixed textured scene "will, however, recuperate most of the loss" ([Kaziakhmedov, Dworetzky and Fridrich, Limits of Data Driven Steganography Detectors, ACM IH&MMSec 2023](http://ws2.binghamton.edu/fridrich/Research/WAWTY-final_v3.pdf)).

## ALASKA, 2019 and 2020: steganalysis "into the wild"

Rémi Cogranne and Quentin Giboulot of the Troyes University of Technology and Patrick Bas of the École Centrale de Lille organized the ALASKA challenges, "in part, inspired by the BOSS competition", with partial funding from a French national research programme of 2018 named on the site ([ALASKA](https://alaska.utt.fr)). Their site explains why a new image base was needed: BOSS's images were "rather homogeneous since it is based on a small set of 8 DLSR cameras", all processed the same way, uncompressed, greyscale and of the same size.

**ALASKA#1** (2019) used highly heterogeneous JPEG images and ranked entries by the missed-detection rate at a 5% false-alarm rate; the site's hall of fame lists Binghamton University first with 24.37% missed detections, ahead of Shenzhen University with 50% ([ALASKA](https://alaska.utt.fr)). The winners, Yassine Yousfi, Jan Butora, Fridrich and Quentin Giboulot, trained SRNet "tile detectors" for each quality factor from 60 to 98 on combinations of the luminance and two colour channels, and developed the reverse JPEG compatibility attack for qualities 99 and 100 ([Yousfi, Butora, Fridrich and Giboulot, 2019](http://ws2.binghamton.edu/fridrich/Research/ALASKA-preprint1.pdf)).

**ALASKA#2** (2020) ran on the Kaggle competition platform. The organizers' page describes a base of 80,000 images from more than 40 cameras, "included smartphones, tablets, low-end cameras to high-end full frame DLSR"; a test set of 5,000 images and four training sets of 75,000; colour JPEG images at quality factors 95, 90 and 75; three embedding algorithms described in the previous part, J-UNIWARD, UERD and J-MiPOD, at an average payload of 0.4 bits per non-zero AC (alternating current, that is, non-average) coefficient varying with image complexity; $25,000 in prizes for the three best performers; ranking by a weighted area under the ROC curve "to focus on detection accuracy for low false probability"; and an end date of 20 July 2020 ([ALASKA](https://alaska.utt.fr)). The Signal Processing Society of the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) announced the challenge on 1 May 2020 with an entry deadline of 13 July 2020 and a special session at the IEEE Workshop on Information Forensics and Security (WIFS) in New York in December 2020 ([IEEE Signal Processing Society, 1 May 2020](https://signalprocessingsociety.org/our-story/news-updates/2020/05/alaska-2-steganalysis-challenge-open)).

The measure deserves a word. The weighted area under the curve (wAUC) used in ALASKA#2 is (2A₀.₄ + A₁)/3, where A₀.₄ is the part of the area under the ROC curve that lies in the band of detection rates from 0 to 0.4 and A₁ the part above 0.4 ([Yousfi, Butora, Khvedchenya and Fridrich, ImageNet Pre-trained CNNs for JPEG Steganalysis, IEEE WIFS 2020](http://ws2.binghamton.edu/fridrich/Research/Alaska-2-Revised.pdf)); the lower band counts twice, which is how the organizers focused the score on low false alarms. The most striking finding came from the competitors: networks pre-trained on ImageNet for ordinary image classification, such as EfficientNet and MixNet, then refined for steganalysis, beat purpose-built ones. In the words of the Binghamton team, joined late by Eugene Khvedchenya, this approach "was predominantly employed by virtually all top performers", because "detecting stego noise is essentially equivalent to detecting traces of the content itself"; their prize-winning blended submission scored 0.932 on the private leaderboard ([Yousfi, Butora, Khvedchenya and Fridrich, 2020](http://ws2.binghamton.edu/fridrich/Research/Alaska-2-Revised.pdf)).

## From the laboratory to the real world

The competitions made explicit what laboratory results hide.

- **Cover-source mismatch.** A detector trained on one camera, one processing chain and one kind of scene degrades on others; the BOSS winners lost points to a lens and a season ([Fridrich, Kodovský, Holub and Goljan, 2011](http://ws2.binghamton.edu/fridrich/Research/hugobreakers05.pdf)), and ALASKA was built to measure exactly that ([ALASKA](https://alaska.utt.fr)).
- **Base rates.** Screening millions of images at a 1% false-alarm rate produces tens of thousands of false alarms, as Provos and Honeyman found ([Provos and Honeyman, 2003](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~chazelle/courses/BIB/stegano-honeyman.pdf)).
- **Size and quantity.** The square root law makes detection easier as images grow ([Ker, Pevný, Kodovský and Fridrich, The Square Root Law of Steganographic Capacity, slides, 2008](http://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/andrew.ker/docs/keretal-acm08-slides.pdf)); conversely, a steganographer can spread a message over many images, and Ker's work on "batch steganography and pooled steganalysis" (2006) and on identifying "the steganographer" as an outlier among many users studies that game ([Ker, publications](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/andrew.ker/publications.html)).
- **Unknown method.** Laboratory detectors are trained against a known algorithm and payload; a real examiner rarely knows either. Targeted attacks on popular tools, and checks for the tools themselves, remain practical complements to the statistical detectors.

In capture-the-flag (CTF) games these tools are routine, because organizers hide flags in images; the [article on CTF competitions](/learn/capture-the-flag-competitions) places such puzzles in the forensics and miscellaneous categories. A game puzzle and an investigation differ in one essential way: in a game the organizer guarantees that something is there.

Commercial products exist as well, and their claims are theirs. WetStone Technologies says it has developed software for cyber-crime investigations "since 1998" and markets StegoHunt MP and StegoEnterprise, which "assist with the detection of steganography, using advanced algorithms and forensic methods to identify possible carriers of concealed information or malware" ([WetStone Technologies](https://wetstonetech.com); [WetStone, DFIR page](https://www.wetstonetech.com/dfir/)). Data loss prevention (DLP) platforms have added the function: Netskope documents a "Steganographic Detection" feature for its Advanced DLP customers, a machine-learning model that looks for "structural anomalies consistent with known steganography tools and methods", only for PNG and BMP images, not for images smaller than 250 pixels in height or width, and that produces an alert rather than an incident; the company states that the model "is engineered to identify the presence of steganography, not to extract or decode the hidden content" ([Netskope, Steganographic Detection](https://docs.netskope.com/en/steganographic-detection/)). For forensic examiners, Gary Kessler's "An Overview of Steganography for the Computer Forensics Examiner", published in the FBI's Forensic Science Communications in July 2004, was written for those "who need a practical understanding of steganography without delving into the mathematics" ([Embry-Riddle Aeronautical University, Scholarly Commons record](https://commons.erau.edu/db-security-studies/19)).

## The Brazilian angle

Brazil's steganalysis research has a clear starting point at the University of Campinas (Unicamp). Anderson de Rezende Rocha defended his master's dissertation, "Progressive randomization for steganalysis", at Unicamp's Institute of Computing on 17 February 2006, advised by Siome Klein Goldenstein, with a scholarship from the São Paulo Research Foundation (FAPESP), process 04/02384-1, for the analysis and implementation of techniques to detect messages hidden in digital images. The record describes "a new methodology to detect the presence of hidden digital content in the Least Significant Bits (LSBs) of images": a progressive series of LSB-modifying transformations, each representing a possible hiding scenario of increasing size, whose statistics reveal whether the input image carries a message, validated "with 20,000 real, non-synthetic images" ([FAPESP Virtual Library, Progressive randomization for steganalysis](https://bv.fapesp.br/en/dissertacoes-teses/74471/progressive-randomization-for-steganalysis)). Rocha and Goldenstein generalized the descriptor for image categorization at the International Conference on Computer Vision (ICCV) in 2007 ([Rocha and Goldenstein, PR: More than Meets the Eye, ICCV 2007](https://ic.unicamp.br/~siome/papers/pr-iccv07.pdf)) and published "Progressive randomization: Seeing the unseen" in Computer Vision and Image Understanding (volume 114, number 3, March 2010, pages 349 to 362), validated "using two different inference scenarios and four image databases" ([FAPESP Virtual Library, Progressive randomization: Seeing the unseen](https://bv.fapesp.br/en/publicacao/40780/progressive-randomization-seeing-the-unseen)). Their 2008 tutorial in a Brazilian journal is cited in the previous part.

The subject also reaches Brazilian students now. At the 26th Brazilian Cybersecurity Symposium (SBSeg) in Armação dos Búzios in 2026, a paper in the undergraduate research workshop, "STEGS2GAN", by João Pedro B. S. Souza, Breno W. B. Correia and Kleber M. Trevisani, describes a generative adversarial network (GAN) whose generator is trained to hide text in images and whose discriminator is trained to detect it, published on 1 September 2026 ([SBC OpenLib, SBSeg 2026 WTICG](https://sol.sbc.org.br/index.php/sbseg_estendido/article/view/44520)). Generated-content steganography is the subject of a later part of this series.

One Brazilian case is often cited in this context, and its status should be read carefully. Agence France-Presse (AFP) reported on 11 March 2008, citing the newspaper Folha de S.Paulo of the previous day, that according to Brazilian police the Colombian drug trafficker Juan Carlos Ramírez Abadía, arrested in Brazil in August 2007, had hidden encrypted documents and voice messages in images of Hello Kitty attached to emails, and that the hidden messages were decoded by the United States Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) because Brazilian police lacked the necessary equipment ([AFPBB News, 11 March 2008](https://www.afpbb.com/articles/-/2362503)). This is a press report of a police statement, not a finding of a court read for this article; how such cases reach the record is discussed in the later part on espionage and the courts.

## What defenders can do

- **Match the detector to the format and the threat.** LSB replacement in PNG or BMP files falls to structural detectors (chi-square, RS, sample pairs) and to free tools that combine them; JPEG schemes need calibrated or learned features; modern adaptive schemes at low payload are a research problem, not a screening target.
- **Budget for false alarms.** Decide the false-alarm rate you can afford before you scan, and treat a detector's output as triage, not proof; the base-rate example above is the reason.
- **Look for the tools, not only the images.** A strong statistical detector is rarely needed when the tool that embedded the message, or a flaw in it, gives the game away; signature work began with Johnson and Jajodia and continues in cases like CVE-2021-27211.
- **Use the controls you already have.** The United States National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) lists "monitoring for steganography" under SC-7(10) and notes that covert exfiltration means "include steganography" under SI-4(18) ([NIST SP 800-53 Revision 5, September 2020](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/SpecialPublications/NIST.SP.800-53r5.pdf)); data loss prevention and the egress controls described in [DLP fundamentals](/learn/dlp-fundamentals) apply. Where detection is impractical, removing the channel, by re-encoding images at the boundary, is often cheaper; a later part covers stripping hidden content in practice.

## What to remember

- Steganalysis decides whether a message is present and sometimes how long it is; by the strict definition, a scheme is broken as soon as a detector beats random guessing.
- The chi-square attack (Westfeld and Pfitzmann, 1999) exploits the equalization of pairs of values by LSB replacement and needs no original image; it is strong on sequential embedding and weak on short scattered messages.
- RS analysis (2001) and sample pair analysis (2003) estimate the length of scattered LSB replacement from how flipping changes local smoothness and pair statistics.
- Calibration (2002) breaks JPEG schemes such as F5 by estimating the cover's statistics from a cropped, recompressed copy.
- Universal steganalysis grew from Farid's wavelet statistics (2002) to rich models with 34,671 features and ensemble classifiers (2012), then to deep networks (from 2014 to 2015), which now lead.
- BOSS (2010 to 2011) and ALASKA (2019 and 2020) showed that detection depends on the cover source; pre-trained ImageNet networks won ALASKA#2.
- A detector's false-alarm rate, multiplied by the number of innocent files, decides whether it is usable in practice.
