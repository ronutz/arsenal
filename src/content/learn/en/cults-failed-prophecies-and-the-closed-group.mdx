Two questions run through the history of small, intense groups. The first is what believers do when a prophecy fails: when the day passes and the world is still there. The second is what happens inside a group that cuts itself off from outsiders, under a leader who controls what members hear: whether its members chose freely, were deceived, or were coerced, and who has the right to decide. Both questions are bitterly contested, by scholars, by former members, by families and by the groups themselves, and the words used to ask them, "cult" and "brainwashing" among them, are part of the dispute. This part of the series on [mass hysteria and collective belief](/learn/mass-hysteria-how-groups-come-to-believe-anything) follows the cases through their primary records: the writings of William Miller and his followers, the papers behind a famous psychology book, the reports of the United States (US) Congress and Justice Department, the Brazilian dissertations, inspectors' findings and court decisions. After reading, you can say what each inquiry and court found, why the words are disputed, and what the evidence says, and does not say, about belief that survives disconfirmation.

This part discusses deaths, including suicides. It describes no methods. ## The words, and why they are contested

In 1986 a task force of the American Psychological Association (APA) on Deceptive and Indirect Methods of Persuasion and Control (DIMPAC), led by the psychologist Margaret Singer, defined a cult of the "totalist type" as "a group or movement exhibiting a great or excessive devotion or dedication to some person, idea, or thing and employing unethically manipulative (i.e., deceptive and indirect) techniques of persuasion and control designed to advance the goals of the group's leaders, to the actual or possible detriment of members, their families, or the community", listing among the techniques "isolation from former friends and family", "powerful group pressures", "information management" and "promotion of total dependency on the group and fear of leaving it" ([DIMPAC draft report, 1986, CESNUR copy](https://www.cesnur.org/testi/DIMPAC.htm)). The report estimated that "at least ten million Americans have been at least transiently involved with cultic groups during the past decade and a half", while noting that "No reliable data exist which would permit a comparison of the frequency of physical or psychological harm in religious cults and in mainstream society" ([DIMPAC, 1986](https://www.cesnur.org/testi/DIMPAC.htm)).

Many sociologists of religion reject the word for groups they prefer to call new religious movements. The sociologist Nancy Ammerman, advising the Justice and Treasury Departments after Waco in 1993, wrote that people who call themselves "cult experts" "are certainly not recognized as such by the academic community", and that "The notion of 'cult brainwashing' has been thoroughly discredited in the academic community" ([Ammerman, Report to the Justice and Treasury Departments, 3 September 1993](https://hirr.hartfordinternational.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Report-to-the-Justice-and-Treasury-Departments.pdf)). Official inquiries have been cautious too. The congressional staff report on Jonestown declared it "outside the parameters of this committee's inquiry" whether Peoples Temple was a "cult", while concluding that "Although People's Temple may have been a bona fide church in its Indiana and early California origins, it progressively lost that characterization in almost every respect" ([House Committee on Foreign Affairs, staff investigative group report, 15 May 1979, text](https://jonestown.sdsu.edu/?p=13674)). Special Counsel John Danforth's report on Waco recorded that Branch Davidians "objected to" the word "compound" for their home, and "In deference to them" called it the Branch Davidian complex ([Danforth, Final Report to the Deputy Attorney General, 8 November 2000](https://www.policinginstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/Danforth-Report-Waco-1993-Incident_11-8-2000.pdf)). This part uses each group's own name, and "cult" only where a source uses it.

## The Millerites and the Great Disappointment, 1844

### The prophecy and its scale

William Miller, a farmer from Hampton, New York, "generally known as a lecturer on the prophecies" in the subtitle of his 1853 biography, published in 1836 the lectures he titled Evidence from Scripture and History of the Second Coming of Christ, about the Year 1843 ([Miller, 1836, Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/WilliamMillerEvidenceFromScriptureAndHistoryOfTheSecondComingOf)). In his Apology and Defence of 1845 he wrote that he had given some four thousand lectures in about five hundred towns across New England, the Middle States, Ohio, Michigan, Maryland, the District of Columbia and Canada, and estimated that Advent congregations had been raised in nearly a thousand places, "some fifty thousand believers" ([Miller, Apology and Defence, 1845, Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/WilliamMillerMr.MillersApologyAndDefence1845)). The estimate is his own and the only figure found for this part. When the year 1843 to 1844 passed, Miller wrote that he "frankly acknowledged my disappointment" about the exact period but held that "my faith was unchanged in any essential feature" ([Miller, 1845](https://archive.org/details/WilliamMillerMr.MillersApologyAndDefence1845)).

The final date came from his followers. According to Sylvester Bliss's biography of 1853, some had fixed on "the tenth day of the seventh month of the current Jewish year", which "fell on the 22d day of October"; the idea "was not generally received with favor" by those close to Miller until a few weeks before, and Miller accepted it only on 6 October ([Bliss, Memoirs of William Miller, 1853, Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/memoirsofwilliam00blis)). After 22 October 1844 Miller wrote: "I confess my error", adding "yet I still believe that the day of the Lord is near, even at the door" ([Bliss, 1853](https://archive.org/details/memoirsofwilliam00blis)).

### What believers felt, and what they did next

Hiram Edson, a Millerite farmer in western New York, wrote of the day after: "Our fondest hopes and expectations were blasted", and "such a spirit of weeping came over us as I never experienced before ... We wept, and wept, till the day dawn." On 23 October, crossing a cornfield, he reached a new reading: that on 22 October 1844 Christ had entered "the second apartment" of the heavenly sanctuary rather than returning to earth; with two companions he published it in 1845, and it became a founding doctrine of the Sabbatarian Adventists, later the Seventh-day Adventist Church ([Strayer, Encyclopedia of Seventh-day Adventists, entry "Edson, Hiram"](https://encyclopedia.adventist.org/assets/pdf/article-bjin.pdf)). The date was kept and its meaning moved from earth to heaven, the kind of reinterpretation that later scholars of failed prophecy would catalogue.

### What contemporaries believed, and the sourcing problem

Physicians and newspapers saw in Millerism a danger to sanity. The American Journal of Insanity wrote in January 1845 that newspapers for months had carried "accounts of suicides and insanity produced by it", and that three asylums in the northern states had admitted "thirty-two patients" in the past year "whose insanity was attributed to Millerism" ([American Journal of Insanity 1(3), January 1845, 249-253, Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/sim_american-journal-of-psychiatry_1845-01_1)). The same article allowed "something for exaggeration and mistakes", granted that "the promulgators and believers of this doctrine were sincere and pious people", and argued that "such things spread chiefly by contagion and imitation", advising families not to attend such preaching ([American Journal of Insanity, 1845](https://archive.org/details/sim_american-journal-of-psychiatry_1845-01_1)). The causes of admission recorded in that era are attributions by asylum doctors, not diagnoses in the modern sense; the count is a record of what was believed, and the figures cannot be checked against case files here.

The most famous image of the movement, believers in white "ascension robes" waiting on hilltops, has the same problem. Comic stories of robes circulated in the press, such as "A 'Millerite' Miracle", reprinted by a Pennsylvania weekly in October 1845 from a New York paper, in which a prankster in "a robe of cheap cotton" is hoisted into a tree ([Jeffersonian Republican, Stroudsburg, 9 October 1845, Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/C.a.p.AmilleriteMiracle.-InJeffersonianRepublican1845Oct9)). Bliss, a Millerite writing in 1853, replied that "the published accounts of these were greatly exaggerated" and that the reports of ascension robes "were demonstrated over and over again to be false and scandalous" ([Bliss, 1853](https://archive.org/details/memoirsofwilliam00blis)). <Claim status="disputed" by="by the press of 1844-1845 against Miller's biographer">Whether Millerites wore ascension robes</Claim> cannot be settled from these sources: one side wrote satire, the other apologetics, and the twentieth-century historians who examined the newspaper record could not be read for this part.

## When Prophecy Fails, and what the archives show

### The book and its claim

In 1956 the psychologists Leon Festinger, Henry Riecken and Stanley Schachter published When Prophecy Fails, about a small group around Dorothy Martin, a Chicago housewife, who predicted a flood for 21 December 1954 from which flying saucers would rescue the faithful ([Internet Archive, catalogue record of the 1956 edition](https://archive.org/details/whenprophecyfail0000fest)). The book became the founding case study of cognitive dissonance, which the APA Dictionary of Psychology defines as "an unpleasant psychological state resulting from inconsistency between two or more elements in a cognitive system", one that "creates a motivational drive in an individual to reduce the dissonance" ([APA Dictionary of Psychology, "cognitive dissonance"](https://dictionary.apa.org/cognitive-dissonance)). The sociologist Lorne Dawson summarized the book's claim: after the failure, "a faithful core persisted and redoubled its efforts to convince others", and he noted that many of the group's members "were actually social scientists engaged in covert participant observation" ([Dawson, Nova Religio, 1999](https://www.gwern.net/doc/sociology/1999-dawson.pdf)).

### What later studies found

Reviewing later studies of groups whose prophecies failed, Dawson found that many survived but "The reasons are much more complicated than When Prophecy Fails implies": "only a minority of groups studied (four of thirteen) sought to convert others to compensate for their disappointment", so the original law "is incorrect, or, at any rate, it is framed too narrowly". He quoted J. Gordon Melton: "the denial of failure is not just another option, but the common mode of adaptation of millennial groups following the failure of a prophecy" ([Dawson, 1999](https://www.gwern.net/doc/sociology/1999-dawson.pdf)). The site's essay [The end is always near](/blog/the-end-is-always-near-apocalypse-fear-and-who-owns-the-feed) follows the same pattern into Harold Camping's failed predictions of 2011.

### The archival critique

In 2025 the political scientist Thomas Kelly published a study drawing on "newly unsealed archival material", including Leon Festinger's papers and the notes of the observers held at the Bentley Historical Library in Ann Arbor, Michigan. His abstract states that when the flood did not come Martin "recanted, her group dissolved, and efforts to proselytize ceased", that "the group actively proselytized well before the prophecy failed and quickly abandoned their beliefs afterward", and that the documents "expose serious ethical violations by the researchers, including fabricated psychic messages, covert manipulation, and interference in a child welfare investigation"; one co-author, Riecken, "posed as a spiritual authority and later admitted he had 'precipitated' the climactic events of the study" ([Kelly, Debunking "When Prophecy Fails", preprint revised 1 September 2025](https://osf.io/9j7qc_v2); [published in the Journal of the History of the Behavioral Sciences 62(1), 4 November 2025, record](https://doi.org/10.1002/jhbs.70043)). Kelly also documents that Charles Laughead, a physician in the group, had been forced out of his job at the Michigan State University health centre shortly before the predicted date after trying to convert student patients ([Kelly, 2025](https://osf.io/9j7qc_v2)).

The authors cannot answer: Festinger, Riecken and Schachter are dead. Other scholars have qualified the critique. The historian Aaron Gulyas told The Debrief that it was "an interesting take" but that "The focus on the 'proselytization' angle seems a bit narrow to me", and that Martin's and the Laugheads' "commitment to UFO belief deepened despite the failure of the 'prophecy'" ([The Debrief, 13 November 2025](https://thedebrief.org/alarm-bells-went-off-new-research-takes-a-critical-look-at-the-landmark-ufo-cult-study-when-prophecy-fails/)). Kelly himself says that Martin later suggested the promised rescue might have been "a spiritual uplifting" and later went on, as "Sister Thedra", to claim messages from Jesus and from extraterrestrials ([The Debrief, 2025](https://thedebrief.org/alarm-bells-went-off-new-research-takes-a-critical-look-at-the-landmark-ufo-cult-study-when-prophecy-fails/)). <Claim status="disputed" by="by Kelly (2025) against the 1956 account; qualified by Gulyas">Whether the Seekers doubled down after the failure</Claim> is now contested; that the observers intervened in the group they studied is documented in their own papers, according to Kelly. The study is a case where the researchers were themselves part of the influence: the techniques of covert manipulation they used are the subject of [social engineering: pretexting, vishing and baiting](/learn/social-engineering-pretexting-vishing-and-baiting).

## The Borboletas Azuis, Campina Grande, 1980

In Campina Grande, Paraíba, a cotton merchant named Roldão Mangueira de Figueiredo, born in 1905, founded the Casa de Caridade Jesus no Horto, mixing popular Catholicism with Kardecist spiritism; a seventeen-year-old member announced a purifying flood for 13 May 1980, after which a new world free of oppression would begin ([Mangueira, master's dissertation, Universidade Federal da Paraíba (UFPB), 2014](https://repositorio.ufpb.br/jspui/bitstream/tede/4248/1/arquivototal.pdf)). The members wore blue and white robes, and the town's mocking reaction gave them the name "Borboletas Azuis" ("Blue Butterflies"), which brought national press attention; by one researcher's count cited in the dissertation there were "setecentas pessoas" ("seven hundred people") among the faithful months before the date ([Mangueira, 2014](https://repositorio.ufpb.br/jspui/bitstream/tede/4248/1/arquivototal.pdf)).

After the flood failed to come, the dissertation records an exodus: disappointed followers left with carts of the food and belongings stored for the new era, and the number of members "cai exponencialmente" ("falls exponentially"). The remnant reinterpreted the failure: the new leader, supported by two women of the group, affirmed "que Deus mudara de ideia, instaurando uma nova oportunidade a humanidade desviada e corrompida" ("that God had changed his mind, granting a new opportunity to a strayed and corrupt humanity") ([Mangueira, 2014](https://repositorio.ufpb.br/jspui/bitstream/tede/4248/1/arquivototal.pdf)). The dissertation also records an earlier episode at the Açude Velho, the town's reservoir, where a crowd urged the leader to walk on the water and the police intervened to protect the members from a hail of mud ([Mangueira, 2014](https://repositorio.ufpb.br/jspui/bitstream/tede/4248/1/arquivototal.pdf)). The author dedicates the work to his grandfather, a son of the founder, which readers may weigh; his account rests on interviews with remaining members and on the newspapers of the time, including the Diário da Borborema. No court case was found: the Borboletas Azuis broke no law in the record, and their story is one of belief, ridicule and reinterpretation.

## Jonestown, 1978

### The deaths and the inquiry

On 18 November 1978, US Representative Leo Ryan was shot dead at the Port Kaituma airstrip in Guyana after visiting Jonestown, the agricultural settlement of the Peoples Temple, and that evening the mass deaths at Jonestown began. The House Committee on Foreign Affairs' staff report of 15 May 1979 states that the events "ultimately claimed 909 lives, including that of Jim Jones", and calls them a "mass suicide/murder" ([House Committee on Foreign Affairs, 1979](https://jonestown.sdsu.edu/?p=13674)). The Jonestown Institute at San Diego State University counts 918 dead in Guyana that day: 909 in Jonestown, five at the airstrip, including Ryan, three journalists and a defecting member, and four at the Temple's house in Georgetown; the toll "included 304 children and minors under 18", and different totals in circulation come from who is included and from early reports ([Jonestown Institute, How many people died on November 18?](https://jonestown.sdsu.edu/?p=35368); [Jonestown Institute, Why are there differences in the final death toll?](https://jonestown.sdsu.edu/?p=68414)). Its list of survivors who were in Guyana that day has 87 names ([Jonestown Institute, Who Survived the Jonestown Tragedy?](https://jonestown.sdsu.edu/?p=37978)).

The staff report concluded of Jones: "As we have studied him and interviewed those who knew him well and had come under his influence, we have concluded that he was first and foremost a master of mind control." It recorded that a member who defected in May 1978 had sworn an affidavit alleging "mass suicide rehearsals at Jonestown", that the US embassy had been aware that California foster children might be living there, and it recommended a "Concentrated Program of Research and Training on Cults", while observing that "Generalities, of course, are always difficult if not dangerous" ([House Committee on Foreign Affairs, 1979](https://jonestown.sdsu.edu/?p=13674)). The Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) holds some 48,000 pages: about 10,000 from its investigation, code-named RYMUR (for "Ryan murder"), and about 38,000 of the Temple's own records recovered in Jonestown; the Jonestown Institute obtained many through Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) requests and litigation, and the FBI still claims exemptions for many RYMUR records ([Jonestown Institute, The FBI and Peoples Temple](https://jonestown.sdsu.edu/?p=94902)).

The children and those who did not choose are why the House report spoke of "suicide/murder" and not of suicide alone. Survivors and relatives have written their own accounts, which the Jonestown Institute publishes alongside the official records; the label a reader chooses for the deaths is itself one of the open questions this part leaves to the reader.

## Waco, 1993

### What happened, and what the inquiries found

On 28 February 1993 agents of the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco and Firearms (ATF) tried to serve warrants at the Mount Carmel complex of the Branch Davidians near Waco, Texas; four agents and six Davidians died in the gunfight. After a 51-day standoff the complex burned on 19 April. The Special Counsel's report counts "at least 82" Davidian dead, including the six of 28 February but excluding two unborn children, and says the exact number cannot be stated with certainty "because of the extensive burning and commingling of bodies" ([Danforth, 2000](https://www.policinginstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/Danforth-Report-Waco-1993-Incident_11-8-2000.pdf)). During the standoff David Koresh, the leader, promised to come out after writing an interpretation of the Seven Seals of the Book of Revelation; the negotiators concluded that this "was another empty promise" because he "failed to turn over an interpretation of any of the Seven Seals" ([Danforth, 2000](https://www.policinginstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/Danforth-Report-Waco-1993-Incident_11-8-2000.pdf)).

The first Justice Department evaluation, by Edward Dennis Jr., was delivered on 8 October 1993 ([Dennis, Evaluation of the Handling of the Branch Davidian Stand-off in Waco, Texas, 8 October 1993, NCJRS copy](https://www.ojp.gov/pdffiles1/Digitization/145687NCJRS.pdf)). Ammerman, one of the outside experts consulted, criticized the ATF for relying on "angry ex-members and eager deprogrammers", wrote that "people within the 'anti-cult' community had targeted the Branch Davidians for attention", and found that "Pressure from encroaching tanks, psychological warfare tactics, and the like, continually worked at cross-purposes with the negotiating strategies" ([Ammerman, 1993](https://hirr.hartfordinternational.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Report-to-the-Justice-and-Treasury-Departments.pdf)).

In 1999, after it emerged that the FBI had fired pyrotechnic tear gas rounds on the last day, contrary to six years of denials, Attorney General Janet Reno appointed former Senator John Danforth as special counsel ([CBS News, 21 July 2000](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/independent-waco-probe-clears-feds)). His investigation lasted 14 months, interviewed "exactly 1,001 witnesses" and reviewed over 2.3 million pages; its final report of 8 November 2000 states "with certainty" that the government "did not cause the fire", "did not direct gunfire at the Branch Davidian complex" and "did not improperly employ the armed forces", and that "Responsibility for the tragedy of Waco rests with certain of the Branch Davidians and their leader", who, the report says, spread fuel and lit the fire and killed some of their own people by gunfire, "including at least five children" ([Danforth, 2000](https://www.policinginstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/Danforth-Report-Waco-1993-Incident_11-8-2000.pdf)). The same report found that FBI and Justice Department officials had failed to disclose the use of the pyrotechnic rounds until August 1999, and that this "has greatly undermined public confidence in government" ([Danforth, 2000](https://www.policinginstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/Danforth-Report-Waco-1993-Incident_11-8-2000.pdf)).

### The courts and the Davidians' side

In the criminal trial, the jury on 26 February 1994 acquitted the Davidian defendants of conspiracy and murder, convicted five of the lesser offence of manslaughter and several on firearms counts, and acquitted three of all charges; in June 2000 the Supreme Court sent the firearms sentences back for resentencing ([Danforth, 2000](https://www.policinginstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/Danforth-Report-Waco-1993-Incident_11-8-2000.pdf)). In the civil suit brought by survivors and relatives, an advisory jury found on 14 July 2000 that the plaintiffs had not proved excessive force by the ATF or negligence by the FBI, and the court agreed in September 2000 ([Danforth, 2000](https://www.policinginstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/Danforth-Report-Waco-1993-Incident_11-8-2000.pdf)). The Davidians' lawyers did not accept the outcome: Jack Zimmerman said "You've got all these unanswered questions that are going to linger ... The judge didn't let all the evidence in; the jury didn't hear everything", and Dick DeGuerin, Koresh's lawyer, said "This case shouldn't have been tried in Waco" ([Dallas Morning News, 15 July 2000, CESNUR copy](https://www.cesnur.org/testi/waco107.htm)). An attorney for the survivors called the special counsel's re-enactment of the infrared footage into question ([CBS News, 21 July 2000](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/independent-waco-probe-clears-feds)).

## Heaven's Gate, 1997

On 26 March 1997 sheriff's deputies found 39 members of Heaven's Gate dead in a house in Rancho Santa Fe, near San Diego; the group, led by Marshall Applewhite, had taught that the comet Hale-Bopp was a sign to leave Earth, and members recorded farewell videos ([NBC San Diego, 26 March 2017](https://www.nbcsandiego.com/news/local/Heavens-Gate-Mass-Suicide-20-Years-Later-417138623.html); [Deseret News, Associated Press report, 28 March 1997](https://www.deseret.com/1997/3/28/19303405/video-offers-a-chilling-look-at-suicide-scene/)). The two press accounts disagree on the composition of the dead, "21 men and 18 women" in the 2017 account and "the 21 women and 18 men" in the report of 1997 ([NBC San Diego, 2017](https://www.nbcsandiego.com/news/local/Heavens-Gate-Mass-Suicide-20-Years-Later-417138623.html); [Deseret News, 1997](https://www.deseret.com/1997/3/28/19303405/video-offers-a-chilling-look-at-suicide-scene/)); the official records were not found for this part. The group had posted on its website that "Hale-Bopp's approach is the 'marker' we've been waiting for" ([Deseret News, 1997](https://www.deseret.com/1997/3/28/19303405/video-offers-a-chilling-look-at-suicide-scene/)). A retired detective who was first on the scene said visitors to the sheriff's museum still "express shock or sadness about how people could do this to themselves" ([NBC San Diego, 2017](https://www.nbcsandiego.com/news/local/Heavens-Gate-Mass-Suicide-20-Years-Later-417138623.html)).

## The brainwashing dispute

### Singer's side

The idea that groups can take over a mind came from the Cold War studies of Chinese "thought reform", which the DIMPAC report cites, describing "the subjugation of an entire nation to a thought reform program which induced millions to espouse new philosophies" ([DIMPAC, 1986](https://www.cesnur.org/testi/DIMPAC.htm)). The report argued that totalist cults differ from "groups that are merely unorthodox or innovative" in their use of manipulative techniques, and that "Cultic groups have caused concern because they tend, unlike psychotherapy, to be totalist in character and to exploit rather than fulfill needs" ([DIMPAC, 1986](https://www.cesnur.org/testi/DIMPAC.htm)). Singer testified as an expert in many lawsuits brought by former members against groups.

### The APA's response

On 11 May 1987 the APA's Board of Social and Ethical Responsibility for Psychology (BSERP) told the task force that it was "unable to accept the report", that "the report lacks the scientific rigor and evenhanded critical approach necessary for APA imprimatur", and asked members not to distribute it "without indicating that the report was unacceptable to the Board". It added that "BSERP does not believe that we have sufficient information available to guide us in taking a position on this issue" ([APA, BSERP memorandum, 11 May 1987, CESNUR copy](https://www.cesnur.org/testi/APA.htm)). One of the external reviewers enclosed with the memorandum wrote: "The term 'brainwashing' is not a recognized theoretical concept" ([APA, 1987](https://www.cesnur.org/testi/APA.htm)). The board rejected a report; it did not adopt the opposite position.

Earlier that year the APA had joined an amicus brief in a California case brought by former members of the Unification Church, Molko against the Holy Spirit Association, arguing that the coercive-persuasion theory was not accepted science. The brief cited studies in which "fewer than one in ten of those who got as far as attending a Church workshop agree to join the Church, and fewer than one in twenty remain with the Church for two years", including Eileen Barker's The Making of a Moonie (1984) ([APA and others, amicus brief in Molko v. Holy Spirit Association, February 1987, CESNUR copy](https://cesnur.org/testi/molko_brief.htm)). The APA later withdrew its signature; a later APA letter, quoted in the CESNUR edition, says that "APA's decision to withdraw from this case was based on procedural as opposed to substantive concerns" ([CESNUR, Molko brief](https://cesnur.org/testi/molko_brief.htm)).

### The courts

In 1990 a federal court in California excluded Singer's and Richard Ofshe's testimony on thought reform in a criminal case, United States v. Fishman, finding "no consensus or general acceptance" of their theory in the professional associations and that "the APA ultimately rejected the Singer task force report on coercive persuasion"; Singer could testify on the defendant's mental state, but "cannot support her opinion with testimony that involves thought reform" ([United States v. Fishman, 743 F. Supp. 713, Northern District of California, 1990, CourtListener](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2593631/united-states-v-fishman/)). The dispute moved from labs to courtrooms and stayed there: the ruling did not deny that groups can harm members, only that one theory of how they do it met the legal test for scientific evidence.

<Claim status="disputed" by="by Singer and DIMPAC against the BSERP, the APA's amicus brief and later courts">That groups can override a member's will by "brainwashing"</Claim> is not accepted as science; <Claim status="established" by="by official inquiries and labour inspectors cited below">that groups can isolate, deceive, exploit and harm members</Claim> is documented case by case.

## Coercive control: from the courtroom to the statute book

The language of the law has moved from "brainwashing" to coercion and control. In England and Wales, section 76 of the Serious Crime Act 2015 made it an offence to repeatedly or continuously engage in behaviour towards another person "that is controlling or coercive" in an intimate or family relationship, where it has "a serious effect" on the victim ([Serious Crime Act 2015, section 76, legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/9/section/76)). In Brazil, Law 14,188 of 28 July 2021 added article 147-B to the Penal Code, punishing with six months to two years of imprisonment whoever causes a woman emotional harm by acts that aim "a degradar ou a controlar suas ações, comportamentos, crenças e decisões, mediante ameaça, constrangimento, humilhação, manipulação, isolamento" ("to degrade or control her actions, behaviour, beliefs and decisions, by threat, constraint, humiliation, manipulation, isolation") and other means ([Senado Federal, Lei nº 14.188, de 28 de julho de 2021](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/34628171/publicacao/34628407)). Both laws concern intimate or family relationships, not religious groups, but they name the same behaviours that the DIMPAC report listed for cults, and they put the question to courts as facts to prove, not as a theory of the mind.

## Brazilian cases from the record

### A church, its farms and 565 workers

In Minas Gerais the Federal Police (PF) of Varginha indicted 43 people linked to the "Comunidade Evangélica Jesus, a Verdade que Marca" in February 2016, after almost five years of investigation, for fraud, money laundering and slave-like labour; members had been recruited at a church in São Paulo and persuaded to live and work on farms in Minas Gerais, "doando tudo o que tinham para a seita" ("giving everything they had to the sect"); assets worth R$ 100 million were frozen, and the defence lawyers denied their clients' guilt and said they had not been notified of the inquiry ([Tribuna do Paraná, 17 February 2016](https://www.tribunapr.com.br/noticias/brasil/pf-indicia-43-da-seita-evangelica-jesus-a-verdade-que-marca/)). In March 2018, in Operation Canaã, the Ministry of Labour fined a company that used the Igreja Cristã Traduzindo o Verbo for keeping 565 workers in conditions analogous to slavery on nine farms in Minas Gerais and Bahia and in businesses in São Paulo; 438 had no registered work card and 32 were adolescents; the workers received no pay, only housing and food, and the Federal Police had arrested 13 leaders on a federal court's order ([Correio Braziliense, Blog do Servidor, 15 March 2018](https://blogs.correiobraziliense.com.br/servidor/ministerio-do-trabalho-autua-empresa-ligada-igreja-por-manter-565-trabalhadores-em-condicao-analoga-escravidao/)).

What made the case different was the workers' own view. The operation's coordinator, the labour inspector Marcelo Campos, said that "as vítimas não se achavam exploradas e diziam trabalhar em nome da fé e da coletividade" ("the victims did not consider themselves exploited and said they worked in the name of faith and the community"), and that, unlike in other operations, "Eles achavam que nós éramos demônios que os estavam retirando de sua missão e não concordaram em deixar os locais" ("They thought we were demons taking them away from their mission, and they did not agree to leave") ([Correio Braziliense, 2018](https://blogs.correiobraziliense.com.br/servidor/ministerio-do-trabalho-autua-empresa-ligada-igreja-por-manter-565-trabalhadores-em-condicao-analoga-escravidao/)). The final outcome of the criminal cases was not found for this part; the leaders are presumed innocent until a final judgment, and the findings above are those of the police and the labour inspectors, disputed by the defence.

### Altamira: an acquittal and convictions

In Altamira, Pará, five boys were attacked and some killed between 1989 and 1993 in crimes that prosecutors linked to "magia negra e rituais satânicos" ("black magic and satanic rituals") and to a sect, the Lineamento Universal Superior ([Supremo Tribunal Federal (STF), news of 16 September 2003, RHC 83178](https://noticias.stf.jus.br/?p=4274)). The case shows how belief about a sect can drive a prosecution and how courts test it. The alleged leader's defence argued before the STF that the testimony naming her was "fantasiosa" ("fanciful") and that the witness had recognized her from a photograph in a news magazine ([STF, news of 11 June 2003](https://noticias.stf.jus.br/?p=3632)); the Court's Second Panel unanimously denied her appeal on 16 September 2003 ([STF, 16 September 2003](https://noticias.stf.jus.br/?p=4274)). In December 2003, after a 17-day trial in Belém, a jury acquitted her by six votes to one, after the defence presented a police document indicating that she was not in Altamira at the time of the crimes; four other defendants, among them two doctors, were sentenced to terms of 35 to 77 years ([Al Jazeera, 6 December 2003](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2003/12/6/brazil-mutilating-woman-freed)). One of the convicted, a businessman sentenced to 57 years, argued in a habeas corpus that the case was "o maior erro judiciário da Justiça brasileira" ("the greatest judicial error of Brazilian justice") and attributed the crimes to another man ([STF, 7 December 2004, HC 85223](https://noticias.stf.jus.br/?p=6931)); a doctor among the convicted was allowed to remain free until a final judgment ([STF, 25 November 2005, HC 87236](https://noticias.stf.jus.br/?p=9011)). Accusations of satanic crime and the false memories that can feed them are the subject of the part on [the Satanic panic, false memories and the Escola Base case](/learn/satanic-panic-false-memories-and-the-escola-base-case).

## Who steered, and who did not

In these cases someone usually did steer: a leader who set the date, controlled what members heard and, in Jonestown and Waco, decided matters of life and death, according to the official inquiries. But the record also shows influence from outside the group. Researchers in the 1954 group intervened, by their own notes as Kelly reads them; at Waco, the outside experts' report found that informants with "a direct ideological (and financial) interest in arousing suspicion" had shaped the government's view, and that psychological pressure undercut the negotiations ([Ammerman, 1993](https://hirr.hartfordinternational.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Report-to-the-Justice-and-Treasury-Departments.pdf)); and in the press of 1844 and 1980, ridicule pushed believers further inside. The techniques of isolation, repetition and controlled information are the same that the [psychological operations series](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign) maps for states and fraudsters: see [the human attack surface](/learn/the-human-attack-surface-why-repetition-works) for repetition, [why misbeliefs persist](/learn/why-misbeliefs-persist) for belief that outlives correction, and [prebunking and inoculation](/learn/prebunking-and-inoculation) for what can be done in advance. The group dynamics underneath are in [conformity, cascades and contagion](/learn/conformity-cascades-and-contagion-the-mechanisms).

## What to remember

- "Cult" and "brainwashing" are contested words: the APA rejected Singer's task-force report in 1987 while declining to take a position, and a federal court in 1990 excluded thought-reform testimony as lacking general acceptance.
- The Millerites expected Christ on 22 October 1844; Miller confessed error but kept his faith, Edson moved the event to heaven, and Adventist churches grew from the reinterpretation. Asylum figures and ascension-robe stories of the time are attributions and satire, not verified facts.
- When Prophecy Fails (1956) founded the study of cognitive dissonance; later reviews found that most groups survive failure but few seek converts, and a 2025 archival study argues that the group did not double down and that the researchers manipulated it.
- In Campina Grande the Borboletas Azuis awaited a flood on 13 May 1980; most left, and those who stayed said God had changed his mind.
- Jonestown: 909 dead in the settlement, 918 in Guyana that day, 304 of them minors; the House inquiry called it "mass suicide/murder".
- Waco: four agents and at least 82 Davidians dead; the special counsel cleared the government of causing the fire but found that officials hid the pyrotechnic rounds for six years; the Davidians' lawyers rejected the outcome.
- In Brazil, labour inspectors found 565 workers in slave-like conditions in a church's businesses, workers who said they served in the name of faith; in Altamira a jury acquitted the alleged sect leader and convicted four others.
