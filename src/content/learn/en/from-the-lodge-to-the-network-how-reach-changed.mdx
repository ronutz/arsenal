The title borrows an old image: the lodge, a closed circle whose members meet, vouch for one another and pass the word along. For most of the twentieth century a message reached a large audience in one of two ways. Someone who owned a press, a transmitter or a mailing list sent it out, and people who were trusted in their own circles relayed it to those who had not heard. Networked platforms changed who can broadcast, what it costs and how the relaying happens, and messaging apps put a forward button inside the circle itself. This part of the series opened by [the map from the small lie to the campaign](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign) sets out what the research on reach has measured: the broadcast era and its people in between, the price of reach on platforms, what diffusion studies found about cascades and broadcasts, the best-known study of true and false news and the reanalysis that challenged it, and what forwarding limits on WhatsApp did in Brazil and India. The earlier history of radio and print propaganda is in [the short history of propaganda](/learn/a-short-history-of-propaganda-and-psychological-warfare); fake accounts and bought engagement are in [the part on bots, sockpuppets and astroturfing](/learn/bots-sockpuppets-and-astroturfing); the 2018 Brazilian mass-messaging cases are in [the part on Brazil's courts and Congress](/learn/brazil-mass-messaging-the-courts-and-the-cpmi). Whether networks also close people inside like-minded circles is the subject of [the next part, on echo chambers and filter bubbles](/learn/echo-chambers-and-filter-bubbles-the-evidence-and-the-dispute).

## Before the network: broadcasts and the people in between

### Opinion leaders: the lodge in sociological terms

Early research on mass media and voting proposed that broadcasts reached much of their audience only indirectly, through conversation with other people, a hypothesis that David Yanagizawa-Drott traces to work by Lazarsfeld and colleagues in 1944 and by Katz and Lazarsfeld in 1955 ([Yanagizawa-Drott, Quarterly Journal of Economics, August 2014, author's copy](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037d-4eed-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content)). A 1957 review in Public Opinion Quarterly by Elihu Katz, a former staff member of Columbia University's Bureau of Applied Social Research, tested across several successive studies the hypothesis that "ideas often flow from radio and print to opinion leaders and from these to the less active sections of the population", and reported that it was largely supported and considerably refined ([The Two-Step Flow of Communication: An Up-To-Date Report on an Hypothesis, Public Opinion Quarterly, 1957, repository record](https://repository.upenn.edu/asc_papers/271); [Crossref record](https://doi.org/10.1086/266687)). Elizabeth Dubois and Grant Blank summarize the figure at its centre: "Katz and Lazarsfeld famously identified the opinion leader who is found in every social stratum and who is an above average consumer of news media (1955)", who "shares political information and opinions with their everyday associates who are normally less interested in politics and who normally consume less news" ([Dubois and Blank, The echo chamber is overstated, Information, Communication & Society, 2018, accepted manuscript in Oxford's repository](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A34c4d33f-7cee-4fc0-aaad-0ce5311ac0de/files/m988c6e898fd3b881e39fb291545f004e)). The model is known as the two-step flow, and its lesson recurs throughout this part: reach and influence are not the same thing, and who passes a message on can matter as much as who sent it.

Some commentators read platforms as undoing that lesson. Sacha Altay, Manon Berriche and Alberto Acerbi quote the observation that "we might see the role of Facebook and other social media platforms as returning us to a pre-Katz and Lazarsfeld era", with fears of direct injection of disinformation into the public, and reply that "These premises are at odds with what we know about human psychology" ([Altay, Berriche and Acerbi, Misinformation on Misinformation, Social Media + Society, January to March 2023](https://iris.unitn.it/bitstream/11572/361723/6/altay-et-al-2023-misinformation-on-misinformation-conceptual-and-methodological-challenges.pdf)).

### Radio: measured effects, and a dispute about how large

Two economic studies used differences in radio reception to estimate what broadcasts did. Maja Adena, Ruben Enikolopov, Maria Petrova, Veronica Santarosa and Ekaterina Zhuravskaya studied Germany in the 1920s and 1930s and found that while the Weimar government aired pro-government political news, the growth of Nazi popularity slowed in areas with access to radio; that the effect reversed after Hitler's appointment as chancellor; that radio propaganda then helped the Nazis enrol party members and incited anti-Semitic acts and denunciations; and that <Claim status="reported" by="by its authors">"Nazi radio was most effective in places where anti-Semitism was historically high and had a negative effect in places with historically low anti-Semitism"</Claim> ([Adena et al., Radio and the Rise of the Nazis in Prewar Germany, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2015, abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:130:y:2015:i:4:p:1885-1939)). The broadcast worked on predispositions already present, not on a blank audience.

David Yanagizawa-Drott used village-level data from the 1994 Rwandan genocide to estimate the effect of Radio Télévision Libre des Mille Collines (RTLM), which encouraged violence against the Tutsi minority. He estimated that <Claim status="reported" by="by its author">"An estimated 51,000 perpetrators, or approximately 10 percent of the overall violence, can be attributed to the station"</Claim>, and that the broadcasts increased militia violence not only in villages with reception but also in neighbouring villages, "spillovers" that "are estimated to have caused more militia violence than the direct effects" ([Yanagizawa-Drott, Propaganda and Conflict: Evidence from the Rwandan Genocide, Quarterly Journal of Economics, August 2014, author's copy](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037d-4eed-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content)). His conclusion joins the two halves of the old model: mass media "can affect participation in violence directly due to exposure, and indirectly due to social interactions", and he traces to Katz and Lazarsfeld's 1955 work the emphasis on "local opinion leaders" in "intermediating the effects of mass media" (same). Seven years earlier Scott Straus, analysing exposure, timing and content and interviewing perpetrators, reached a different reading: his article "refutes the conventional wisdom that broadcasts from the notorious radio station RTLM were a primary determinant of genocide" and finds "evidence of conditional media effects, which take on significance only when situated in a broader context of violence" ([Straus, What Is the Relationship between Hate Radio and Violence?, Politics & Society 35(4), 2007, abstract in a citation catalogue](https://catalog.ihsn.org/citations/47633)). <Claim status="disputed">How much the station itself caused</Claim> therefore has two answers on the record: an earlier study of exposure, timing and testimony that found only conditional effects, and a later village-level estimate that attributes about a tenth of the violence to the broadcasts and their spillovers.

### Mass contact: reach is not persuasion

The direct mailing, the phone bank and the television spot were the broadcast tools of election campaigns. Joshua Kalla and David Broockman pooled 40 field experiments and added nine of their own and concluded that <Claim status="reported" by="by its authors">"the best estimate of the effects of campaign contact and advertising on Americans' candidates choices in general elections is zero"</Claim>; persuasion appeared only in two rare circumstances, when candidates took unusually unpopular positions and campaigns invested heavily in finding persuadable voters, or when contact came long before election day and was measured immediately, an effect that decayed ([Kalla and Broockman, The Minimal Persuasive Effects of Campaign Contact in General Elections, American Political Science Review 112(1), published online September 2017](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/minimal-persuasive-effects-of-campaign-contact-in-general-elections-evidence-from-49-field-experiments/753665A313C4AB433DBF7110299B7433)). Their finding concerns United States (US) general elections and vote choice, not turnout, primaries or other countries.

### Chain letters: person-to-person mail before platforms

Before reshare buttons, person-to-person spread could be traced in chain letters. David Liben-Nowell and Jon Kleinberg reconstructed the paths of petitions that circulated by email, each recipient adding a name and forwarding the list, and found that rather than fanning out widely in a few steps, "the progress of these chain letters proceeds in a narrow but very deep tree-like pattern, continuing for several hundred steps" ([Liben-Nowell and Kleinberg, Tracing information flow on a global scale using Internet chain-letter data, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 19 March 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2290760/)). The tree they rebuilt from a petition against the start of the war in Iraq had 18,119 nodes, of which 17,079 (94.26%) had exactly one child, with a median depth of 288. A message passed on by hand, in that case, travelled far by going person to person, slowly and in a thin line.

### Brazil: reach rationed by law

In Brazil, broadcast reach in elections is rationed by law. On 23 August 2018 the electoral court, the Tribunal Superior Eleitoral (TSE), presented the time each presidential candidate would have in the free electoral broadcasts on radio and television, which ran in two daily blocks from 1 September to 4 October; the division follows the size of the parties of each coalition in the Chamber of Deputies, and the number of 30-second spots follows the same rule ([O Imparcial, republishing Agência Brasil, 23 August 2018](https://oimparcial.com.br/politica/2018/08/tse-divulgou-hoje-o-tempo-de-cada-candidato-na-campanha-de-tv-e-radio/)). Geraldo Alckmin's nine-party coalition received five minutes and 32 seconds per block and 434 spots; Jair Bolsonaro's two-party coalition received eight seconds and 11 spots (same). The Jornal do Brasil put Alckmin's daily exposure at 11 minutes and 4 seconds across the two blocks ([Jornal do Brasil, 24 August 2018](https://www.jb.com.br/pais/eleicoes_2018/2018/08/376-tse-divulga-a-fatia-de-cada-coligacao-e-legendas-de-direita-ficam-com-73-do-tempo-na-tv-e-no-radio.html)). The rule is article 47 of the elections law, Lei 9.504/1997: in the wording in force in 2018, the reserved time went "90% (noventa por cento) distribuídos proporcionalmente ao número de representantes na Câmara dos Deputados" (90% in proportion to seats in the Chamber of Deputies) and "10% (dez por cento) distribuídos igualitariamente" (10% equally) ([Lei 9.504/1997, art. 47, § 2º](https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/leis/l9504.htm)).

Bolsonaro won. The day after the run-off, O Estado de S. Paulo wrote that he had overcome "o pouco tempo de TV no primeiro turno (apenas oito segundos)", the scant television time of the first round, a single allied party and a campaign with few resources, and that in the week of the first round he led interactions on Facebook with more than five million ([O Estado de S. Paulo, 29 October 2018, Senate press clipping archive](https://www2.senado.leg.br/jspui/bitstream/id/551206/1/noticia.html)). The same report gave competing explanations. The political scientist Kleber Carrilho said Bolsonaro's rise "põe em xeque a efetividade da propaganda eleitoral" in the paper's paraphrase, calling broadcast propaganda's effectiveness into question; Vera Chaia, of the Pontifical Catholic University of São Paulo, said "O que mais pesou foi ser contrário ao PT", what weighed most was being against the Workers' Party (Partido dos Trabalhadores, PT). The report also recalls that after the stabbing he suffered in Juiz de Fora he campaigned from home (same). <Claim status="disputed">How much of the result is owed to networks rather than to opposition to the PT, the attack or other factors</Claim> is argued, not measured, in the sources read here ([O Estado de S. Paulo, 29 October 2018](https://www2.senado.leg.br/jspui/bitstream/id/551206/1/noticia.html)).

## What a platform changes: the price and the carrier of reach

### Ads from fifty cents a day, and the shares that follow

On platforms the price of a first broadcast fell to almost nothing. Facebook's general counsel, Colin Stretch, told a US Senate subcommittee on 31 October 2017 that "Advertisers can serve ads on our platform for as little as $0.50 per day" ([Testimony of Colin Stretch, Senate Judiciary Subcommittee on Crime and Terrorism, 31 October 2017](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/10-31-17%20Stretch%20Testimony.pdf)). He then set out what the Russian Internet Research Agency (IRA) had done with those tools. In Facebook's account, <Claim status="attributed" by="by Facebook">fake accounts associated with the IRA spent approximately $100,000 on more than 3,000 Facebook and Instagram ads between June 2015 and August 2017, promoting about 120 Pages that posted more than 80,000 pieces of content</Claim>; an estimated 11.4 million people in the US saw at least one ad; for half of the ads less than $3 was spent; and about a quarter of the ads were never shown to anyone.

The paid reach was the smaller part. Stretch estimated that about 29 million people were served content directly from the IRA's posts, that because posts were shared, liked and followed "three times more people may have been exposed", and that "approximately 126 million people may have been served content from a Page associated with the IRA at some point during the two-year period", which he put at "about four-thousandths of one percent (0.004%) of content in News Feed, or approximately 1 out of 23,000 pieces of content" (same). Facebook's first public account, in September 2017, had described about $100,000 in ad spending connected to about 470 inauthentic accounts and Pages that "likely operated out of Russia" ([Facebook, Alex Stamos, An Update On Information Operations On Facebook, 6 September 2017](https://about.fb.com/news/2017/09/information-operations-update/)). The attributions and the denials are set out in [the part on state campaigns](/learn/state-influence-campaigns-on-the-record). For the question of reach, the record shows two things at once: organic sharing multiplied a small budget, and the result was still a minute share of what users saw. Whether that exposure changed attitudes or votes is a separate question, and [the part on bots](/learn/bots-sockpuppets-and-astroturfing) reports a study that found no measurable relationship.

### A price list for messages

Messaging apps had their own market. On 18 October 2018 the Folha de S.Paulo reported that companies were buying packages of mass WhatsApp messages against the Workers' Party, with contracts of up to R$12 million, and that <Claim status="reported" by="by the Folha de S.Paulo">"Os preços variam de R$ 0,08 a R$ 0,12 por disparo de mensagem para a base própria do candidato e de R$ 0,30 a R$ 0,40 quando a base é fornecida pela agência"</Claim>, prices of R$0.08 to R$0.12 per message to the candidate's own list and R$0.30 to R$0.40 when the agency supplied the list ([Folha de S.Paulo, 18 October 2018](https://www1.folha.uol.com.br/poder/2018/10/empresarios-bancam-campanha-contra-o-pt-pelo-whatsapp.shtml)). The denials of those named, the electoral court's findings and the rules that followed are in [the part on Brazil's courts and Congress](/learn/brazil-mass-messaging-the-courts-and-the-cpmi); the market for bought followers, likes and views is priced in [the part on bots](/learn/bots-sockpuppets-and-astroturfing).

### One account, three hundred million views

Reach on a platform does not need fake accounts or paid messages when an account already has a large following and the platform's recommendations add more. In January 2025 a federal deputy, Nikolas Ferreira of the Partido Liberal (PL), posted a video about new rules of the Federal Revenue Service on the reporting of financial transactions, including Pix transfers. The fact-checking outlet Aos Fatos reported that the recording "atingiu quase 300 milhões de visualizações em dois dias", nearly 300 million views in two days, and that in it Ferreira says expressly that "o Pix não será taxado", Pix will not be taxed, then adds "Mas não duvido que possa ser", that he would not doubt it could be ([Aos Fatos, 16 January 2025](https://www.aosfatos.org/noticias/video-nikolas-ferreira-pix/)). Ferreira told a television news programme, as Aos Fatos quotes him, that he had made it "bem claro que o Pix não será taxado", very clear that Pix would not be taxed.

On 15 January 2025 the government revoked the rule. The Finance Minister, Fernando Haddad, said "É para evitar judicialização e mentiras que a Receita Federal está tomando a atitude de revogar", it is to avoid litigation and lies that the Revenue Service is revoking it ([InfoMoney, 15 January 2025](https://www.infomoney.com.br/politica/pix-governo-lula-recua-e-revogara-norma-da-receita-sobre-movimentacao-financeira/)). Ferreira answered on the news channel CNN Brasil the same day: "Se era fake news, por que revogou?", if it was fake news, why did they revoke it? ([CNN Brasil, 15 January 2025](https://www.cnnbrasil.com.br/politica/se-era-fake-news-por-que-revogou-diz-nikolas-ferreira-a-cnn/)).

Aos Fatos offered three possible explanations for the reach: false posts about taxing Pix were already circulating; the newspaper O Globo had reported that a campaign strategist helped define the approach, and the strategist, Duda Lima, told CNN Brasil, as Aos Fatos quotes him, that "Ninguém precisa inventar nada", nobody needs to invent anything, and that he was retired from election campaigns; and Meta had announced on 7 January the return of political content to recommendations, so the video might have been shown to people who did not follow the deputy. <Claim status="reported" by="by Aos Fatos">Asked by Aos Fatos, Meta neither confirmed nor denied whether it was already recommending political content to Brazilian users when the video spread</Claim> ([Aos Fatos, 16 January 2025](https://www.aosfatos.org/noticias/video-nikolas-ferreira-pix/)). The case is a broadcast, not a chain: one account, a following of millions and a recommendation system, with the political argument over who misled whom recorded on both sides.

## Cascades and broadcasts: what the diffusion studies found

### Most things do not spread at all

When a post is reshared and the reshares are reshared, the tree of who got it from whom is called a cascade. Sharad Goel, Duncan Watts and Daniel Goldstein, then at Yahoo! Research, mapped cascades in seven online domains, from communication platforms to networked games, and found "strikingly similar patterns across all domains": depending on the domain, from 73% to 95% of the items they followed showed "no diffusion at all", and "less than 1% of cascades consist of more than seven nodes, and less than 4% extend further than one degree from the seed node" ([Goel, Watts and Goldstein, The Structure of Online Diffusion Networks, 2012](https://5harad.com/papers/diffusion.pdf)). Even for the largest cascades, they wrote, "the bulk of adoptions often takes place within one degree of a few dominant individuals".

### Structural virality: telling a broadcast from a chain

Goel, Ashton Anderson, Jake Hofman and Watts then proposed a measure for the difference between the two old modes of reach. Their "structural virality" "interpolates between two conceptual extremes: content that gains its popularity through a single, large broadcast and that which grows through multiple generations with any one individual directly responsible for only a fraction of the total adoption"; technically, it is the average distance between all pairs of nodes in the cascade tree ([Goel et al., The Structural Virality of Online Diffusion, Management Science 62(1), published online July 2015](https://5harad.com/papers/twiral.pdf)). Analysing about a billion diffusion events on Twitter, news stories, videos, images and petitions, they found "surprising structural diversity", but that structural virality "is typically low, and remains so independent of size, suggesting that popularity is largely driven by the size of the largest broadcast". Even the largest image and video cascades, of 10,000 reposts or more, had a median structural virality below 3, "barely more than the theoretical minimum of 2"; petitions spread most like chains, with medians between 7 and 8. Viral hits, they estimated, appear at a rate "closer to one in a million".

The phrase "going viral" suggests person-to-person contagion. On the data these studies used, what made something popular was mostly a big account broadcasting it, with chains as the exception.

### Popularity is hard to predict

Social influence also makes popularity less predictable. Salganik, Dodds and Watts built an artificial music market in which 14,341 participants downloaded unknown songs with or without seeing what others had chosen, and found that "Increasing the strength of social influence increased both inequality and unpredictability of success", with quality only partly deciding the outcome: "The best songs rarely did poorly, and the worst rarely did well, but any other result was possible" ([Salganik, Dodds and Watts, Science, February 2006, abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:%2210.1126/science.1121066%22&format=json&resultType=core)). For campaigns this cuts both ways: a small push can tip an outcome, and the same push can come to nothing.

## True and false news: the Vosoughi study and its critics

### What the 2018 study found

The most cited study on how falsehood travels is by Soroush Vosoughi, Deb Roy and Sinan Aral, of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT). They collected ~126,000 rumour cascades "spread by ~3 million people more than 4.5 million times" on Twitter from 2006 to 2017, taking every rumour investigated by six fact-checking organizations whose verdicts agreed "between 95 and 98% of the time" ([Vosoughi, Roy and Aral, The spread of true and false news online, Science 359, 9 March 2018](https://politics.media.mit.edu/papers/Vosoughi_Science.pdf)). Their headline: <Claim status="disputed" by="by Juul and Ugander">"falsehood diffused significantly farther, faster, deeper, and more broadly than the truth in all categories of information"</Claim>. The top 1% of false cascades "routinely diffused to between 1000 and 100,000 people", while the truth rarely reached more than 1,000; "It took the truth about six times as long as falsehood to reach 1500 people"; and falsehoods were 70% more likely to be retweeted, even controlling for the accounts' age, activity and followers. Politics was the largest category, with ~45,000 cascades, and false political news travelled deeper and more broadly than other false news.

The authors looked for explanations in the users and found the opposite of what one might expect: those who spread false news had fewer followers, followed fewer people and were less active. They found false news more novel than true news, and replies to false stories expressing more fear, disgust and surprise. They also addressed their sample: "a selection bias may arise from the restriction of our sample to tweets fact checked by the six organizations", so they had a second sample of rumours, not checked by any fact-checker, verified by three undergraduates, and report "results nearly identical to those estimated with our main data set". Their analysis of automated accounts, which found that bots spread true and false news at the same rate, is described in [the part on bots](/learn/bots-sockpuppets-and-astroturfing).

### The reanalysis: matching cascades by size

Jonas Juul and Johan Ugander reanalysed both the Vosoughi data and the Goel data with a simple control: compare cascades of the same size. For true and false news, they wrote, "the reported structural differences can almost entirely be explained by false-news cascades being larger"; for videos, images, news and petitions, "structural differences persist when controlling for size" ([Juul and Ugander, Comparing information diffusion mechanisms by matching on cascade size, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 118(46), 8 November 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8609637/)). A model trained to predict whether a size-matched cascade was true or false from its depth, breadth and virality did no better than random guessing. Differences between false political news and other false news also disappeared once size was matched.

Their reading is not that false news is harmless. It is that the mechanism appears the same and the difference is one of degree: <Claim status="reported" by="by Juul and Ugander">their findings "are consistent with the mechanisms underlying true- and false-news diffusion being quite similar, differing primarily in the basic infectiousness of their spreading process"</Claim>, which, they argue, means that reducing the person-to-person "infectiousness" of false information could limit its depth and breadth as well. They state their own limits: the theory applies to idealized processes, and fact-checked true news is plausibly more like fact-checked false news than true news in general.

### What the sample covers

Altay, Berriche and Acerbi add a point about scope: "Vosoughi and his colleagues did not examine the spread of true and false news online but of 'contested news' that fact-checkers classified as either true or false, leaving aside a large number of uncontested news that is extremely viral", a bias the authors themselves publicly explained, but "journalists and scientists quickly overgeneralized, and nuances were lost" ([Altay, Berriche and Acerbi, Misinformation on Misinformation, Social Media + Society, January to March 2023](https://iris.unitn.it/bitstream/11572/361723/6/altay-et-al-2023-misinformation-on-misinformation-conceptual-and-methodological-challenges.pdf)). They add that "This is not to say that the Vosoughi et al. findings on fact-checked news do not hold".

| Claim | What the sources read here support |
|---|---|
| False rumours on Twitter reached larger audiences than true ones in the fact-checked sample | Found by Vosoughi, Roy and Aral; accepted by the reanalysis |
| False news spreads through a different, more viral mechanism | Disputed: Juul and Ugander find the structural differences almost vanish when cascades of equal size are compared |
| The result describes all news online | Not supported: the sample was rumours that fact-checkers investigated, as the authors and Altay and colleagues note |
| Popularity online mostly comes from chains of sharing | Not supported by Goel and colleagues, who found most popularity driven by the largest broadcast |

How much misinformation people actually see is a different measure from how far it spreads; the exposure studies are in [the human attack surface](/learn/the-human-attack-surface-why-repetition-works), including the finding that "Only 1% of individuals accounted for 80% of fake news source exposures" on Twitter in 2016 ([Grinberg et al., Science, January 2019, abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:%2210.1126/science.aau2706%22&format=json&resultType=core)).

## Messaging apps: the lodge with a forward button

### Limits on forwarding

WhatsApp combines the closed circle with the relay. Groups were limited to 256 members, and the app offered both forwarding and broadcast lists; Philipe de Freitas Melo and colleagues, at the Universidade Federal de Minas Gerais (UFMG) and MIT, described public groups, joined through shared invitation links, as "a shortcut for information to directly traverse" between communities ([de Freitas Melo et al., Can WhatsApp Counter Misinformation by Limiting Message Forwarding?, arXiv 1909.08740, 23 September 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.08740)). The company changed its forwarding rules in steps:

| Date | What WhatsApp announced |
|---|---|
| 19 July 2018 | A test limiting forwarding for all users; in India, where people forward more than anywhere else, a lower limit of five chats and removal of the quick-forward button next to media |
| 21 January 2019 | After six months of evaluation, all users on current versions limited to forwarding to five chats at once; the company said the limit had significantly reduced forwarded messages worldwide |
| 7 April 2020 | Messages labelled as highly forwarded "can only be forwarded to one chat at a time" |

Sources: [WhatsApp, More changes to forwarding, 19 July 2018, updated 21 January 2019](https://blog.whatsapp.com/more-changes-to-forwarding?lang=en); [WhatsApp, Keeping WhatsApp Personal and Private, 7 April 2020](https://blog.whatsapp.com/Keeping-WhatsApp-Personal-and-Private).

### What the limits measurably did

The measured effects published so far come from the company. In April 2020 WhatsApp wrote of its earlier limits that <Claim status="reported" by="by WhatsApp">"At the time, we saw a 25% decrease in total message forwards globally"</Claim> ([WhatsApp, 7 April 2020](https://blog.whatsapp.com/Keeping-WhatsApp-Personal-and-Private)). Three weeks after the one-chat limit, a spokesperson told TechCrunch that <Claim status="reported" by="by WhatsApp">"globally there has been a 70% reduction in the number of highly forwarded messages sent on WhatsApp"</Claim> ([TechCrunch, 27 April 2020](https://techcrunch.com/2020/04/27/whatsapps-new-limit-cuts-virality-of-highly-forwarded-messages-by-70)). Both figures count messages forwarded, not what people believed or did, and no independent audit of them was found in the sources read for this part.

The one published attempt to estimate the effect on spread used a model. De Freitas Melo and colleagues fed data from public political groups in Brazil, India and Indonesia into an epidemiological model and concluded that <Claim status="reported" by="by its authors">limits of about five forwards "offer a delay in the message propagation of up to two orders of magnitude in comparison with the original limit of 256"</Claim>, but that "depending on the virality of the content, those limits are not effective in preventing a message to reach the entire network quickly" ([de Freitas Melo et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.08740)). They found that 80% of images lasted no more than two days in the groups, enough in India to reach half the users of public groups, and suggested a "quarantine approach" that would temporarily restrict the virality features of suspect users and content, especially during elections. Their own stated limit is that public groups are a small, observable part of a mostly private platform.

### India: rumours, mobs and a letter

In India the limits followed killings. On 3 July 2018 the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology said, through the government's Press Information Bureau (PIB), that "Instances of lynching of innocent people have been noticed recently because of large number of irresponsible and explosive messages filled with rumours and provocation are being circulated on WhatsApp", naming killings in Assam, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Tripura and West Bengal, and that "WhatsApp must take immediate action to end this menace" ([Press Information Bureau, WhatsApp warned for abuse of their platform, 3 July 2018](https://pib.gov.in/newsite/PrintRelease.aspx?relid=180364)). WhatsApp replied in a letter to the ministry: "Like the government of India, we're horrified by these terrible acts of violence", describing a label it was testing to show when a message had been forwarded rather than composed by the sender, and a setting letting administrators decide who can post in a group ([The Nation (Thailand), 9 July 2018](https://www.nationthailand.com/international/30349671)).

Two studies looked inside the Indian case. Shakuntala Banaji and Ram Bhat, of the London School of Economics and Political Science (LSE), working from November 2018 to August 2019 with one of 20 research awards funded by WhatsApp, held focus groups and expert interviews in several states. They were told, they write, "strongly and repeatedly that the immediate source of a forward" (the person who passed the message to the group or to the individual) "is one of the most important factors in a user's decision to forward a piece of information, misinformation, disinformation or fake news", and that if that person is regarded as trusted, "then even the most implausible, or fake-looking messages, are accepted as accurate, and passed on"; civic trust, they found, "follows ideological, family and communal ties" ([Banaji and Bhat, WhatsApp Vigilantes, LSE, 2019](https://www.lse.ac.uk/media-and-communications/assets/documents/research/projects/WhatsApp-Misinformation-Report.pdf)). The old two-step flow, in other words, moved into the group chat: the trusted relay became the strongest signal.

Kiran Garimella and Dean Eckles joined more than 5,000 public political groups and collected over 5 million messages from October 2018 to June 2019, around India's national election, 1.6 million of them images. With journalists annotating samples, they estimated that "misinformation makes up a substantial portion (13%) of all image sharing in these political groups"; old images taken out of context were roughly 34% of the misinformation images, memes with false quotes or statistics 30% and doctored images 10% ([Garimella and Eckles, Images and misinformation in political groups: Evidence from WhatsApp in India, Harvard Kennedy School Misinformation Review, 7 July 2020](https://misinforeview.hks.harvard.edu/article/images-and-misinformation-in-political-groups-evidence-from-whatsapp-in-india/)). They warn that their data come from public groups and "may be missing, e.g., fringe, extreme content, which might not be discussed in open groups", and that classifiers to detect such images were brittle when content shifted over time.

### Brazil: larger groups, after the election

Brazil's 2018 public-group research and the forwarding limits in force at the time are set out in [the part on Brazil's courts and Congress](/learn/brazil-mass-messaging-the-courts-and-the-cpmi). The next design change, a feature called Communities that would let groups with thousands of people coexist inside the app, became an electoral dispute in 2022. On 15 April 2022 President Bolsonaro said that an agreement he understood WhatsApp to have made with the electoral court to delay the feature in Brazil "é inadmissível, inaceitável, e não vai ser cumprido", is inadmissible, unacceptable and will not be carried out, and called it censorship; the newspaper noted that groups were then limited to 256 people ([Diário do Nordeste, 15 April 2022](https://diariodonordeste.verdesmares.com.br/pontopoder/bolsonaro-critica-acordo-entre-whatsapp-e-tse-para-eleicoes-nao-vai-ser-cumprido-1.3218204)). On 27 April, after representatives of Meta met the president, WhatsApp said that the launch in Brazil "ocorrerá somente após o período eleitoral", would happen only after the election period, that the decision "foi tomada exclusivamente pela empresa" and "não foi tomada a pedido nem por acordo" with the court; it added that it had signed a memorandum of understanding with the court on other matters, such as a channel for reporting accounts suspected of mass sending, none of which mentioned Communities ([O Povo, 27 April 2022](https://www.opovo.com.br/noticias/politica/2022/04/27/whatsapp-confirma-que-lancamento-do-comunidades-nao-foi-adiado-por-acordo-com-tse.html)).

## What the record supports, and what it does not

| Question | What the sources read here support |
|---|---|
| Did platforms lower the cost of a first broadcast? | Yes: ads from $0.50 a day (Facebook, 2017); priced message packages (Folha, 2018, as reported) |
| Do most posts spread person to person? | No: most cascades stop at the seed or one step, and popularity tracks the largest broadcast (Goel and colleagues) |
| Does falsehood spread by a different mechanism? | Disputed: larger false cascades in a fact-checked sample, but no structural difference at equal size (Juul and Ugander) |
| Did WhatsApp's limits reduce forwarding? | The company reports a 25% fall in forwards and a 70% fall in highly forwarded messages; a model finds delay rather than prevention for highly viral content |
| Does the trusted sender matter? | Yes, in the Indian fieldwork (Banaji and Bhat), as it did in the two-step flow studies |
| Does reach decide persuasion or elections? | Not on this evidence: campaign contact shows near-zero persuasion in US general elections (Kalla and Broockman); radio effects depended on context (Adena and colleagues; Straus) |

## What defenders can do

- **Platforms** have used friction rather than deletion on messaging apps: forwarding limits, labels for forwarded and highly forwarded messages, and group settings (WhatsApp, 2018 to 2020). Juul and Ugander argue that measures which lower person-to-person "infectiousness" at the boundary of veracity should also reduce how deep and wide false cascades grow.
- **Researchers** can compare like with like: matching cascades by size before comparing their structure (Juul and Ugander), stating whether a sample is fact-checked rumours or all news (Altay and colleagues), and saying when data come from public groups rather than the private chats where most messaging happens (de Freitas Melo and colleagues; Garimella and Eckles).
- **Electoral authorities** in Brazil ration broadcast reach by law and have signed memoranda with platforms on reporting channels (TSE and WhatsApp, as WhatsApp described them in 2022); the rules on mass messaging are in [the part on Brazil's courts and Congress](/learn/brazil-mass-messaging-the-courts-and-the-cpmi).
- **Readers** can treat who forwarded a message as a reason to check it rather than a reason to trust it, since the Indian fieldwork found that trust in the sender overrode doubts about the content, and can treat a "forwarded many times" label as a sign that a message did not come from someone they know.

## What to remember

- Before platforms, reach came from owning a broadcast channel, and influence often passed through trusted opinion leaders: the two-step flow.
- Radio's measured effects depended on context: Nazi radio worked where anti-Semitism was already high, and for Rwanda a village-level estimate attributes about 10% of the genocide's violence to one station while an earlier study found only conditional effects.
- Mass campaign contact shows near-zero persuasion in US general elections; reach and persuasion are different things.
- Platforms cut the price of a first broadcast: the IRA's roughly $100,000 in ads sat beside organic reach Facebook estimated at up to 126 million people, 0.004% of News Feed content.
- Most online cascades never spread at all, and popularity mostly tracks the largest broadcast, not long chains of sharing.
- False rumours reached more people than true ones in the fact-checked Twitter sample of Vosoughi and colleagues; when cascades of equal size are compared, the structural differences almost vanish.
- WhatsApp cut forwarding to five chats in 2019 and highly forwarded messages to one in 2020; it reports falls of 25% and 70% in forwards, and a model found that limits delay but do not stop highly viral content.
- In India, trust in the person who forwarded a message weighed more than the content; in Brazil, broadcast time is rationed by law, and a single video reached nearly 300 million views in two days in 2025.

<ReaderQuestions>

- If popularity mostly comes from the largest broadcast, should rules on reach look at the biggest accounts and the recommendation systems that feed them rather than at ordinary users who forward a message?
- A forwarding limit slows every message, true or false. What evidence would show that the delay it buys is worth what it costs to ordinary communication, and who should be able to check that evidence?
- When the only published figures on the effect of a platform's design change come from the platform, what would an independent measurement need to have access to?
- Brazil rations television time by party size but leaves an account with millions of followers free to reach hundreds of millions of views. Is that a gap, a sensible difference between public airwaves and private platforms, or something else?
- If trust in the sender is what makes people pass a message on, who carries responsibility when a trusted relative forwards a falsehood: the relative, the person who wrote it, the platform, or no one?

</ReaderQuestions>
