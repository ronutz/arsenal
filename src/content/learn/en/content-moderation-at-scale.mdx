In the 180 days before 15 November 2026, providers of online platforms filed 4,693,088,940 statements of reasons for moderation decisions with the European Commission's database, from 396 active platforms, and 45% of the decisions were fully automated ([DSA Transparency Database, home page statistics, read 15 November 2026](https://transparency.dsa.ec.europa.eu/)). When the Oversight Board began studying Meta's cross-check programme, after October 2021, Meta told it that it was performing "about 100 million enforcement attempts on content every day", a volume at which, the Board noted, "even if Meta were able to make content decisions with 99% accuracy, it would still make one million mistakes a day" ([Oversight Board, Policy Advisory Opinion on Meta's Cross-Check Program, 6 December 2022](https://www.oversightboard.com/news/501654971916288-oversight-board-publishes-policy-advisory-opinion-on-meta-s-cross-check-program/)). This part of the series opened by [the map from the small lie to the campaign](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign) looks at that arbitration: how moderation works, what the numbers platforms publish show and hide, how appeals and the Oversight Board operate and where they stop, where moderation failed by omission and where by excess, what Brazilian courts have required, and who does the work. What platforms rank rather than remove is the subject of **[Recommendation and profiling: what platforms disclose](/learn/recommendation-and-profiling-what-platforms-disclose)**; the legal regimes are compared in **[The law: Section 230, the DSA, the Marco Civil and the rest](/learn/platform-law-section-230-the-dsa-and-the-marco-civil)**.

## What moderation is

The European Union's (EU) Digital Services Act (DSA) defines "content moderation" as the activities, "whether automated or not", aimed at "detecting, identifying and addressing illegal content or information incompatible with their terms and conditions", including measures "such as demotion, demonetisation, disabling of access to, or removal thereof", and measures against the user, "such as the termination or suspension of a recipient's account" ([Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, article 3(t), 19 October 2022](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065)). The definition matters because it treats ranking a post lower as moderation, not only deleting it. The same regulation forbids imposing on providers a "general obligation to monitor the information which providers of intermediary services transmit or store" ([DSA, article 8](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065)). In practice two rulebooks apply at once, the platform's own and the law of each country. TikTok described the sequence in 2023: content reported as illegal "will first be reviewed against our Community Guidelines and removed globally if it violates TikTok's policies"; if not, "our new dedicated team of moderators and legal specialists will assess whether it violates the law and we will restrict access to the content in that country only"; and the company said it "primarily" uses "machine moderation and teams of moderators" ([TikTok, An update on fulfilling our commitments under the Digital Services Act, 4 August 2023](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-eu/fulfilling-commitments-dsa-update)). The demotion of "borderline" videos that YouTube applies, discussed in the companion part on recommendation, is moderation in the DSA's sense even though the video stays online.

The legal scholar Kate Klonick, who interviewed early staff at Facebook and YouTube, describes the forms moderation takes: before publication (ex ante) or after (ex post); "reactive, in which moderators passively assess content and update software only after others bring the content to their attention, or proactive, in which teams of moderators actively seek out published content for removal"; and either "automatically made by software or manually made by humans" ([Kate Klonick, The New Governors, Harvard Law Review, April 2018](https://harvardlawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/1598-1670_Online.pdf)). The rules began small. When Facebook formed its first specialized moderation team, she records, its moderation rested on "one page of internal 'rules' applied globally to all users", which one early staff member summarized as "a list of things you should delete: so it was things like Hitler and naked people", and another as "if it makes you feel bad in your gut, then go ahead and take it down"; the first draft of comprehensive rules, written in 2009, ran to "roughly 15,000 words", and in January 2009 Facebook opened its Dublin office and began outsourcing moderation through consulting groups.

## The rules, and how they change

Each platform writes its own rules (Meta's Community Standards, YouTube's Community Guidelines, TikTok's Community Guidelines) and changes them on its own schedule. The largest recent change came on 7 January 2025, when Meta's Chief Global Affairs Officer, Joel Kaplan, announced that the company would end third-party fact-checking in the United States in favour of Community Notes, lift "restrictions on topics like immigration, gender identity and gender", focus automated systems on "illegal and high-severity violations, like terrorism, child sexual exploitation, drugs, fraud and scams", and, "For less severe policy violations", "rely on someone reporting an issue before we take any action" ([Meta, More Speech and Fewer Mistakes, 7 January 2025, updated 29 May 2025](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/01/meta-more-speech-fewer-mistakes/)). The stated reason was error: "in December 2024, we removed millions of pieces of content every day", and "we think one to two out of every 10 of these actions may have been mistakes". The post said Meta was "getting rid of most of these demotions and requiring greater confidence that the content violates for the rest", was "requiring multiple reviewers to reach a determination in order to take something down" in more cases, had begun using large language models, a kind of artificial intelligence (AI), to give a second opinion on some content before enforcement, and that under Community Notes "Meta won't write Community Notes or decide which ones show up". It also said Meta would move "the trust and safety teams that write our content policies and review content out of California to Texas and other US locations", and argued, quoting Mark Zuckerberg's 2019 Georgetown speech, that free expression "has been the driving force behind progress".

Meta's first report after the change claimed <Claim status="reported" by="by Meta">"a roughly 50% reduction in enforcement mistakes on our platforms in the United States from Q4 2024 to Q1 2025"</Claim>, with prevalence of violating content "largely" unchanged "for most problem areas" ([Meta, update of 29 May 2025](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/01/meta-more-speech-fewer-mistakes/)). Engadget, reading the same quarterly reports, noted the exceptions: on Facebook, the prevalence of violent and graphic content rose from 0.06–0.07% at the end of 2024 to 0.09%, and bullying and harassment from 0.06–0.07% to 0.07–0.08%, which Meta attributed partly to its attempts to "reduce enforcement mistakes"; content actioned for hateful conduct fell to 3.4 million pieces, the lowest since 2018, spam removals from 730 million to 366 million, and fake accounts removed on Facebook from 1.4 billion to 1 billion; and Meta "didn't explain how it calculated" the 50% figure ([Engadget, 29 May 2025](https://www.engadget.com/social-media/facebook-sees-rise-in-violent-content-and-harassment-after-policy-changes-182651544.html)). Meta's own Transparency Center page for the quarter gives the reasoning: "In December 2024, for example, we removed millions of pieces of content every day. Yet we estimated that one to two out of every 10 of those actions may have been mistakes"; the steps included "Requiring more confidence that content violates our policies before we remove it", and it reports the same two prevalence increases ([Meta, Integrity Reports, First Quarter 2025, 29 May 2025](https://transparency.meta.com/reports/integrity-reports-q1-2025/)). Its first half-yearly report, in March 2026, said that "less than 0.1%" of the content produced on Facebook and Instagram in a quarter "was removed incorrectly", while false positives "increased over 100% during a very brief period of the quarter due to a bug" ([Meta, Integrity Reports, H1 2026, 19 March 2026](https://transparency.meta.com/reports/integrity-reports-h1-2026/)).

The Oversight Board, which Meta funds and which reviews its decisions, wrote in April 2025 that <Claim status="attributed" by="by the Oversight Board">"Meta's January 7, 2025, policy and enforcement changes were announced hastily, in a departure from regular procedure"</Claim>, with "no public information shared as to what, if any, prior human rights due diligence the company performed", and asked Meta to "Assess the human rights impact of the January 7 Hateful Conduct policy updates" and to "Continually assess the effectiveness of Community Notes compared to third-party fact-checking" ([Oversight Board, Wide-Ranging Decisions Protect Speech and Address Harms, 23 April 2025, read through a fetch tool](https://www.oversightboard.com/?p=39438)).

The two sides of the change are on the record in their own terms. For Meta, "Too much harmless content gets censored, too many people find themselves wrongly locked up in 'Facebook jail'" ([Meta, 7 January 2025](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/01/meta-more-speech-fewer-mistakes/)). Against it stand the Board's procedural objection and Meta's own figures showing higher prevalence of violent content and harassment on Facebook. Neither the numbers read here nor the Board's decisions settle which effect is larger.

## Counting: what the numbers show and hide

### Statements of reasons and the database

The DSA requires every hosting provider to give the affected user "a clear and specific statement of reasons" for restricting content or accounts, stating among other things "information on the use made of automated means in taking the decision" and the legal or contractual ground relied on ([DSA, article 17](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065)); online platforms must send those decisions and statements to "a publicly accessible machine-readable database managed by the Commission" without personal data ([DSA, article 24(5)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065)). The database's own summary for the last 180 days lists "Other violation of provider's terms and conditions", "Unsafe, non-compliant or prohibited products" and "Consumer information infringements" as the most reported grounds, and disabling access and removal as the top restrictions ([DSA Transparency Database, read 15 November 2026](https://transparency.dsa.ec.europa.eu/)).

Most statements of reasons (SoRs) are not about speech at all. In a sample of 131 million statements from November 2023, "Google Shopping has submitted more than half of the total SoRs (52.2%), followed by TikTok (17.1%), Amazon (10.8%), Facebook (9.8%)", with X at 0.03% ([Kaushal et al., Automated Transparency, arXiv, 3 May 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.02894)); in the first 100 days, Google Shopping filed "more than a billion SoRs (67.8% of the total)", and the eight social media platforms together accounted for 23.8% ([Trujillo, Fagni and Cresci, 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.10269)). Statements of reasons cover product listings as much as posts. Two research teams tested what the database can be trusted to say. Amaury Trujillo, Tiziano Fagni and Stefano Cresci, of Italy's National Research Council, analysed "all 353.12M records submitted by the eight largest social media platforms in the EU during the first 100 days of the database" and found that "a remarkable fraction of the database data is inconsistent" and that "the platform X (formerly Twitter) presents the most inconsistencies" ([Trujillo, Fagni and Cresci, The DSA Transparency Database: Auditing Self-reported Moderation Actions by Social Media, Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction, April 2025, arXiv version of 7 February 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.10269)). The differences in automation were stark: "none of the SoRs submitted by X specified the use of automated means whatsoever", while "TikTok predominantly indicates fully automated means for moderation (92%)"; Meta's platforms "declared heavy use of fully automated means in their transparency reports, but not a single fully automated action is reported in their submissions", and TikTok's figures in its report and in the database differed by about 50 percentage points. Rishabh Kaushal and colleagues, analysing a sample of 131 million statements from November 2023, concluded that "compliance remains problematic, as the current database structure allows for a lot of discretion from platforms in terms of transparency practices" ([Kaushal et al., Automated Transparency: A Legal and Empirical Analysis of the Digital Services Act Transparency Database, arXiv, 3 May 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.02894)).

### Who reviews, in which language

Very large platforms must report "the human resources" they dedicate to moderation in the EU, "broken down by each applicable official language of the Member States", with their "qualifications and linguistic expertise" ([DSA, article 42(2)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065)). Those European reports are, for Brazil, the only official numbers. The fact-checking agency Aos Fatos found in them that X listed 41 moderators native in Portuguese, Meta 58 fluent in Portuguese (adding that "para línguas amplamente faladas fora da União Europeia, existem revisores de conteúdo adicionais", there are additional reviewers for languages widely spoken outside the EU, without saying how many) and YouTube 464, a figure counting "quantos funcionários revisaram pelo menos dez vídeos" (how many staff reviewed at least ten videos) in a language ([Aos Fatos, Com demissões e sem 'PL das Fake News', 'big techs' aprofundam apagão de dados sobre moderação, 15 July 2024](https://www.aosfatos.org/noticias/big-techs-apagao-dados-moderacao/)). Asked for Brazil-specific figures, no company provided them as requested: YouTube cited "equipes dedicadas ao mercado brasileiro" (teams dedicated to the Brazilian market) within "mais de 20.000 revisores" (more than 20,000 reviewers) worldwide, TikTok a chart showing 4.5% of its human moderators working mainly in Portuguese and more than 40,000 trust and safety staff, Kwai a 15% increase in its moderation team for the 2024 municipal elections, and Meta did not respond. Aos Fatos reported that Meta contracts outsourcing firms employing Brazilian moderators in at least Bogotá, Lisbon, Barcelona and Athens, and a worker it interviewed recalled that Facebook's moderation left Brazil in April 2020.

### What a good report would contain

The Santa Clara Principles, written in 2018 by human rights groups and academics and revised in 2020 and 2021 by a coalition that includes the Brazilian research centre InternetLab, ask companies to publish, by country and rule, the total content actioned and accounts suspended, the number of appeals, the number "(or percentage) of successful appeals", the outcomes of appeals against content "initially flagged by automated detection", and content restored "proactively, without any appeal" ([Santa Clara Principles 2.0](https://santaclaraprinciples.org/)). They also hold that "Companies should only use automated processes to identify or remove content or suspend accounts, whether supplemented by human review or not, when there is sufficiently high confidence in the quality and accuracy of those processes", and that "those making moderation and appeal decisions understand the language, culture, and political and social context of the posts they are moderating". The document says twelve major companies, Meta, Google, Apple, Reddit, Twitter and GitHub among them, have endorsed the principles since 2018. A further principle bears directly on cases such as Brazil's: "Companies should recognise the particular risks to users' rights that result from state involvement in content moderation processes", including demands from state actors.

## Appeals and redress

The DSA gives users at least six months to contest a decision, "electronically and free of charge", through the platform's internal complaint system ([DSA, article 20](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065)), and then the right to take the dispute to "any out-of-court dispute settlement body" certified by a national regulator, without losing the right to go to court ([DSA, article 21](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065)); notices from designated "trusted flaggers" must be handled with priority ([DSA, article 22](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065)).

The volume of internal appeals is large. Meta reported to the Oversight Board that it "received more than 7 million appeals during the month of February 2024" against removals under its hateful conduct rules alone ([Oversight Board, From Bold Experiment to Essential Institution, 4 December 2025, read through a fetch tool](https://www.oversightboard.com/news/from-bold-experiment-to-essential-institution/)). In October 2025 the European Commission preliminarily found that Facebook's and Instagram's appeal mechanisms do "not appear to allow users to provide explanations or supporting evidence to substantiate their appeals", and that their mechanisms for flagging illegal content use "so-called 'dark patterns', or deceptive interface designs" ([European Commission, IP/25/2503, 24 October 2025](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_25_2503)).

The external route is newer. By mid-2026 nine out-of-court bodies had been certified in eight member states; an analysis written by staff of User Rights, itself one of the certified bodies, and of the company Tremau reported that in User Rights' 2025 data "in 84.4% of concluded proceedings, the platform's original decision was deemed unjustified", that Appeals Centre Europe could not review the content in over 7,000 cases and in the 3,000 it reviewed "decided in favor of the user in 59% of the cases", and that platforms implemented the bodies' outcomes unevenly: the effectiveness rate "approached 90%" at TikTok and was "around 13%" for Meta's platforms ([DSA Observatory, State of Play of DSA Dispute Settlement: Meaningful Redress, Uneven Results, 8 June 2026](https://dsa-observatory.eu/2026/06/08/state-of-play-of-dsa-dispute-settlement-meaningful-redress-uneven-results/)). The bodies' decisions are not binding; platforms must engage "in good faith" ([DSA, article 21(2)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065)).

The European Commission has also scrutinized moderation capacity directly: its first DSA proceedings, against X in December 2023, covered "the functioning of the notice and action mechanism for illegal content in the EU mandated by the DSA, including in light of X's content moderation resources", and "the effectiveness of X's so-called 'Community Notes' system in the EU" ([European Commission, IP/23/6709, 18 December 2023](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_23_6709)).

## The Oversight Board: cases and limits

Meta's Oversight Board hears appeals from users who have "exhausted Meta's appeals process on Facebook, Instagram or Threads" and cases referred by Meta; "Case decisions are binding, unless implementing them could violate the law", while its policy recommendations are not ([Oversight Board, home page, read 15 November 2026](https://www.oversightboard.com/)). In its five-year review the Board counted "200+ decisions" since January 2021, 21 members, more than 11,000 public comments, and said Meta "has implemented 75% of the more than 300" recommendations it issued; its home page, read in November 2026, displayed "250+" recommendations and "60%" "Implemented or In Progress", figures the sources read do not reconcile ([Oversight Board, 4 December 2025](https://www.oversightboard.com/news/from-bold-experiment-to-essential-institution/); [home page](https://www.oversightboard.com/)). It is funded through an "irrevocable trust" that covers its operations "up to 2027", and "Meta cannot withdraw funding already committed if it disagrees with our decisions" ([Oversight Board, 4 December 2025](https://www.oversightboard.com/news/from-bold-experiment-to-essential-institution/)).

The same review names the limits: "Meta is not legally required to implement every recommendation", "At times, Meta has declined to implement our recommendations", and "the Board never gets as much access or influence as it would like"; it describes itself as "still the only oversight body of its kind" and says that "In 2026, our scope expands once more as we pilot the ability to review Meta's decisions removing and impacting accounts" ([Oversight Board, 4 December 2025](https://www.oversightboard.com/news/from-bold-experiment-to-essential-institution/)). Meta has 60 days to respond to each recommendation, according to the same review. Frances Haugen, a former Facebook product manager, told the United States Senate in 2021 that the company's closed design left it without oversight <Claim status="alleged" by="by Frances Haugen">"even from its own Oversight Board, which is as blind as the public"</Claim> ([Statement of Frances Haugen, 4 October 2021](https://www.commerce.senate.gov/services/files/FC8A558E-824E-4914-BEDB-3A7B1190BD49)).

Three cases show what the Board has done with its scope:

- **The suspension of Donald Trump (May 2021).** The Board upheld Facebook's decision of 7 January 2021 to restrict the then president's accounts, but found that "it was not appropriate for Facebook to impose the indeterminate and standardless penalty of indefinite suspension", gave Facebook six months to complete its review and wrote that "Facebook seeks to avoid its responsibilities" ([Oversight Board, 5 May 2021, read through a fetch tool](https://www.oversightboard.com/news/226612455899839-oversight-board-upholds-former-president-trump-s-suspension-finds-facebook-failed-to-impose-proper-penalty/)).
- **Cross-check (December 2022).** Asked by Meta to review the programme that gives extra review to posts by listed users, the Board found it "appears more directly structured to satisfy business concerns", that it "grants certain users greater protection than others", and that "on average, it can take more than five days to reach a decision on content from users on its cross-check lists", leaving content identified as violating online while most viral; Meta "does not inform users that they are on cross-check lists". The Board later said it had made "32 recommendations to put more effective guardrails around the cross-check system" ([Oversight Board, 6 December 2022](https://www.oversightboard.com/news/501654971916288-oversight-board-publishes-policy-advisory-opinion-on-meta-s-cross-check-program/); [4 December 2025](https://www.oversightboard.com/news/from-bold-experiment-to-essential-institution/)).
- **The Brazilian general's speech (June 2023).** A video posted on 3 January 2023, two days after President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva took office, showed a Brazilian general calling people to "go to the National Congress … [and the] Supreme Court", with a caption calling to "besiege" Congress; "four users reported the content seven times between January 3 and 4", and successive moderators found no violation; Meta removed it on 20 January, "after the Board shortlisted this case", by which time, on 8 January, "supporters of former president Bolsonaro broke into the National Congress, Supreme Court, and presidential offices" ([Oversight Board, 22 June 2023, read through a fetch tool](https://www.oversightboard.com/news/6509720125757695-oversight-board-overturns-meta-s-original-decision-in-brazilian-general-s-speech-case/)). The Board found the post <Claim status="established" by="by the Oversight Board">"an unambiguous call to forcibly enter government buildings situated in the Three Powers Plaza in Brasília"</Claim>, said that "Meta's content moderators repeatedly assessed this content as non-violating and failed to escalate it for further review", and recommended that Meta "develop a framework for evaluating the company's election integrity efforts".

Set against the numbers above, the Board's decisions number in the hundreds over five years while Meta's appeals ran to more than 7 million in a single month for one policy ([Oversight Board, 4 December 2025](https://www.oversightboard.com/news/from-bold-experiment-to-essential-institution/)).

## Failure by omission: Myanmar

Moderation can fail by leaving harmful content up as well as by taking legitimate content down. The United Nations (UN) Independent International Fact-Finding Mission on Myanmar wrote in 2018 that, for many people there, "Facebook is the Internet", that the platform's "wide reach, relative user anonymity, and difficulty of monitoring or removing posts also make it a suitable instrument to spread messages that may constitute hate speech", and that <Claim status="established" by="by the UN Fact-Finding Mission">"The Mission itself experienced a slow and ineffective response from Facebook"</Claim> when it reported a post calling a human rights defender a "national traitor", which was shared over 1,000 times with comments calling for his death ([United Nations Human Rights Council, Report of the detailed findings of the Independent International Fact-Finding Mission on Myanmar, A/HRC/39/CRP.2, 17 September 2018](https://www.ohchr.org/sites/default/files/Documents/HRBodies/HRCouncil/FFM-Myanmar/A_HRC_39_CRP.2.pdf)). The Mission recorded that Facebook had acknowledged being "too slow", and regretted "that Facebook has been unable to provide country-specific data about the spread of hate speech on its platform". In November 2018 Facebook published a human rights assessment it had commissioned from Business for Social Responsibility (BSR), saying that "prior to this year, we weren't doing enough to help prevent our platform from being used to foment division and incite offline violence", and that it had "hired and onboarded 99" native Myanmar-language reviewers towards a goal of at least 100; in an update of August 2020 it said it had "begun to lawfully provide data" preserved in 2018 to the UN's Independent Investigative Mechanism for Myanmar ([Meta, An Independent Assessment of the Human Rights Impact of Facebook in Myanmar, 5 November 2018, updated 26 August 2020](https://about.fb.com/news/2018/11/myanmar-hria/)).

## Brazil: courts, elections and platforms

Brazil's rules on moderation come mostly from its courts.

**The electoral court.** The Tribunal Superior Eleitoral (TSE) amended its propaganda resolution in 2024 to make providers "solidariamente responsáveis, civil e administrativamente" (jointly liable, civilly and administratively) if they fail to remove, during the electoral period, "condutas, informações e atos antidemocráticos" (antidemocratic conduct, information and acts) and the "divulgação ou compartilhamento de fatos notoriamente inverídicos ou gravemente descontextualizados" (dissemination of notoriously false or seriously decontextualized facts) ([TSE, Resolution 23.610/2019, consolidated text, article 9-E, added by Resolution 23.732/2024, read through a fetch tool](https://www.tse.jus.br/legislacao/compilada/res/2019/resolucao-no-23-610-de-18-de-dezembro-de-2019)). The court's earlier handling of mass messaging is set out in **[Brazil: mass messaging, the courts and the CPMI](/learn/brazil-mass-messaging-the-courts-and-the-cpmi)**.

**The Supreme Court on liability.** The final thesis of the Supremo Tribunal Federal (STF), published in June 2026, holds that article 19 of the Marco Civil da Internet, which requires a specific court order before a platform can be liable for users' content, is "parcialmente inconstitucional" (partially unconstitutional); it makes providers jointly liable "em casos de crime ou atos ilícitos" (in cases of crimes or unlawful acts) once notified, keeps the court-order rule for offences against honour, presumes fault for paid ads and "mecanismos artificiais de disseminação inorgânica" (artificial mechanisms of inorganic dissemination), and makes providers liable for a "falha sistêmica" (systemic failure) to prevent or remove a closed list of serious crimes, including antidemocratic acts, terrorism, incitement to suicide and child sexual abuse ([STF, final thesis on Tema 987, 17 June 2026](https://noticias-stf-wp-prd.s3.sa-east-1.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/wpallimport/uploads/2026/06/17180658/MCI_tese.pdf)). The thesis also obliges providers to adopt "autorregulação que abranja, necessariamente, sistema de notificações, devido processo e relatórios anuais de transparência" (self-regulation that necessarily includes a notice system, due process and annual transparency reports), lets a user whose content was removed ask a court to restore it, and requires a headquarters and legal representative in Brazil. The votes and arguments are in the opener and the [part on the law](/learn/platform-law-section-230-the-dsa-and-the-marco-civil).

**The X suspension.** On 30 August 2024 Justice Alexandre de Moraes suspended X throughout Brazil, in Petition 12404, after the company failed to comply with court orders and pay fines; on 2 September the STF's First Panel upheld the order unanimously, including a daily fine of R$50,000 on people and companies using "subterfúgios tecnológicos (como o uso de VPN, entre outros)" (technological subterfuges such as a virtual private network, VPN) to keep using it, with Justice Luiz Fux reserving that the decision should not reach people who were not party to the case unless they used the platform to defraud it ([STF, STF confirma decisão que suspendeu o X, 2 September 2024](https://noticias.stf.jus.br/?p=93576); [Teletime, 2 September 2024](https://teletime.com.br/02/09/2024/primeira-turma-do-stf-confirma-decisao-que-bloqueou-x-no-brasil/)). Justice Flávio Dino wrote that "O poder econômico e o tamanho da conta bancária não fazem nascer uma esdrúxula imunidade de jurisdição" (economic power and the size of a bank account do not create a bizarre immunity from jurisdiction). Elon Musk had closed X's Brazilian office in August, <Claim status="alleged" by="by Elon Musk, as reported">alleging persecution by the court</Claim> ([Correio Braziliense, 8 October 2024](https://www.correiobraziliense.com.br/politica/2024/10/6960399-moraes-autoriza-que-o-x-volte-ao-ar-em-todo-o-pais.html)). On 8 October 2024 Moraes authorized X's return after it appointed a legal representative, blocked the accounts the court had ordered blocked and paid R$28.6 million in fines, writing that the return was conditioned "unicamente, ao cumprimento integral da legislação brasileira" (solely on full compliance with Brazilian law) ([Congresso em Foco, 8 October 2024](https://www.congressoemfoco.com.br/noticia/5947/apos-mais-de-um-mes-de-bloqueio-stf-autoriza-retorno-imediato-do-x)).

**The general's speech.** The Oversight Board case above is the clearest Brazilian example of a platform's reviewers missing a call to violence before it happened, by Meta's own later admission that "its repeated decisions to leave the content on Facebook were in error" ([Oversight Board, 22 June 2023](https://www.oversightboard.com/news/6509720125757695-oversight-board-overturns-meta-s-original-decision-in-brazilian-general-s-speech-case/)).

## Who does the work

Meta told Agence France-Presse (AFP) in June 2023 that its moderators, working at more than 20 sites around the world, handle "over two million posts daily" ([The Standard (AFP), 21 September 2024](https://www.standardmedia.co.ke/business/amp/national/article/2001503117/court-rules-meta-can-be-sued-over-moderator-sackings)). Much of that work is done through contractors, and it has reached the courts on three continents.

**United States.** In 2020 Facebook agreed to pay $52 million in a preliminary settlement filed in San Mateo Superior Court, California, covering 11,250 current and former moderators in California, Arizona, Texas and Florida; each would receive at least $1,000, more with a diagnosis of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) or related conditions, and up to $50,000 in damages; Facebook also agreed to tools such as muting audio by default and turning videos black and white, and to weekly sessions with a licensed mental health professional ([The Verge, 12 May 2020](https://www.theverge.com/2020/5/12/21255870/facebook-content-moderator-settlement-scola-ptsd-mental-health)). The case began in September 2018 when a former moderator, Selena Scola, sued, alleging she developed PTSD after nine months of viewing images of rape, murder and suicide.

**Kenya.** Daniel Motaung, a South African moderator who worked in Nairobi for Sama, a Meta contractor, sued over working conditions and his dismissal, which he alleges followed an attempt to form a union; a second case groups 185 moderators dismissed by Sama ([The Standard (AFP), 21 September 2024](https://www.standardmedia.co.ke/business/amp/national/article/2001503117/court-rules-meta-can-be-sued-over-moderator-sackings)). Meta argued it could not be sued in Kenya, where it had no activities and was not the direct employer; on 20 September 2024 the Court of Appeal upheld the labour court's April 2023 ruling that Meta could face trial. In December 2024 medical reports filed with the court stated that, of the 144 moderators who volunteered for assessment, <Claim status="reported" by="by the court filing, as reported by CNN">81% were classed as suffering from "severe" PTSD</Claim>; Meta declined to comment on the reports because of the litigation but said its contracts with third-party firms set expectations on counselling, training and fair pay and that reviewers can blur graphic content or show it in black and white, and Sama did not respond; Martha Dark, co-executive director of Foxglove, the British non-profit supporting the case, said that <Claim status="alleged" by="by Foxglove">"moderating Facebook is dangerous, even deadly, work that inflicts lifelong PTSD on almost everyone who moderates it"</Claim> ([CNN, as republished by KVIA, 22 December 2024](https://kvia.com/news/business-technology/cnn-business-consumer/2024/12/22/facebook-inflicted-lifelong-trauma-on-kenyan-content-moderators-campaigners-say-as-more-than-140-are-diagnosed-with-ptsd/)). A separate Nairobi case brought by a Kenyan NGO and two Ethiopians accuses Meta of inaction over hateful content ([The Standard (AFP), 21 September 2024](https://www.standardmedia.co.ke/business/amp/national/article/2001503117/court-rules-meta-can-be-sued-over-moderator-sackings)).

**Brazilian moderators abroad.** Brazilians who moderate Portuguese-language content for Meta through contractors in Bogotá described to Aos Fatos security checks on arrival and a room where "O único objeto permitido na sala onde é feita a moderação é uma garrafa d'água" (the only object allowed in the moderation room is a bottle of water); one said ads are reviewed by Colombians who speak Portuguese with less fluency ([Aos Fatos, 15 July 2024](https://www.aosfatos.org/noticias/big-techs-apagao-dados-moderacao/)). A study by Safernet, a Brazilian non-governmental organization (NGO), found job openings for Portuguese-speaking moderators in 39 cities in 22 countries, according to the same report.

## What defenders can do

- **Read the statement of reasons.** In the EU, every restriction must come with the ground relied on and whether automation was used; the database lets anyone compare decisions across platforms ([DSA, article 17](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065); [DSA Transparency Database](https://transparency.dsa.ec.europa.eu/)).
- **Use every level of appeal.** Internal appeal first, then a certified out-of-court body and, if needed, a court; outcomes differ sharply by platform ([DSA Observatory, 8 June 2026](https://dsa-observatory.eu/2026/06/08/state-of-play-of-dsa-dispute-settlement-meaningful-redress-uneven-results/)). In Brazil, the STF's thesis lets a user ask a court to restore removed content ([STF, 17 June 2026](https://noticias-stf-wp-prd.s3.sa-east-1.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/wpallimport/uploads/2026/06/17180658/MCI_tese.pdf)).
- **Treat transparency figures as self-reported.** Researchers found large inconsistencies between platforms' reports and their database submissions ([Trujillo, Fagni and Cresci, 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.10269)); prevalence, removal and error figures depend on definitions the company sets.
- **Ask about language.** The number of reviewers per language is the first question for any non-English market; the Santa Clara Principles ask for it, and the DSA requires it in the EU ([Santa Clara Principles 2.0](https://santaclaraprinciples.org/); [DSA, article 42(2)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065)).
- **Report through the formal channel.** Notices under the DSA, and in Brazil extrajudicial notices under the STF's thesis, create the record that liability now turns on.

## What to remember

- The DSA defines content moderation broadly, including demotion and demonetization; in 180 days to 15 November 2026 platforms filed 4,693,088,940 statements of reasons, 45% for fully automated decisions.
- At the scale Meta reported to its Oversight Board, about 100 million enforcement attempts a day, 99% accuracy would still mean a million mistakes a day; in January 2025 Meta said one to two in ten removals might be mistakes and cut proactive enforcement for less severe violations, reporting fewer mistakes and, on Facebook, higher prevalence of violent content and harassment.
- Self-reported figures are inconsistent: X declared no automation in the database, TikTok 92% fully automated, and Meta none, despite its reports.
- The DSA requires statements of reasons, free internal appeals and access to out-of-court bodies; those bodies overturned most decisions they reviewed, but Meta implemented far fewer of their outcomes than TikTok.
- The Oversight Board's decisions are binding case by case, its recommendations are not; it has issued over 200 decisions in five years, against more than 7 million appeals to Meta in one month for one policy.
- Moderation fails both ways: the UN mission on Myanmar found Facebook slow and ineffective, and Meta's reviewers left up a Brazilian general's call to besiege Congress until after 8 January 2023.
- Brazil's rules come from the courts: the TSE's joint liability for antidemocratic content in elections, the STF's ruling on article 19, final thesis of June 2026, with a duty to avoid systemic failure, and the 2024 suspension of X.
- Moderators have won a $52 million settlement in the United States and the right to sue Meta in Kenya, where most of those assessed were diagnosed with severe PTSD.

<ReaderQuestions>

- If a system that is 99% accurate still makes a million mistakes a day, which mistakes should it be designed to make: leaving harmful content up, or taking legitimate speech down?
- When a company rewrites its rules and the measured prevalence of harassment rises while its count of mistakes falls, who should decide whether the trade was worth it?
- Can an oversight body funded by the company it oversees, deciding a few dozen cases a year, change practice at a scale of millions of decisions a day, and how would anyone know?
- If Brazilian content is reviewed by people in Bogotá, Lisbon or Athens, and the only official count of Portuguese-speaking moderators comes from European reports, what should a Brazilian regulator be able to demand?
- When moderators develop PTSD from the work that keeps feeds usable, whose cost is that: the contractor's, the platform's, or the users' who never see what was removed?

</ReaderQuestions>
