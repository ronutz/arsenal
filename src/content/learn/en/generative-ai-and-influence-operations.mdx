After what Microsoft's analysts call the "democratization" of generative artificial intelligence (AI) "in late 2022" ([Microsoft, 17 April 2024](https://blogs.microsoft.com/on-the-issues/2024/04/17/russia-us-election-interference-deepfakes-ai/)), a common forecast was that covert influence operations would grow in quantity, quality and personalization ([Simon, Altay and Mercier, 18 October 2023](https://misinforeview.hks.harvard.edu/article/misinformation-reloaded-fears-about-the-impact-of-generative-ai-on-misinformation-are-overblown/)). Three years of evidence now exist from three kinds of source: the threat reports of the companies whose models and platforms the operations used, independent counts of AI in elections, and experiments that measure how far language models change people's minds. They do not all point the same way. This part of the series opened by [the map from the small lie to the campaign](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign) sets them side by side, with the rules the European Union (EU) and Brazil adopted and the limits of the tools meant to detect synthetic content. Voice clones and video deepfakes used for fraud are covered in [their own part](/learn/voice-clones-and-deepfakes-in-fraud); this one is about campaigns to shape opinion. After reading, you can read an AI threat report for what it measures and what it cannot see, weigh the persuasion studies with their critiques, and say what labels and detectors can and cannot do.

## The forecast and its critics

The case for concern was set out before most of the evidence existed. In January 2023 researchers from Georgetown University's Center for Security and Emerging Technology, OpenAI and the Stanford Internet Observatory wrote that language models bring "the promise of automating the creation of convincing and misleading text for use in influence operations, rather than having to rely on human labor", raising "the prospect of highly scalable" campaigns, "perhaps even highly persuasive" ones; they called the exercise "inherently speculative" and concluded that "language models could significantly affect how influence operations are waged in the future" ([Goldstein et al., Generative Language Models and Automated Influence Operations, 10 January 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.04246)).

The counter-case came in October 2023 from Felix Simon, Sacha Altay and Hugo Mercier, who sorted the worries into "increased quantity, quality, and personalization of misinformation" and argued that "current concerns about the effects of generative AI on the misinformation landscape are overblown", the first three arguments being "at the moment speculative", with existing research suggesting "at best modest effects" ([Simon, Altay and Mercier, Harvard Kennedy School Misinformation Review, 18 October 2023](https://misinforeview.hks.harvard.edu/article/misinformation-reloaded-fears-about-the-impact-of-generative-ai-on-misinformation-are-overblown/)). The EU's legislators took the first view as a premise: the AI Act says such systems raise "new risks of misinformation and manipulation at scale, fraud, impersonation and consumer deception" ([Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, recital 133, 13 June 2024](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202401689)).

## How to read an AI threat report

Most of the evidence on real operations comes from companies reporting on their own services. Three features shape what such a report can show.

**What the company can see.** An AI company sees prompts and outputs on its own models; a platform sees accounts and posts on its own service. OpenAI described its position in October 2024: threat actors "most often used our models to perform tasks in a specific, intermediate phase of activity", after acquiring email addresses and social media accounts but before deploying finished posts or malware through distribution channels ([OpenAI, Influence and cyber operations: an update, October 2024](https://cdn.openai.com/threat-intelligence-reports/influence-and-cyber-operations-an-update_October-2024.pdf)). Neither kind of company sees operations that use other tools; in February 2026 OpenAI itself described a Chinese operation that relied on "locally-deployed AI models, especially Chinese ones" ([OpenAI, Disrupting malicious uses of our models: an update, February 2026](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/df438d70-e3fe-4a6c-a403-ff632def8f79/disrupting-malicious-uses-of-ai.pdf)).

**How reach is scored.** Several reports use the Breakout Scale that Ben Nimmo, who is listed first among the authors of OpenAI's October 2024 report, proposed at the Brookings Institution in 2020. It "divides IOs into six categories": Category One operations "only spread within one community on one platform"; Category Two operations spread across platforms or communities but not both; Category Three operations "spread across multiple social media platforms and reach multiple communities"; Category Four operations "break out from social media completely and are amplified by mainstream media"; Category Five operations "are amplified by high-profile individuals such as celebrities and political candidates"; and an operation "reaches Category Six if it triggers a policy response or some other form of concrete action, or if it includes a call for violence" ([Nimmo, The Breakout Scale, Brookings, September 2020](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/Nimmo_influence_operations_PDF.pdf)). The scale measures spread, not persuasion: Nimmo notes that traditional measurement of influence on sentiment would need "repetitive opinion polling before, during, and after the campaign".

**Who is reporting.** The companies that publish these assessments sell the models or run the platforms concerned. Meta says its investigators "focus on behavior when we investigate and take down these campaigns, not on the content they post" ([Meta, What We Saw on Our Platforms During 2024's Global Elections, 3 December 2024](https://about.fb.com/news/2024/12/2024-global-elections-meta-platforms/)). Engagement figures can themselves be fake: in one 2025 case OpenAI warned that "All engagement figures should thus be treated with caution" ([OpenAI, Disrupting malicious uses of AI: June 2025](https://cdn.openai.com/threat-intelligence-reports/5f73af09-a3a3-4a55-992e-069237681620/disrupting-malicious-uses-of-ai-june-2025.pdf)).

## What the companies found, 2024 to 2026

### OpenAI

OpenAI's first report, in May 2024, covered five operations: two from Russia, which it called "Bad Grammar" and Doppelganger, the Chinese network known as Spamouflage, the Iranian International Union of Virtual Media (IUVM), and "a commercial company in Israel called STOIC", whose campaign it nicknamed "Zero Zeno" ([OpenAI, AI and Covert Influence Operations: Latest Trends, May 2024](https://downloads.ctfassets.net/kftzwdyauwt9/5IMxzTmUclSOAcWUXbkVrK/3cfab518e6b10789ab8843bcca18b633/Threat_Intel_Report.pdf)). All five generated content; some produced "texts with fewer language errors than would have been possible for human operators", others "large volumes of short comments"; some generated replies to their own posts "to create the appearance of engagement". The company's verdict: "none of the five operations included in our case studies scored higher than a 2", and "these trends reveal a threat landscape marked by evolution, not revolution". It added a distinction worth keeping: "It is important here to distinguish between effort and effect." Operators also exposed themselves: Bad Grammar "posted content that included refusal messages from our model", and Doppelganger's attempts to generate images of European politicians were refused.

By October 2024 OpenAI said it had disrupted "more than 20 operations and deceptive networks from around the world" that year, including four with election content (the US, Rwanda, India and the European Parliament); it assessed "that all of the election-related operations were in Category Two" ([OpenAI, October 2024](https://cdn.openai.com/threat-intelligence-reports/influence-and-cyber-operations-an-update_October-2024.pdf)). The episode with the widest reach involved no AI at all: a post on X that appeared to expose a Russian troll whose ChatGPT credits had run out "quickly went viral", but OpenAI found that the message "was not generated by our models, but appears to have been manually created", and concluded that "the deceptive activity that achieved the greatest social media reach and media interest was a hoax about the use of AI, not the use of AI itself".

In June 2025, four of ten cases "likely had a Chinese origin" ([OpenAI, June 2025](https://cdn.openai.com/threat-intelligence-reports/5f73af09-a3a3-4a55-992e-069237681620/disrupting-malicious-uses-of-ai-june-2025.pdf)). In one, which OpenAI called "Sneer Review", the operators also used ChatGPT to draft an internal "performance review describing, in detail, the steps taken to establish and run the operation"; on a TikTok video, "we observed 220 comments produced, while the TikTok video displayed 199 comments, suggesting that the majority of visible engagement was AI-generated". Two videos drew a combined 25,000 likes, and OpenAI would place the operation "at the low end of Category 3" if those figures were authentic.

The February 2026 report described a ChatGPT account "linked to an individual associated with Chinese law enforcement" whose status reports described "cyber special operations" against critics at home and abroad, "engaging at least hundreds of staff, thousands of fake accounts across scores of platforms"; when the user tried to plan an operation against the Japanese prime minister, "our model refused" ([OpenAI, February 2026](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/df438d70-e3fe-4a6c-a403-ff632def8f79/disrupting-malicious-uses-of-ai.pdf)). Comparing posts from the same batches, OpenAI found that "some AI-generated social media posts received tens of thousands of views, while other posts created in the same batch received almost none", and concluded that "The use of AI-generated content on its own does not appear to have been the decisive factor", the popularity of the posting accounts mattering more.

Two 2026 cases scored low again. The June 2026 report described two clusters of accounts likely from China that generated comments and images claiming that data centres for AI raised electricity prices for American families, and criticizing US tariffs; OpenAI assessed the activity "as Category One" and "found no evidence of meaningful breakout beyond its own activity" ([OpenAI, June 2026 Threat Report](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/96b559fa-c165-4575-805d-e636909e2f78/June-2026-Threat-Report.pdf)). In August 2026 it reported a cluster very likely from Russia that promoted a self-described expert community, the "International Burke Institute", on five platforms; it placed the operation "at the lower end of Category Three", noting that typical posts "only received low numbers of views" while its Telegram channels counted "10-20,000 followers each" ([OpenAI, Disrupting a new covert influence campaign from Russia, 25 August 2026, read through a fetch tool](https://openai.com/index/disrupting-malicious-uses-of-ai-influence-campaign-russia/)).

### Meta

Meta's report for the first quarter of 2024 said: "So far, we have not seen novel GenAI-driven tactics that would impede our ability to disrupt" operations, while recording "photo and image creation, AI-generated video news readers" and an Israel-based network that "posted likely AI-generated comments under Pages of media organizations and public figures" ([Meta, Adversarial Threat Report, First Quarter 2024, May 2024, read through a fetch tool](https://transparency.meta.com/sr/Q1-2024-Adversarial-threat-report)). It added: "We have not seen threat actors use photo-realistic AI-generated media of politicians as a broader trend at this time."

At the end of the election year, Meta's president of global affairs, Nick Clegg, wrote that the risks of AI deepfakes and AI-enabled campaigns "did not materialize in a significant way and that any such impact was modest and limited in scope"; during the election periods of the major elections Meta monitored, which included Brazil's local elections, "ratings on AI content related to elections, politics and social topics represented less than 1% of all fact-checked misinformation" ([Meta, 3 December 2024](https://about.fb.com/news/2024/12/2024-global-elections-meta-platforms/)). Meta's own image generator "rejected 590,000 requests to generate images of President-elect Trump, Vice President-elect Vance, Vice President Harris, Governor Walz, and President Biden" in the month before the US election, and the covert networks it tracked "made only incremental productivity and content-generation gains using generative AI". The less-than-1% figure counts only content that fact-checkers rated, so it depends on what they chose to check.

### Google

Google's Threat Analysis Group (TAG) reported in June 2024 that DRAGONBRIDGE, its name for the Chinese network others call Spamouflage, had posted "thousands of videos and comments on YouTube using synthetic audio and avatars" before Taiwan's election of 13 January 2024, with "AI-generated news hosts speaking to the camera in the style of a news broadcast"; the campaign "had limited reach, with practically no engagement from organic users" ([Google TAG, 26 June 2024](https://blog.google/threat-analysis-group/google-disrupted-dragonbridge-activity-q1-2024/)). Of the more than 57,000 DRAGONBRIDGE YouTube channels disabled in 2023, "80% had zero subscribers", and its use of AI-generated videos or images "has not resulted in significantly higher engagement from real viewers".

In January 2025 the Google Threat Intelligence Group (GTIG) published an analysis of how government-backed actors used its Gemini assistant: information operations actors used it "for research; content generation including developing personas and messaging; translation and localization; and to find ways to increase their reach", Iranian actors "accounting for three quarters of all use by IO actors"; AI, it concluded, "is not yet the game-changer it is sometimes portrayed to be" ([GTIG, Adversarial Misuse of Generative AI, 29 January 2025](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/adversarial-misuse-generative-ai)). Its November 2025 update found actors asking Gemini to write articles and build tools to automate their workflow, but "we have not identified these generated articles in the wild", and "None of these attempts have created breakthrough capabilities for IO campaigns" ([GTIG, AI Threat Tracker, 5 November 2025](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/threat-actor-usage-of-ai-tools)).

### Microsoft

Microsoft's Threat Analysis Center (MTAC) reported in April 2024 that Taiwan's January election "saw a surge in the use of AI-generated content to augment IO operations by CCP-affiliated actors" (CCP being the Chinese Communist Party), "the first time that Microsoft Threat Intelligence has witnessed a nation-state actor using AI content in attempts to influence a foreign election"; on election day the group it calls Storm-1376 "posted suspected AI-generated fake audio" of the Foxconn founder Terry Gou endorsing another candidate, which "YouTube quickly removed" ([Microsoft, China tests US voter fault lines and ramps AI content, 4 April 2024](https://blogs.microsoft.com/on-the-issues/2024/04/04/china-ai-influence-elections-mtac-cybersecurity/)). Of Chinese AI content on US topics it said: "There is little evidence these efforts have been successful in swaying opinion."

Two weeks later MTAC drew lessons that cut against the forecast: "the use of high-production synthetic deepfake videos of world leaders and candidates has so far not caused mass deception and broad-based confusion", audiences being "more likely to gravitate towards and share simple digital forgeries"; it listed three conditions under which generative AI does work better: "AI-enhanced content is more influential than fully AI-generated content", "AI audio is more impactful than AI video", and fakes "purporting to come from a private setting such as a phone call" are more effective than public ones ([Microsoft, 17 April 2024](https://blogs.microsoft.com/on-the-issues/2024/04/17/russia-us-election-interference-deepfakes-ai/)). In October 2024 it reported Russian "AI-enhanced deepfake videos" about Vice President Harris, one video about Tim Walz "gaining more than 5 million views on X in the first 24 hours", while "most of these videos received minimal engagement" ([Microsoft, 23 October 2024](https://blogs.microsoft.com/on-the-issues/2024/10/23/as-the-u-s-election-nears-russia-iran-and-china-step-up-influence-efforts/)).

### Anthropic, and a government advisory

Anthropic reported in March 2025 that an "influence-as-a-service" operation had used its Claude model not only to write content but "to decide when social media bot accounts would comment, like, or re-share posts from authentic social media users", orchestrating "over a hundred social media bot accounts"; the operation "engaged with tens of thousands of authentic social media accounts", but "No content achieved viral status", and the company said the narratives were consistent with state-affiliated campaigns but "we have not confirmed this attribution" ([Anthropic, Detecting and countering malicious uses of Claude, March 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/news/detecting-and-countering-malicious-uses-of-claude-march-2025)).

One account comes from governments rather than companies. In July 2024 the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), the US Cyber National Mission Force (CNMF) and Dutch and Canadian agencies warned that affiliates of RT, "a Russian state-sponsored media organization", had used Meliorator, "a covert artificial intelligence (AI) enhanced software package", to "create fictitious online personas" on X and spread disinformation about the US, Poland, Germany, the Netherlands, Spain, Ukraine and Israel ([FBI, CNMF and partners, joint advisory AA24-191A, 9 July 2024](https://www.ic3.gov/CSA/2024/240709.pdf)). <Claim status="attributed" by="by the US, Dutch and Canadian authorities">That RT affiliates ran the AI-enhanced bot farm</Claim> rests on the advisory. The same day the Justice Department announced "the seizure of two domain names and the search of 968 social media accounts used by Russian actors to create an AI-enhanced social media bot farm", which it said RT had developed and the Federal Security Service (FSB) had operated ([DOJ, 9 July 2024](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/justice-department-leads-efforts-among-federal-international-and-private-sector-partners)).

## What independent counts found

**Election deepfakes.** Sayash Kapoor and Arvind Narayanan of Princeton analysed all 78 instances of AI use in the WIRED AI Elections Project's 2024 database: "there was no deceptive intent in 39 of the 78 cases", most of those being campaign material; for the deceptive cases, they estimated the cost of making similar content without AI at "no more than a few hundred dollars", and they cite the News Literacy Project's finding "that cheap fakes were used seven times more often than AI-generated content" in the US election ([Kapoor and Narayanan, Knight First Amendment Institute, 13 December 2024](https://knightcolumbia.org/blog/we-looked-at-78-election-deepfakes-political-misinformation-is-not-an-ai-problem)). They concede that the low total "could either indicate that AI deepfakes are a much smaller problem so far than anticipated or that the database has many missing entries".

**Brazil's 2024 municipal elections.** A survey of the electoral courts' official gazettes by the fact-checking agency Aos Fatos with ICL Notícias and the Centro Latinoamericano de Investigação Jornalística (Clip) found that, by the first round on 6 October, "a Justiça Eleitoral publicou decisões sobre ao menos 159 casos envolvendo o uso de IA na propaganda eleitoral", decisions in at least 159 cases about AI in campaign propaganda; 132 of the actions sought the removal of videos, 79 of them on Instagram, and the Northeast accounted for 81 cases ([Aos Fatos, 21 October 2024](https://www.aosfatos.org/noticias/acoes-inteligencia-artificial-justica-eleitoral/)). The cases included fake television news segments, forged audio confessions and dead political patrons "resurrected" to endorse candidates. The survey counted what petitions alleged, "sem considerar se o uso da IA foi reconhecido ou não pela Justiça", without regard to whether the courts confirmed AI use; a Ceará prosecutor, Emmanuel Girão, judged the cases in his state of low gravity: "Aquele nível de inteligência artificial [mais sofisticado] não chegou aqui", the most sophisticated level of AI had not arrived there.

**A research centre's year of elections.** The Alan Turing Institute's Centre for Emerging Technology and Security (CETaS), which followed the 2024 elections, wrote that it "consistently found no evidence that AI-enabled disinformation had measurably altered an election result in jurisdictions ranging from the UK and the European Union to Taiwan and India"; for the US presidential election it found "a lack of evidence" of a measurable impact on the results, "primarily due to insufficient data on the impact of such disinformation on real-world voter behaviour", while "deceptive AI-generated content did shape US election discourse by amplifying other forms of disinformation and inflaming political debates" ([CETaS, Stockwell et al., 13 November 2024](https://cetas.turing.ac.uk/publications/ai-enabled-influence-operations-safeguarding-future-elections)).

The counts and the company reports describe the same pattern from different angles, but both depend on detection: an AI-made item that nobody identified appears in neither.

## Persuasion: what the experiments show

Reach is one question; whether AI-written messages change minds when people do read them is another, and here the experimental results are stronger.

### Messages

Josh Goldstein, Jason Chao, Shelby Grossman, Alex Stamos and Michael Tomz compared real foreign propaganda articles with texts generated by GPT-3 in a preregistered US survey experiment and found that "GPT-3 can create highly persuasive text"; with prompt editing or curation by a fluent speaker it became, "under certain conditions, as persuasive as the original propaganda" ([Goldstein et al., PNAS Nexus 3(2), 20 February 2024, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=38380055)).

Tailoring added less than feared. Kobi Hackenburg and Helen Margetts built an application that fed each participant's demographic and political data into GPT-4 prompts and tested it on 8,587 people: GPT-4's messages were "broadly persuasive, in some cases increasing support for an issue stance by up to 12 percentage points", but "the persuasive impact of microtargeted messages was not statistically different from that of non-microtargeted messages (4.83 vs. 6.20 percentage points, respectively, P = 0.226)" ([Hackenburg and Margetts, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS) 121(24), June 2024, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=38848300)). Size added less than feared too: messages from 24 models tested on 25,982 people showed "sharply diminishing returns, such that current frontier models are only slightly more persuasive than models smaller in size by an order of magnitude or more" ([Hackenburg et al., PNAS 122(10), March 2025, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=40053360)).

### Conversations

Dialogue appears stronger than a single message. Francesco Salvi, Manoel Horta Ribeiro, Riccardo Gallotti and Robert West had 900 participants debate either a person or GPT-4: where the two were not equally persuasive, "GPT-4 with personalization was more persuasive 64.4% of the time" ([Salvi et al., Nature Human Behaviour 9, published 19 May 2025, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=40389594)). An author correction published on 3 September 2026 fixed "a calculation error": the difference between personalized and non-personalized GPT-4 "was not statistically significant" (P = 0.07), so "we cannot conclusively establish from this comparison alone that personalization confers an additional advantage over non-personalized GPT-4", although GPT-4-based microtargeting still "strongly outperforms human performance" ([Salvi et al., Author Correction, Nature Human Behaviour, 3 September 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-026-02588-0)).

The largest study so far, by Hackenburg, Ben Tappin and colleagues at the UK AI Security Institute, Oxford, the London School of Economics, Stanford, MIT and Cornell, ran three experiments with 76,977 participants, 19 models and 707 political issues, and checked the accuracy of 466,769 claims the models made ([Hackenburg et al., Science 390, 4 December 2025, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=41343633)). Persuasive power came "more from post-training and prompting methods", which "boosted persuasiveness by as much as 51 and 27%, respectively", "than from personalization or increasing model scale"; and "where they increased AI persuasiveness, they also systematically decreased factual accuracy". A commentary in the same issue by Lisa Argyle carried the summary "Large-scale studies of persuasive artificial intelligence reveal an extensive threat of misinformation" ([Argyle, Science, 4 December 2025, record](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=41343655)).

In actual elections, Hause Lin, David Rand and colleagues randomly assigned participants in the 2024 US presidential, 2025 Canadian federal and 2025 Polish presidential elections to converse with a model advocating one of the top two candidates, and "observed significant treatment effects on candidate preference that are larger than typically observed from traditional video advertisements"; the models persuaded "with relevant facts and evidence, rather than using sophisticated psychological persuasion techniques", but "across all three countries, the AI models advocating for candidates on the political right made more inaccurate claims" ([Lin et al., Nature 648, December 2025, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=41345316)).

### The conspiracy study and its expression of concern

The best known result runs the other way, towards truth. Thomas Costello, Gordon Pennycook and David Rand engaged 2,190 conspiracy believers in "personalized evidence-based dialogues with GPT-4 Turbo": "The intervention reduced conspiracy belief by ~20%. The effect remained 2 months later", even "among participants with deeply entrenched beliefs" ([Costello, Pennycook and Rand, Science 385, 13 September 2024, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=39264999)). A perspective in the same issue summarized it as "Conversation with a trained chatbot can reduce conspiratorial beliefs" ([Bago and Bonnefon, Science, 13 September 2024, record](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=39265030)).

On 11 June 2026 Science published an editorial expression of concern on the paper, signed by H. Holden Thorp ([PubMed record, 11 June 2026](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=42275523)). According to Retraction Watch, the notice says the authors learned of issues with the public dataset that "made it challenging to reproduce some of the specific values reported in the manuscript" and found "inconsistencies in the application of screening criteria between the manuscript and published analysis pipeline"; the authors reported the problem themselves, and their reanalysis "produces results that match those in the original article in direction, statistical significance, and substantive size" ([Retraction Watch, 11 June 2026](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/06/11/science-ai-chatbots-debunk-conspiracy-theories-expression-of-concern/)). Costello told the site that "None of the findings or conclusions will change as a result of this process", and Brendan Nyhan said he and his students had reproduced the results in two independent experiments at Dartmouth College. An erratum followed on 22 October 2026 ([PubMed record, 22 October 2026](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=42912168)). The erratum, on Science's site, says the authors rebuilt the analysis from the raw data: 15 rows that should not have entered study 1 came out, its sample went from 763 to 748 and its headline effect from 16.76 to 16.55 points of reduction on the 0 to 100 scale, study 2's sample is 1,346 rather than 1,515, and "the paper's principal conclusions and nearly all secondary inferences remain unchanged"; the one claim that lost significance concerned the small subgroup whose focal conspiracy was unambiguously true ([Science, erratum, 22 October 2026](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aem1178)). The same day Science marked its notice as rescinded: "The concerns described here have been addressed through an Erratum. Therefore, this Editorial Expression of Concern has been rescinded." ([Science, 11 June 2026, updated 22 October 2026](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aej2383)). <Claim status="established" by="by Science's erratum">The corrected values differ slightly and the principal conclusions stand</Claim>, and an independent team has reported the direction of the finding reproduced.

Two follow-ups by the same group test what drives the effect. With 955 participants, telling people the debunker was a human expert rather than an AI made no significant difference: "AI persuasion is not reliant on the messenger being an AI model: it succeeds by generating compelling messages" ([Boissin et al., PNAS Nexus 4(11), 14 October 2025, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=41179846)). And a preprint posted on 8 January 2026, not yet peer-reviewed in the version read here, instructed models either to argue against a conspiracy theory or for it ("bunking") with 3,996 Americans: "we did not find consistent evidence of a truth advantage: the LLMs were able to both substantially increase and decrease average conspiracy belief", LLM meaning large language model; prompting models "to only provide accurate information dramatically reduced bunking effectiveness", one frontier model "almost entirely refused to promote conspiracies", and "subsequent corrections were able to reverse the bunking effect" ([Costello et al., arXiv 2601.05050, January 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05050)).

### What the experiments do not show

These are survey experiments: participants are recruited and paid to read or converse, which is the step that the threat reports find operations failing to achieve outside the laboratory. The May 2024 OpenAI report makes the same point from the other side: content "requires a distribution system" and has to compete with everything real people post ([OpenAI, May 2024](https://downloads.ctfassets.net/kftzwdyauwt9/5IMxzTmUclSOAcWUXbkVrK/3cfab518e6b10789ab8843bcca18b633/Threat_Intel_Report.pdf)). Simon, Altay and Mercier's argument about personalization, and Hackenburg and Margetts's null result on microtargeting, are consistent with Salvi's corrected debate result, in which personalization gave GPT-4 no significant edge over its non-personalized version, and with the levers study's finding that personalization had "smaller effects". <Claim status="established" by="by several preregistered experiments">Language models can write messages and hold conversations about as persuasive as people's, and sometimes more</Claim>; <Claim status="disputed">whether that capacity translates into influence at scale through covert operations</Claim> is the question the two bodies of evidence leave open.

## The rules

### The European Union

Article 50 of the AI Act sets the transparency duties. Providers of AI systems that interact with people must ensure the people "are informed that they are interacting with an AI system, unless this is obvious"; providers of systems "generating synthetic audio, image, video or text content, shall ensure that the outputs of the AI system are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated", as far as technically feasible; deployers of deep fakes must disclose them; and deployers of AI-generated text "published with the purpose of informing the public on matters of public interest shall disclose that the text has been artificially generated or manipulated", unless it "has undergone a process of human review or editorial control" ([Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 50](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202401689)). Recital 133 names the techniques in view: "watermarks, metadata identifications, cryptographic methods for proving provenance and authenticity of content, logging methods, fingerprints or other techniques".

The Commission published a voluntary Code of Practice on marking and labelling AI-generated content on 10 June 2026, for obligations "that will apply from 2 August 2026" ([European Commission, 10 June 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-publishes-code-practice-marking-and-labelling-ai-generated-content)); the simplifying amendment approved by the Council on 30 June 2026 cut the grace period for providers' "transparency solutions for artificially generated content" to three months, "with the new deadline set on 2 December 2026" ([Council of the EU, via the EU Delegation to Chile, 30 June 2026](https://www.eeas.europa.eu/delegations/chile/artificial-intelligence-council-gives-final-green-light-simplify-and-streamline-rules_en)). Under the Digital Services Act (DSA), the Commission's election guidelines of April 2024 ask very large online platforms and search engines that can be used to create generative AI content to make it "detectable", using such techniques, which they call "particularly important for any generative AI content concerning candidates, politicians, or political parties" ([European Commission, Guidelines on the mitigation of systemic risks for electoral processes, C/2024/3014, Official Journal of 26 April 2024](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:C_202403014)). The deep-fake provisions are examined in [the part on voice clones](/learn/voice-clones-and-deepfakes-in-fraud).

### Brazil

Brazil's electoral court, the Tribunal Superior Eleitoral (TSE), wrote AI rules into its propaganda resolution for the 2024 municipal elections through Resolution 23.732 of 27 February 2024: synthetic multimedia content in campaign material must be labelled; chatbots and avatars may not simulate conversation, "vedada qualquer simulação de interlocução com a pessoa candidata ou outra pessoa real", any simulated exchange with the candidate or another real person being forbidden; deep fakes to harm or favour a candidacy are banned, and their use "configura abuso do poder político e uso indevido dos meios de comunicação social", constitutes abuse of political power and misuse of the media; and platforms are jointly liable in listed cases ([TSE, Resolution 23.732/2024, read through a fetch tool](https://www.tse.jus.br/legislacao/compilada/res/2024/resolucao-no-23-732-de-27-de-fevereiro-de-2024)). The full set of rules, with the 2026 changes, is in [the part on Brazil's courts and the CPMI](/learn/brazil-mass-messaging-the-courts-and-the-cpmi).

For the October 2026 elections, Resolution 23.755/2026 added duties for providers of AI systems themselves. According to a technical note by ARTIGO 19, Aláfia Lab and the Instituto de Referência em Internet e Sociedade (IRIS), a new paragraph forbids them to "ranquear, recomendar, sugerir ou priorizar informações sobre candidatos, campanhas, partidos políticos, federações ou coligações", to rank, recommend, suggest or prioritize information about candidates and parties, and bars any electoral favouring, "inclusive por respostas automatizadas", including through automated answers ([ARTIGO 19, Aláfia Lab and IRIS, technical note, March 2026](https://artigo19.org/wp-content/blogs.dir/24/files/2026/03/NOTA_TSE2026-VF.pdf)). The note welcomes the rule, citing an Aláfia Lab survey in which "9,7% dos brasileiros já utilizam chatbots de IA como fonte de informação", 9.7% of Brazilians already use AI chatbots as an information source, and warns that chatbots can "alucinar" and generate "ciclos de retroalimentação de desinformação", feedback loops of disinformation. It also records that "a própria Justiça Eleitoral ainda não consolidou um entendimento do que exatamente se entende por deep fake", the electoral courts have not settled what exactly a deep fake is.

## Detection: why it is hard

**Classifiers for text.** OpenAI launched a classifier for AI-written text on 31 January 2023 that, on its own evaluation set, "correctly identifies 26% of AI-written text (true positives)" while labelling human text as AI-written "9% of the time"; the company withdrew it on 20 July 2023 "due to its low rate of accuracy" ([OpenAI, New AI classifier for indicating AI-written text, read through a fetch tool](https://openai.com/index/new-ai-classifier-for-indicating-ai-written-text/)). Weixin Liang and colleagues at Stanford found that such detectors "frequently misclassify non-native English writing as AI generated" ([Liang et al., Patterns 4(7), July 2023, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=37521038)), and Vinu Sankar Sadasivan and colleagues showed that a "recursive paraphrasing" attack "can significantly reduce detection rates" while "only slightly" degrading text quality ([Sadasivan et al., arXiv 2303.11156, March 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11156)).

**Watermarks in the output.** A watermark is embedded when the content is generated. John Kirchenbauer and colleagues proposed one for text in 2023 that promotes a randomized set of "green" tokens during sampling, detectable "without access to the language model API or parameters" ([Kirchenbauer et al., arXiv 2301.10226, January 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.10226)). Google DeepMind described SynthID-Text in Nature in October 2024, with a live experiment on nearly 20 million Gemini responses, and listed its limits: watermarks "require coordination between actors running the LLM text generation services", "the rise of open-source models presents a challenge", they are vulnerable to "stealing, spoofing and scrubbing attacks", and they "are weakened by edits to the text, such as through LLM paraphrasing" ([Dathathri et al., Nature 634, 23 October 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-08025-4)). For images, Mehrdad Saberi and colleagues showed that a "diffusion purification attack" removes low-perturbation watermarks and that watermarking methods "are vulnerable to spoofing" ([Saberi et al., arXiv 2310.00076, September 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.00076)).

**Provenance records.** The Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA) specifies signed "Content Credentials" that record a file's history. Its explainer answers the question "Can the provenance metadata be removed?" with "Yes it can", and adds watermarking and fingerprints as "soft bindings" to recover it; it describes the standard as "not a cure-all for misinformation" and warns that "provenance information alone cannot tell you whether the digital content is true, accurate or factual" ([C2PA, Explainer 2.2](https://spec.c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.2/explainer/Explainer.html)). The US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) notes that metadata "can similarly be stripped altogether, as it often is when files are shared (e.g., via social media platforms)", that a digital signature "cannot verify that the metadata is accurate", that "Text watermarks generally cannot be embedded or detected reliably when the text has low entropy", and that "none of these techniques can be considered as a comprehensive solution" ([NIST AI 100-4, Reducing Risks Posed by Synthetic Content, November 2024](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.100-4.pdf)).

**What platforms say.** Introducing labels for AI images in February 2024, Meta wrote that "it's not yet possible to identify all AI-generated content, and there are ways that people can strip out invisible markers", and that other companies had not yet put such signals in audio and video tools at scale, "so we can't yet detect those signals" ([Meta, Labeling AI-Generated Images on Facebook, Instagram and Threads, 6 February 2024](https://about.fb.com/news/2024/02/labeling-ai-generated-images-on-facebook-instagram-and-threads/)). Labelling binds the lawful; an influence operation that conceals its origin has no reason to mark its output, which is why the platforms' own reports rely on behaviour. The reverse problem, true recordings dismissed as fake, is the [liar's dividend](/learn/voice-clones-and-deepfakes-in-fraud).

## What defenders can do, according to these sources

- **Investigate behaviour, not only content.** Meta attributes its continued ability to disrupt networks to focusing "on behavior" rather than on whether content was AI-made ([Meta, 3 December 2024](https://about.fb.com/news/2024/12/2024-global-elections-meta-platforms/)).
- **Use the AI companies' vantage point.** OpenAI argues that investigating the "intermediate" phase lets AI companies complement "upstream" providers such as email services and "downstream" platforms, and reports that its own AI tools let it "compress some analytical steps from days to minutes" ([OpenAI, October 2024](https://cdn.openai.com/threat-intelligence-reports/influence-and-cyber-operations-an-update_October-2024.pdf)).
- **Share indicators.** The May 2024 report credits peers' earlier research and publishes domains "to enable open-source research" ([OpenAI, May 2024](https://downloads.ctfassets.net/kftzwdyauwt9/5IMxzTmUclSOAcWUXbkVrK/3cfab518e6b10789ab8843bcca18b633/Threat_Intel_Report.pdf)); the 2024 advisory gave platforms technical details of Meliorator for the same reason ([joint advisory, 2024](https://www.ic3.gov/CSA/2024/240709.pdf)).
- **Be careful not to amplify.** Nimmo's scale stresses that journalists, policymakers and celebrities "can play a pivotal role in bringing IOs to new audiences" ([Nimmo, 2020](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/Nimmo_influence_operations_PDF.pdf)); the most viral AI story of OpenAI's 2024 report was a hoax about AI.
- **Use provenance where it helps, for authentic material.** The C2PA explainer's use cases include a human rights organization verifying protest footage that carries Content Credentials ([C2PA, Explainer 2.2](https://spec.c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.2/explainer/Explainer.html)); NIST stresses that each technique's value is "use-case and context specific" ([NIST AI 100-4, 2024](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.100-4.pdf)).

Why a persuaded belief, once formed, resists correction is the subject of [Why misbeliefs persist](/learn/why-misbeliefs-persist); the defences arc treats verification and prebunking in their own parts.

## What to remember

- The 2023 forecast was of cheaper, larger and more persuasive operations; critics called it overblown, and the EU wrote the risk into the AI Act.
- OpenAI, Meta, Google, Microsoft and Anthropic report that operations used generative AI for content, translation, fake engagement and automation, and that almost none reached authentic audiences on the Breakout Scale; the most viral case in 2024 was a hoax about AI.
- These reports measure spread on the companies' own services, depend on what they detect, and come from companies with a stake in the subject.
- Independent counts found half the 2024 election deepfakes in one database non-deceptive and cheap fakes more common; in Brazil, courts decided at least 159 AI cases before the 2024 first round.
- Experiments show AI messages and conversations persuade about as well as people, with little added by microtargeting or model size, more by post-training and prompting, and a loss of accuracy where persuasion rose.
- The study in which chatbot dialogues cut conspiracy belief by about 20% received an expression of concern in June 2026, rescinded in October after an erratum that changed values slightly and left the principal conclusions unchanged; an independent team reports the direction of the result reproduced.
- The AI Act requires machine-readable marking and deep-fake disclosure from 2026; the TSE bans electoral deep fakes and simulated candidate chatbots, and in 2026 barred AI assistants from recommending candidates.
- Text detectors misfire, watermarks can be stripped or spoofed, and provenance shows origin, not truth; platforms rely on behaviour.
