The badge that opens an office door, the card that pays a bus fare and the chip inside a car key share one design: a coil, a small chip, no battery, and an answer for any reader that wakes it up. Whether that answer is a secret depends on the chip. This part of the series opened by [Hacking devices: from the blue box to the Flipper Zero](/learn/hacking-devices-from-the-blue-box-to-the-flipper-zero) follows [Radio for hackers: SDR, HackRF, RTL-SDR and rolling codes](/learn/radio-for-hackers-sdr-hackrf-rtl-sdr-and-rolling-codes) from the sub-gigahertz bands down to the 125 kHz and 13.56 MHz of cards, and goes deeper than the card functions of [the Flipper Zero](/learn/the-flipper-zero-sub-ghz-rfid-nfc-infrared-and-the-panic). After reading, you can explain how a passive card is powered and which standards govern it, decode a 26-bit badge number by hand, follow the Proxmark from Jonathan Westhues's bench to the Proxmark 5, read a MIFARE Classic authentication trace line by line, date the breaks of MIFARE Classic, iCLASS and the car immobilizer transponders, place Brazil's Bilhete Único and Brazilian law in the story, and name the migrations and reader settings that defend a door.

## How a card with no battery talks

Jonathan Westhues, who later built the Proxmark, explained the trick in 2003 while cloning his first proximity card: "The reader constantly transmits a rather strong carrier; the tag derives its power and clock from this carrier, kind of like a crystal radio", and the tag answers by changing "how much carrier it reflects back at the reader" ([Jonathan Westhues, Proximity Cards, October 2003](https://cq.cx/prox.pl)). An employee, he wrote, "holds their card within a few inches of the reader; the reader receives a unique id from the card and transmits it to some central computer that tells it whether or not to open the door" (same source). Two frequency families dominate radio-frequency identification (RFID). His later instrument handled "almost any kind of low-(~125 kHz) or high-(~13.56 MHz) frequency RFID tag" ([Jonathan Westhues, A Test Instrument for HF/LF RFID, February 2009](https://cq.cx/proxmark3.pl)): low frequency (LF) for the older proximity badges and car immobilizers, high frequency (HF) for smart cards, transit cards and Near Field Communication (NFC).

At 13.56 MHz the standards are international, published jointly by the International Organization for Standardization and the International Electrotechnical Commission (ISO/IEC). ISO/IEC 14443 covers "proximity cards (PICCs)", its part 1 now in a fourth edition of April 2018 ([ISO, ISO/IEC 14443-1:2018; read again 17 November 2026 in a browser](https://www.iso.org/standard/73596.html)); ISO/IEC 15693 covers "vicinity cards (VICCs)", which work at longer distances, its part 1 in a third edition of July 2018 confirmed in 2024 ([ISO, ISO/IEC 15693-1:2018; read again 17 November 2026 in a browser](https://www.iso.org/standard/70837.html)). NFC is the phone-friendly layer on top: ISO/IEC 18092, the Near Field Communication Interface and Protocol 1 (NFCIP-1), defines "both the active and the passive communication modes" at "the centre frequency of 13,56 MHz", in a third edition published on 8 December 2023 ([IEC Webstore, ISO/IEC 18092:2023; read again 17 November 2026 in a browser](https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/90337)). The industry consortium behind NFC was announced in 2004 by Nokia, Royal Philips Electronics (whose chip business is now NXP) and Sony ([NFC Forum, 15 Years of Innovation, 2019](https://nfc-forum.org/news/2019-06-15-years-of-innovation-celebrating-the-nfc-forums-15th-anniversary)).

The regulators allow both families as low-power inductive devices, with limits measured as field strength at a distance. In the United States the rules are the Federal Communications Commission's (FCC) Part 15; in Brazil, the restricted-radiation rules of the Agência Nacional de Telecomunicações ([Anatel](/glossary/anatel)); in the countries of the European Conference of Postal and Telecommunications Administrations (CEPT), Recommendation 70-03 of the European Radiocommunications Committee (ERC). They line up closely:

| Band | United States | Brazil | CEPT countries |
|---|---|---|---|
| Around 125 kHz | 9 to 490 kHz: 2,400/F(kHz) µV/m at 300 m ([47 CFR 15.209](https://www.ecfr.gov/api/renderer/v1/content/enhanced/current/title-47?part=15&section=15.209)) | RFID at 119 to 135 kHz: 2,400/F(kHz) µV/m at 300 m ([Anatel, Ato 14.448, item 13](https://informacoes.anatel.gov.br/legislacao/resolucoes/2017/1139-resolucao-680)) | 119 to 135 kHz: 66 dBµA/m at 10 m, reduced by a table ([ERC 70-03, Annex 9](https://docdb.cept.org/download/5013)) |
| 13.553 to 13.567 MHz | 15,848 µV/m at 30 m ([47 CFR 15.225](https://www.ecfr.gov/api/renderer/v1/content/enhanced/current/title-47?part=15&section=15.225)) | RFID: 15,848 µV/m at 30 m ([Anatel, Ato 14.448, item 13](https://informacoes.anatel.gov.br/legislacao/resolucoes/2017/1139-resolucao-680)) | RFID only: 60 dBµA/m at 10 m ([ERC 70-03, Annex 9](https://docdb.cept.org/download/5013)) |

The ERC recommendation's Annex 9 lists the uses this covers: car immobilizers, "radio frequency identification (RFID) applications including for example automatic article identification", access control, and "Near Field Communications (NFC) applications" ([ERC Recommendation 70-03, Edition of October 2026, Annex 9](https://docdb.cept.org/download/5013)). The design consequence is the one this article turns on: the card cannot tell a legitimate reader from any other device that produces the same field, so whatever it says, it says to anyone close enough.

## 125 kHz: a badge that is only a number

The classic office badge is an LF proximity card, and HID Global's own white paper on card data formats shows how little it carries. HID describes its standard 26-bit format, ordering code H10301, as an open format, with 255 possible facility codes (1 to 255) and up to 65,535 card numbers (1 to 65,535) per facility code, for a total of 16,711,425 cards without duplication ([HID Corporation, Understanding Card Data Formats, 2006; link dead as of 17 November 2026](https://www.hidglobal.com/sites/default/files/hid-understanding_card_data_formats-wp-en.pdf)). The reader sends the number to the controller over the Wiegand interface, which the paper calls the most prominent interface in the industry for card access control, and HID adds that its specific card formats are identical on 125 kHz Prox and 13.56 MHz iCLASS cards (same source).

### Worked example: decoding a 26-bit badge

The format is a leading even parity bit, an 8-bit facility code, a 16-bit card number and a trailing odd parity bit; the leading parity bit covers the first 12 data bits, which together with it must hold an even number of ones, and the trailing bit makes the last 13 bits odd ([HID Corporation, Understanding Card Data Formats; link dead as of 17 November 2026](https://www.hidglobal.com/sites/default/files/hid-understanding_card_data_formats-wp-en.pdf); the same layout is coded in the Proxmark3 client's H10301 packer: [RfidResearchGroup, proxmark3, client/src/wiegand_formats.c](https://github.com/RfidResearchGroup/proxmark3/blob/master/client/src/wiegand_formats.c)). Take an illustrative card, chosen for this example, with facility code 18 and card number 12345:

| Part | Bits | How it is obtained |
|---|---|---|
| Facility code 18 | 00010010 | 18 in 8 binary digits |
| Card number 12345 | 0011000000111001 | 12345 in 16 binary digits |
| First 12 data bits | 000100100011 | four ones, already even, so the even parity bit is 0 |
| Last 12 data bits | 000000111001 | four ones, so the odd parity bit is 1 to make five |
| The 26 bits sent | 0 00010010 0011000000111001 1 | parity, facility, number, parity |

Nothing in those 26 bits is secret or computed from a key. Anyone who reads them, from the card or from the Wiegand wires behind the reader, holds everything the door will ever ask for; the order of the bits on the wire is the kind of question this site's [bit numbering tool](/tools/bit-numbering-and-order) answers.

### Cloning an ID-only card

Westhues showed the consequence in public, one device at a time. In October 2003 in Waterloo he described a home-made cloner for Motorola FlexPass cards that took "a month of evenings" ([Jonathan Westhues, Proximity Cards, October 2003](https://cq.cx/prox.pl)). In December 2005 in Cambridge, Massachusetts, he described the proxmarkii, which worked "with nearly any kind of 125 kHz card, and many types of 13.56 MHz cards", tested against an HID Prox Card II that uses slow frequency-shift keying (FSK), and noted that the process "would be required to clone any kind of ID-only tag" ([Jonathan Westhues, proxmarkii, December 2005](https://cq.cx/proxmarkii.pl)). He drew the line that still matters: "a tag that uses cryptographic techniques can resist any practical attempts to clone it over the air", although a reader-like device can still talk to it (same source). In 2006 he cloned an implantable VeriChip, marketed for access control, and wrote that someone could "sit next to you on the subway, and read your chip's ID. This takes less than a second" ([Jonathan Westhues, Demo: Cloning a Verichip, January and July 2006](https://cq.cx/verichip.pl)).

Cloning also needs a blank that can pretend to be something else, and such blanks are ordinary products. Microchip's ATA5577C, a read/write LF transponder for 100 to 150 kHz, offers a configurable data rate and Bi-phase, Manchester, FSK, phase-shift keying (PSK) and non-return-to-zero (NRZ) coding, a password mode and a lock bit per block ([Microchip Technology, ATA5577C datasheet, 9187H, July 2014](https://ww1.microchip.com/downloads/en/DeviceDoc/Atmel-9187-RFID-ATA5577C_Datasheet.pdf)); the Proxmark community's notes on downgrade attacks list the T5577 family simply as "a low frequency multi purpose card. Used as clone card" ([RFID Research Group, Proxmark3 repository, Notes on downgrade attacks, 2023](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/RfidResearchGroup/proxmark3/master/doc/hid_downgrade.md)).

### LF transponders with cryptography: the car key

Car immobilizers moved to LF transponders with ciphers, and researchers took those apart too.

- **Texas Instruments' DST, 2005.** Stephen Bono, Matthew Green, Adam Stubblefield, Ari Juels, Aviel Rubin and Michael Szydlo reverse-engineered the Digital Signature Transponder, whose key "is only 40 bits", recovered a key "in under an hour using two responses to arbitrary challenges" with sixteen field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs), and with simulated tags "purchased gasoline at a service station and started an automobile"; they counted more than 150 million immobilizer keys with Texas Instruments LF transponders, fixed-code ones included, and more than seven million cryptographic ExxonMobil SpeedPass tags ([Bono et al., Security Analysis of a Cryptographically-Enabled RFID Device, 14th USENIX Security Symposium, 2005](https://www.usenix.org/legacy/events/sec05/tech/bono/bono.pdf)).
- **NXP's Hitag2, 2012.** Roel Verdult, Flavio Garcia and Josep Balasch wrote that "Hitag2, introduced in 1996, is currently the most widely used transponder in the car immobilizer industry", used by at least 34 car makes in more than 200 models with a proprietary [stream cipher](/glossary/stream-cipher) and 48-bit keys, and that their most serious attack "recovers the secret key from a car in less than six minutes using ordinary hardware"; they informed NXP six months before publication and reported that NXP had for years encouraged the industry to migrate to products with community-reviewed ciphers such as the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) ([Verdult, Garcia and Balasch, Gone in 360 Seconds: Hijacking with Hitag2, USENIX Security 2012](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/usenixsecurity12/sec12-final95.pdf)).
- **Megamos Crypto, 2013 to 2015.** Verdult, Garcia and Barış Ege recovered the 96-bit key of the Megamos Crypto transponder, which they said is used "in most Audi, Fiat, Honda, Volkswagen and Volvo cars", with an attack that "takes only 30 minutes" from beginning to end ([USENIX, Dismantling Megamos Crypto: Wirelessly Lockpicking a Vehicle Immobilizer](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity15/technical-sessions/presentation/verdult)). The paper was accepted for USENIX Security 2013 but "withdrawn from publication by its authors in response to the imposition of an injunction by the High Court of Justice in the United Kingdom prohibiting the authors, their institutions, and anyone who assists them, from publishing key sections of the paper"; USENIX published it in 2015, two years later, and the authors wrote that they had notified the manufacturer in November 2012 ([Verdult, Garcia and Ege, Dismantling Megamos Crypto, USENIX Security 2013 supplement, 2015](https://www.cs.ru.nl/~rverdult/Dismantling_Megamos_Crypto_Wirelessly_Lockpicking_a_Vehicle_Immobilizer-USENIX_2015.pdf)).

## The Proxmark: from a Waterloo bench to the Proxmark 5

The instrument that most of this research used began as Westhues's hobby. His page for the proxmark3, dated February 2009, describes a device that "can act as a reader", "can eavesdrop on a transaction between another reader and a tag", can analyse the signal for power attacks and "can pretend to be a tag itself"; the trick, as with an SDR, is digital signal processing, with a small FPGA filtering the fast 13.56 MHz subcarriers before an ARM7 microcontroller ([Jonathan Westhues, A Test Instrument for HF/LF RFID, February 2009](https://cq.cx/proxmark3.pl)). Of an HID Prox Card II trace he wrote that it "might be demodulated, to obtain the card's ID, or we might just replay it verbatim, to clone the card" (same source). He released the design under the GNU General Public License (GPL) as proxmark3-may23-2007.zip, noted that a group in Nijmegen had built units and used one to demonstrate attacks on MIFARE Classic, and said he had no plans for further development, which others continued (same source). The Hitag2 paper of 2012 calls the "Proxmark III board, originally developed by Jonathan Westhues", the central element of its experimental platform ([Verdult, Garcia and Balasch, USENIX Security 2012](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/usenixsecurity12/sec12-final95.pdf)).

The continuation that dominates today is the RFID Research Group (RRG) repository, known as the Iceman fork, which describes the Proxmark as "the swiss-army tool of RFID", "Originally built by Jonathan Westhues", and asks to be cited with C. Herrmann, P. Teuwen, O. Moiseenko and M. Walker as the first authors; its code is licensed GPLv3 or later ([RFID Research Group, Proxmark3 Iceman repository, README, read 16 November 2026](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/RfidResearchGroup/proxmark3/master/README.md)). Proxmark.com says the Proxmark 3 RDV4 "is designed and manufactured by RRG, a company formed by four people instrumental to the Proxmark 3", including the forum moderators known as iceman and 0xFFFF ([Proxmark.com, Proxmark 3 RDV4, read 16 November 2026](https://proxmark.com/proxmark-3-hardware/proxmark-3-rdv4)).

## MIFARE Classic: the card in a billion pockets

At 13.56 MHz the dominant card for two decades was NXP's MIFARE Classic. The Arnhem court that heard NXP's case in 2008 recorded that "NXP brengt sinds 1995 de zogenaamde MIFARE Classic Chip op de markt" (NXP has marketed the so-called MIFARE Classic chip since 1995) ([Rechtbank Arnhem, judgment in summary proceedings of 18 July 2008, LJN BD7578, case 171900 / KG ZA 08-415](https://sos.cs.ru.nl/applications/rfid/kortgeding-vonnis.pdf)). The Radboud University researchers who broke it wrote that, according to NXP, more than 1 billion MIFARE cards had been sold, about 200 million MIFARE Classic tags were in use, and the family held about 85% of the contactless smart card market ([Garcia et al., Dismantling MIFARE Classic, ESORICS 2008, LNCS 5283, pp. 97–114](https://sos.cs.ru.nl/applications/rfid/2008-esorics.pdf)). The card follows parts 1 to 3 of ISO/IEC 14443-A, but not part 4; instead it uses its own secure layer built on the proprietary CRYPTO1 cipher ([Garcia, van Rossum, Verdult and Wichers Schreur, Wirelessly Pickpocketing a Mifare Classic Card, IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy 2009](https://www.cs.ru.nl/~rverdult/Wirelessly_Pickpocketing_a_Mifare_Classic_Card-SNP_2009.pdf)).

Its memory is divided into sectors of 16-byte blocks; "The last block of each sector is called the sector trailer and stores two secret keys and access conditions", and a reader must authenticate to a sector, with key A or key B as the access conditions say, before touching its blocks ([Garcia et al., ESORICS 2008](https://sos.cs.ru.nl/applications/rfid/2008-esorics.pdf)). CRYPTO1 is "a stream cipher using a 48 bit" key, built on a 48-bit linear feedback shift register (LFSR) and a filter function, and the tag's random number generator is a 16-bit LFSR, so that "the first half of nT determines the second half" (same source).

### Worked example: a MIFARE Classic authentication

The ESORICS paper prints a real authentication between a reader and a MIFARE Classic 1K card. Laid out with the paper's own labels (same source):

| Step | Sender | Bytes (hexadecimal) | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 01 | Reader | 26 | request, type A |
| 02 | Tag | 04 00 | answer to request |
| 03 | Reader | 93 20 | select |
| 04 | Tag | c2 a8 2d f4 b3 | the card's unique identifier (UID) and its check byte |
| 05 | Reader | 93 70 c2 a8 2d f4 b3 ba a3 | select this UID |
| 06 | Tag | 08 b6 dd | "mifare 1k" |
| 07 | Reader | 60 30 76 4a | authenticate for block 30 (0x30) |
| 08 | Tag | 42 97 c0 a4 | the tag's challenge nonce nT, sent in the clear |
| 09 | Reader | 7d db 9b 83 67 eb 5d 83 | the reader's nonce nR and its answer aR, encrypted with keystream |
| 10 | Tag | 8b d4 10 08 | the tag's answer aT, encrypted |

Steps 01 to 06 are the ISO/IEC 14443-A anticollision that every card of the family performs; from step 09 on, "all communication is encrypted", each value combined by exclusive-or (XOR) with keystream from CRYPTO1 (same source). The weaknesses the researchers found live in the lines a casual observer would pass over: nT in step 08 goes in the clear and, coming from a 16-bit generator whose output depends only on the time since power-up, is predictable to anyone who controls that timing (same source). The [nonce](/glossary/nonce) was meant to make each run unique; a nonce from a 16-bit counter is not much of one.

## The break, from Berlin to Nijmegen

The cipher was a trade secret, and the secret held for a long time. Karsten Nohl, David Evans, Starbug and Henryk Plötz wrote in 2008 that "the design of the cipher analyzed in this paper, for example, had not been disclosed for 14 years despite more than a billion shipped units", and that they had reconstructed it "by using a combination of image analysis of circuits and protocol analysis", finding that weak random numbers and a protocol flaw let precomputed tables "find any key in a matter of seconds" ([Nohl, Evans, Starbug and Plötz, Reverse-Engineering a Cryptographic RFID Tag, USENIX Security 2008](https://www.usenix.org/legacy/event/sec08/tech/full_papers/nohl/nohl.pdf)). Nohl and Plötz had first presented the work as "Mifare" in Saal 3 of the 24th Chaos Communication Congress ([24C3](/glossary/ccc-congress)) in Berlin at 23:00 on 28 December 2007 ([24C3, Fahrplan, event 2378](https://events.ccc.de/congress/2007/Fahrplan/events/2378.en.html)), the annual congress of the [Chaos Computer Club](/glossary/chaos-computer-club).

In Nijmegen, the Digital Security group of Radboud University found the cipher's working on 7 March 2008 by a different route, "flawed authentication attempts" against the card, and announced on 12 March that it had cloned a MIFARE Classic access card; for systems where all cards share one key, it described an attack in which "a card of, say, an employee can be cloned by bumping into that person with a portable card reader" ([Radboud University Nijmegen, Digital Security group, press release, 12 March 2008](https://sos.cs.ru.nl/applications/rfid/pressrelease.en.html)). The release cited a report by the Dutch research organization TNO, published on 26 February 2008, estimating a brute-force search of the 48-bit key at about nine hours on advanced equipment, and named the Dutch OV-chipkaart and the London and Hong Kong transit systems among the uses (same source).

NXP went to court. In the university's account, it had informed the Dutch government and NXP confidentially in March, withheld details while they acted, and sent the paper to NXP in June; in early July NXP sought an injunction against Professor Bart Jacobs and the university ([Radboud University Nijmegen, press release, 18 July 2008](https://sos.cs.ru.nl/applications/rfid/pressrelease-courtdecision.en.html)). The Arnhem judge refused it on 18 July 2008. The judgment's summary says that, without further evidence, the chip's algorithm could not be assumed to be a work protected by copyright, that the need to restrict publication had not been convincingly shown "in het licht van artikel 10 lid 2 EVRM" (in the light of article 10(2) of the European Convention on Human Rights), and that "De vorderingen van NXP zijn daarom afgewezen" (NXP's claims are therefore rejected) ([Rechtbank Arnhem, 18 July 2008, LJN BD7578](https://sos.cs.ru.nl/applications/rfid/kortgeding-vonnis.pdf)). The university summarized the reasoning as "in a democratic society it is of great importance that the results of scientific research can be published" ([Radboud University Nijmegen, press release, 18 July 2008](https://sos.cs.ru.nl/applications/rfid/pressrelease-courtdecision.en.html)).

The paper appeared at the European Symposium on Research in Computer Security (ESORICS) 2008 in Málaga that October. Its most serious attack "recovers the secret key from just one or two authentication attempts with a genuine reader in less than a second on ordinary hardware and without any pre-computation", and it lets an eavesdropper "decrypt the whole trace" and "clone a card or to restore a real card to a previous state" ([Garcia et al., Dismantling MIFARE Classic, ESORICS 2008](https://sos.cs.ru.nl/applications/rfid/2008-esorics.pdf)). The next year, at the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) Symposium on Security and Privacy, the same group removed the need for a reader at all: four "card-only" attacks, the most serious recovering a key "in less than a second on ordinary hardware", fast enough to "'pickpocket' a victim's Mifare Classic card, i.e., to clone it immediately" ([Garcia et al., Wirelessly Pickpocketing a Mifare Classic Card, IEEE S&P 2009](https://www.cs.ru.nl/~rverdult/Wirelessly_Pickpocketing_a_Mifare_Classic_Card-SNP_2009.pdf)). Nicolas Courtois, independently, described a card-only attack needing minutes near the card, so that "anybody sitting next to the victim on a train or on a plane is now be able to clone his/her pass", and called the problem "systemic" ([Nicolas T. Courtois, The Dark Side of Security by Obscurity and Cloning MiFare Classic Rail and Building Passes Anywhere, Anytime, IACR ePrint 2009/137, SECRYPT 2009](https://eprint.iacr.org/2009/137)).

## NXP's answer and the long tail

NXP's MIFARE site said it had sent information letters on the subject in 2008 and 2015, that it was aware that several research groups had developed attacks to break the keys of MIFARE Classic cards, and that it recommended MIFARE Plus and MIFARE DESFire, both with strong AES encryption, for new access management implementations ([NXP, MIFARE Classic, Information for System Integrators, update of 12 October 2015; link dead as of 17 November 2026](https://www.mifare.net/products/chip-card-ics/mifare-classic/information-for-system-integrators/)). Its security statement of the same date was blunter: NXP did not recommend designing MIFARE Classic into any security-relevant application ([NXP, Security Statement on Crypto1 Implementations, 12 October 2015; link dead as of 17 November 2026](https://www.mifare.net/en/security-statement-on-crypto1-implementations/)).

The installed base did not disappear, and neither did the attacks. Many integrators had deployed "hardened" MIFARE Classic cards that fixed the implementation mistakes the card-only attacks relied on; Carlo Meijer and Roel Verdult answered at the ACM Conference on Computer and Communications Security (CCS) in 2015 with a ciphertext-only, card-only attack that "solely depends on the cryptographic weaknesses of the crypto1 cipher", needs "only a few minutes of wireless interaction with the card", and can only be avoided by upgrading all cards and readers to a protocol that "inherently breaks their backwards compatibility" ([Meijer and Verdult, Ciphertext-only Cryptanalysis on Hardened Mifare Classic Cards, ACM CCS 2015, pp. 18–30](https://www.cs.ru.nl/~rverdult/Ciphertext-only_Cryptanalysis_on_Hardened_Mifare_Classic_Cards-CCS_2015.pdf)). They noted that the cards were still used in London's Oyster, Boston's Charlie Card, Taiwan's EasyCard and the OV-chipkaart, and in access control at "ministries, banks, prisons and nuclear power stations" (same source).

Then came the clones of the chip itself. In a preprint received on 12 August 2024 and last revised on 7 September 2025, Philippe Teuwen, then at Quarkslab, examined the FM11RF08S, "a new variant of MIFARE Classic" released in 2020 by "the leading Chinese manufacturer of unlicensed 'MIFARE compatible' chips" with countermeasures against all known card-only attacks; he reported that "we discovered a hardware backdoor and successfully cracked its key", which lets anyone who knows it "compromise all user-defined keys on these cards without prior knowledge, simply by accessing the card for a few minutes", and that older cards had "another hardware backdoor key that was common to several manufacturers" ([Philippe Teuwen, MIFARE Classic: exposing the static encrypted nonce variant, IACR ePrint 2024/1275](https://eprint.iacr.org/2024/1275)). The paper's keywords name the maker as Fudan (same source).

## iCLASS, Seos and the downgrade

HID's own answer to Prox was iCLASS, and its security model failed in a familiar way. Flavio Garcia, Gerhard de Koning Gans, Roel Verdult and Milosch Meriac wrote in 2012 that iCLASS, an ISO/IEC 15693 card introduced in 2002 "as a secure replacement of the HID Prox card", had sold more than 300 million units; that "all iClass Standard cards worldwide share the same master key for the iClass application", stored in every reader; and that their attack on iCLASS Elite "directly recovers the master key from only 15 authentication attempts with a legitimate reader", executable "within 5 seconds on an ordinary laptop" ([Garcia, de Koning Gans, Verdult and Meriac, Dismantling iClass and iClass Elite, ESORICS 2012, LNCS 7459, pp. 697–715](https://www.cs.ru.nl/~rverdult/Dismantling_iClass_and_iClass_Elite-ESORICS_2012.pdf)).

Its successor, Seos, introduced in 2012, had no published cryptographic flaw when Sam Haskins and Trevor Stevado of Loudmouth Security showed in March 2023 that it could still be relayed: with inexpensive hardware including the Proxmark 3 RDV4 and the internet as the link, they opened a door in their laboratory "with a card approximately 1960 km away"; the mitigations they discuss "rely on rejecting cards that take too long to reply" ([Haskins and Stevado, Unlocking doors from half a continent away: A relay attack against HID Seos, IACR ePrint 2023/450, received 28 March 2023](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/450)). The relay principle is the same one that defeats passive keyless entry in cars, described in [the previous part](/learn/radio-for-hackers-sdr-hackrf-rtl-sdr-and-rolling-codes).

The cheapest attack on a strong card is often to make the reader accept a weak one. Notes in the Proxmark repository, written in 2023, describe the "downgrade attack": reading the access-control payload from a secure iCLASS SE or Seos credential with an authorized reader and writing it onto a legacy-format card; they say there are no card-only attack vectors against those credentials, but that "most readers are left in their default configuration with iCLASS legacy enabled", which makes the logical copy work ([RFID Research Group, Proxmark3 repository, Notes on downgrade attacks, 2023](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/RfidResearchGroup/proxmark3/master/doc/hid_downgrade.md)). That is a statement by the tool's authors about reader configurations, not a measurement, and it is reported here as theirs.

## Emulators: the Chameleon family

Where the Proxmark is a laboratory, the Chameleon is a card that can be anything. The ChameleonMini, "a freely programmable, portable tool for NFC security analysis that can emulate and clone contactless cards, read RFID tags and sniff/log RF data", reached its Revision G through a Kickstarter project with more than 1,700 backers and is made by Kasper & Oswald GmbH, which warns that third-party clones are sometimes defective and some violate the project's licence ([Kasper & Oswald, ChameleonMini, README, read 16 November 2026](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/emsec/ChameleonMini/master/README.md)). The RFID Research Group's ChameleonUltra moved to Nordic Semiconductor's nRF52840, whose built-in NFC Tag-A module, the developers wrote, emulates a complete MIFARE Classic card with "very good emulation performance", together with Bluetooth Low Energy and USB ([RFID Research Group, ChameleonUltra technical whitepaper, read 16 November 2026](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/wiki/RfidResearchGroup/ChameleonUltra/technical_whitepaper.md)). The whitepaper lists the MIFARE Classic key-recovery attacks the device supports, from "Darkside" and "Nested" to "StaticEncNested", which it links to Teuwen's backdoor paper, and shows DESFire emulation supported only at low data rates (same source). The family has three variants: the Ultra, which adds an NXP MFRC522 reader chip; the Lite, which can only emulate; and the Devkit ([RFID Research Group, ChameleonUltra hardware, read 16 November 2026](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/wiki/RfidResearchGroup/ChameleonUltra/hardware.md)). Its authorized distributors include Lab401 in Lyon and Hacker Warehouse in Santa Ana ([RFID Research Group, ChameleonUltra README, read 16 November 2026](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/RfidResearchGroup/ChameleonUltra/main/README.md)).

## NFC phones: relaying a card through malware

A phone with NFC is a card reader and a card emulator in one pocket, and criminals noticed. On 22 August 2024 ESET Research described NGate, Android malware used against clients of three Czech banks that relayed "data from victims' payment cards, via a malicious app installed on their Android devices, to the attacker's rooted Android phone", which then emulated the card at an automated teller machine (ATM); ESET called it "the first time we have seen Android malware with this capability being used in the wild", and said it was based on NFCGate, a research tool "designed by students at the Technical University of Darmstadt" ([ESET Research, NGate Android malware relays NFC traffic to steal cash, 22 August 2024](https://www.welivesecurity.com/en/eset-research/ngate-android-malware-relays-nfc-traffic-to-steal-cash/)). On 21 April 2026 ESET reported a new variant, built by patching a legitimate NFC relay app called HandyPay, in a campaign it said had run since November 2025 and "targets Android users in Brazil", spread through a website impersonating a Brazilian lottery, Rio de Prêmios, and a fake Google Play page; the variant also steals the card's personal identification number (PIN), and ESET noted that an earlier NGate variant called PhantomCard had also targeted Brazil ([ESET Research, New NGate variant hides in a trojanized NFC payment app, 21 April 2026](https://welivesecurity.com/en/eset-research/new-ngate-variant-hides-in-a-trojanized-nfc-payment-app)). This is relay, not cloning: the payment card's cryptography is untouched, and the attacker borrows the real card in real time, the same principle Haskins and Stevado used against Seos.

## Brazil: the Bilhete Único, the law and the shops

### The Bilhete Único

São Paulo's transit card has lived the MIFARE story. NXP announced in July 2013 that SPTrans, the municipal transport company, was upgrading to MIFARE Plus; the announcement, as reported by the trade site Electronic Specifier, said that "The Bilhete Unico smart card today is based on MIFARE® Classic technology with about 40 million cards deployed since its launch in 2004, with seven million users on regular basis, 10 thousand recharge stations, and more than 340 million transactions per month" ([Electronic Specifier, NXP upgrade SPTrans with its MIFARE Plus, 11 July 2013](https://www.electronicspecifier.com/products/design-automation/mifare-plus-nxp-upgrade-sptrans-with-its-mifare-plus)). SPTrans's 2021 tender for 3.1 million contactless cards specified at least 4 kilobytes of electrically erasable programmable read-only memory (EEPROM) and "segurança no acesso à memória por autenticação mútua entre o cartão e a leitora e algoritmos de criptografia" (memory access secured by mutual authentication between card and reader and encryption algorithms), without naming a chip ([Diário do Transporte, SPTrans abre licitação para compra de 3,1 milhões de "cartões sem contato", 24 June 2021; read again 17 November 2026 in a browser](https://diariodotransporte.com.br/2021/06/24/sptrans-abre-licitacao-para-compra-de-31-milhoes-de-cartoes-sem-contato-para-o-sistema-bilhete-unico/)).

Fraud has been a public matter. In January 2017 the São Paulo city government reported that the Polícia Civil had arrested sellers of fares below the official price at the Lapa and Tatuapé stations, that 27 inquiries had been opened, that 9,904 Bilhete Único cards had been blocked in the first 11 days of January "por suspeita de irregularidades" (on suspicion of irregularities), and that it estimated the losses from the sale of "créditos não-oficiais" (unofficial credit) at R$150 million ([Prefeitura de São Paulo, Operação da Polícia Civil prende quadrilha por fraude no Bilhete Único, 13 January 2017](https://prefeitura.sp.gov.br/w/noticia/operacao-da-policia-civil-prende-quadrilha-por-fraude-no-bilhete-unico)). The city's notices describe unofficial credit and fraudulent cards and tell users to buy only at accredited points; they do not describe the technique ([Prefeitura de São Paulo, Evite fraude em compra de crédito do Bilhete Único, 12 January 2017](https://prefeitura.sp.gov.br/w/noticia/evite-fraude-em-compra-de-credito-do-bilhete-unico)). Neither notice says that the 2017 fraud exploited the weaknesses of MIFARE Classic.

### The law

Brazilian criminal law reaches card cloning through several articles of the Código Penal. Lei 12.737 of 30 November 2012, known as the Lei Carolina Dieckmann ([Lei Carolina Dieckmann](/glossary/lei-carolina-dieckmann)), created article 154-A, invasion of a computing device, whose first paragraph also punishes anyone who "produz, oferece, distribui, vende ou difunde dispositivo ou programa de computador com o intuito de permitir a prática da conduta definida no caput" (produces, offers, distributes, sells or spreads a device or computer program intended to allow the conduct defined in the main clause), and added to article 298 a sole paragraph: "equipara-se a documento particular o cartão de crédito ou débito" (a credit or debit card is equated to a private document) ([Senado Federal, Lei nº 12.737, de 30/11/2012, original publication](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/588113/publicacao/15773241)). Lei 14.155 of 27 May 2021 rewrote article 154-A's main clause, raised its penalty to "reclusão, de 1 (um) a 4 (quatro) anos, e multa", and created article 155's paragraph 4-B, which punishes theft by fraud "cometido por meio de dispositivo eletrônico ou informático" with four to eight years ([Câmara dos Deputados, Lei nº 14.155, de 27 de maio de 2021, original publication](https://www2.camara.leg.br/legin/fed/lei/2021/lei-14155-27-maio-2021-791396-publicacaooriginal-162901-pl.html)). The article 298 rule names credit and debit cards, not access badges or transit cards. In the United States, 18 U.S.C. 1029 punishes whoever "knowingly and with intent to defraud produces, uses, or traffics in one or more counterfeit access devices" ([18 U.S.C. 1029, Legal Information Institute, read 16 November 2026](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1029)). How these rules apply to owning and selling the tools themselves is the subject of a later part of this series on owning hacking gear legally.

### The shops

The tools are not sold into Brazil by their main European distributor: Lab401's FAQ lists Brazil among the countries it does not ship to, "Due to logistical or legal restrictions" ([Lab401, FAQ, read 16 November 2026](https://lab401.com/pages/faq)). The bands the tools use are lawful for licensed products: Anatel's restricted-radiation rules provide for RFID systems at 119 to 135 kHz and in parts of 13.11 to 14.01 MHz, among other bands ([Anatel, Ato 14.448, item 13.1](https://informacoes.anatel.gov.br/legislacao/resolucoes/2017/1139-resolucao-680)), and homologation is a prerequisite for use and sale in the country ([Anatel, Resolução nº 715/2019, art. 55](https://informacoes.anatel.gov.br/legislacao/resolucoes/2019/1350-resolucao-715)).

## What defenders can do

The research above converges on a short list, each item from a source that tested it.

- **Retire ID-only cards.** A 125 kHz badge carries a number, not a secret; Westhues's line between ID-only tags, which can be cloned by replay, and tags "that uses cryptographic techniques" is the line to cross ([Jonathan Westhues, proxmarkii](https://cq.cx/proxmarkii.pl)).
- **Retire MIFARE Classic, not just patch it.** NXP itself advised against designing MIFARE Classic into any security-relevant application and recommended MIFARE Plus or DESFire with AES ([NXP, 12 October 2015; link dead as of 17 November 2026](https://www.mifare.net/en/security-statement-on-crypto1-implementations/)); Meijer and Verdult showed that hardened Classic cards fall to a ciphertext-only attack that only a protocol change avoids ([Meijer and Verdult, CCS 2015](https://www.cs.ru.nl/~rverdult/Ciphertext-only_Cryptanalysis_on_Hardened_Mifare_Classic_Cards-CCS_2015.pdf)). Buy from a licensed supply chain: Teuwen's backdoor was in an unlicensed compatible chip ([Teuwen, ePrint 2024/1275](https://eprint.iacr.org/2024/1275)).
- **Never share one key across a whole system.** Radboud's 2008 release identified systems where all cards and readers share keys as "extremely vulnerable" and key diversification as harder to abuse; the iCLASS Standard master key shared by every reader made one extraction enough ([Radboud press release, 12 March 2008](https://sos.cs.ru.nl/applications/rfid/pressrelease.en.html); [Garcia et al., ESORICS 2012](https://www.cs.ru.nl/~rverdult/Dismantling_iClass_and_iClass_Elite-ESORICS_2012.pdf)).
- **Switch off legacy formats on the readers.** A strong card is only as strong as the weakest format the reader still accepts; the downgrade notes depend on readers left with iCLASS legacy enabled ([RRG, Notes on downgrade attacks](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/RfidResearchGroup/proxmark3/master/doc/hid_downgrade.md)).
- **Bound the reply time.** Against relay, Haskins and Stevado's mitigations reject cards that take too long to answer ([Haskins and Stevado, ePrint 2023/450](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/450)).
- **Do not let the card be the only factor.** Radboud's first advice in 2008 was that, with no technical countermeasure available, "Strengthening of traditional access control measures is therefore advisable", because sensitive sites should have several protections of which the card is one; it also noted that shielding a card in a metal sleeve reduces covert reading but not eavesdropping during use ([Radboud press release, 12 March 2008](https://sos.cs.ru.nl/applications/rfid/pressrelease.en.html)). Meijer and Verdult explained why card-only attacks matter most to integrators: they let the attacker "avoid camera detection" at the entrance ([Meijer and Verdult, CCS 2015](https://www.cs.ru.nl/~rverdult/Ciphertext-only_Cryptanalysis_on_Hardened_Mifare_Classic_Cards-CCS_2015.pdf)).
- **For NFC payments, guard the phone.** ESET's prevention list for NGate includes checking a website's authenticity, installing apps only from official stores, keeping the PIN secret, "Turning off the NFC function on devices when it's not needed", using RFID-blocking sleeves, and using virtual cards protected by biometrics ([ESET Research, 22 August 2024](https://www.welivesecurity.com/en/eset-research/ngate-android-malware-relays-nfc-traffic-to-steal-cash/)).

## What these tools cannot do

The Proxmark, the Chameleon and their cousins are powerful against the systems this article describes because those systems were weak. Against a card whose keys are diversified and whose cipher is sound, a device that can "talk to the tag, and do anything that its reader could" still cannot produce the secret it never sees ([Jonathan Westhues, proxmarkii](https://cq.cx/proxmarkii.pl)). The Proxmark repository's own notes say there are no card-only attack vectors against iCLASS SE and Seos credentials ([RRG, Notes on downgrade attacks](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/RfidResearchGroup/proxmark3/master/doc/hid_downgrade.md)); the published attacks on those systems go around the cryptography, by relay or by a reader that accepts a weaker format. The ChameleonUltra's own table shows DESFire emulation supported only at low data rates ([RFID Research Group, ChameleonUltra whitepaper](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/wiki/RfidResearchGroup/ChameleonUltra/technical_whitepaper.md)), and the NGate campaigns needed a victim to install malware and hold a card to the phone ([ESET Research, 22 August 2024](https://www.welivesecurity.com/en/eset-research/ngate-android-malware-relays-nfc-traffic-to-steal-cash/)). Range is physical: the RDV4's published read distances are 70 and 88 millimetres ([Proxmark.com, RDV4](https://proxmark.com/proxmark-3-hardware/proxmark-3-rdv4)).

## What to remember

- Passive cards take their power from the reader's field and answer whoever produces it: LF at about 125 kHz for older badges and car immobilizers, HF at 13.56 MHz for ISO/IEC 14443, ISO/IEC 15693 and NFC (ISO/IEC 18092).
- A 26-bit HID Prox badge carries an 8-bit facility code and a 16-bit card number between two parity bits; there is no secret to steal, only a number to copy.
- The Proxmark began as Jonathan Westhues's open design (proxmarkii 2005, proxmark3 2007) and lives on in RRG's Iceman fork, the RDV4 (2018) and the Proxmark 5 (2026, firmware in beta).
- MIFARE Classic, on the market since 1995, uses the 48-bit CRYPTO1 cipher; it was reverse-engineered in 2007 and 2008, NXP's bid to stop publication was rejected on 18 July 2008, and keys fall in under a second, card-only since 2009, ciphertext-only on hardened cards since 2015, with a backdoor found in an unlicensed compatible chip in 2024.
- iCLASS shared a master key across Standard cards; Seos was relayed over 1,960 km; downgrade attacks depend on readers that still accept legacy formats.
- In Brazil, São Paulo's Bilhete Único launched on MIFARE Classic in 2004 and moved to MIFARE Plus from 2013; Lei 12.737/2012 and Lei 14.155/2021 set the criminal rules, and NFC relay malware has targeted Brazilian users since 2025.
