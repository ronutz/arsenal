Parentheses are where regex stops being a search string and starts being a small parser. They group sub-patterns, capture what those sub-patterns matched, and, with a `?` prefix, switch into special modes that assert rather than consume.

## Capturing groups

Wrap part of a pattern in parentheses and you get a **capturing group**: the matched text is set aside under a number, counting groups left to right by their opening parenthesis. In `(\d{4})-(\d{2})`, group 1 captures the year and group 2 the month, so against `2026-06` you get `2026` and `06` back separately. Capturing is what lets a tool hand you the *parts* of a match, not just the whole thing.

Sometimes you only want the grouping (to apply a quantifier to several characters at once) without paying for a capture. A **non-capturing group** `(?:...)` does exactly that. `(?:ab)+` repeats the pair `ab` one or more times but creates no numbered capture, which keeps your group numbers meaningful and your matching a touch leaner.

A group that takes no part in a match is not the same as a group that matched nothing. In JavaScript, `/^user=(\w+)(?: ip=([\d.]+))?$/` run against `user=ana` matches, and group 2 comes back as `undefined`, because the optional part never ran; `/(a*)b/` run against `b` gives group 1 as the empty string, because that group ran and matched zero characters. The specification draws the line exactly there: a capture that did not participate is `undefined` ([ECMA-262, RegExpBuiltinExec](https://tc39.es/ecma262/multipage/text-processing.html#sec-regexpbuiltinexec)). Code that quietly turns the first case into a default value writes information into the result that the input never contained.

## Named groups

Counting parentheses gets fragile as patterns grow. A **named group** `(?<year>\d{4})` gives the capture a label, so you read `year` instead of remembering it was group 1. Named groups are clearer to maintain and survive edits that would shift numeric positions. Most modern engines, including JavaScript, support them.

## Backreferences

A **backreference** matches the same text a group already captured. `\1` refers to the first group; `\k<year>` refers to a named one. This is how you catch repetition: `(\w+)\s+\1` finds a doubled word like "the", because `\1` must match whatever `(\w+)` just matched. A backreference matches the captured *text*, not the pattern again, a subtle but important distinction.

## Lookarounds: asserting without consuming

A **lookaround** checks that something is or is not nearby, then throws the position back so the characters it inspected are still available to the rest of the pattern. There are four:

- `(?=...)` is a positive lookahead: *followed by*. `\d+(?= USD)` matches a number only when ` USD` comes next, but the match itself is just the digits.
- `(?!...)` is a negative lookahead: *not followed by*.
- `(?<=...)` is a positive lookbehind: *preceded by*. `(?<=\$)\d+` matches digits that sit right after a dollar sign.
- `(?<!...)` is a negative lookbehind: *not preceded by*.

Because lookarounds consume nothing, you can stack several at one position, a common trick for password rules, where each rule ("contains a digit", "contains an uppercase letter") becomes its own lookahead anchored at the start.

## A word on alternation

The pipe `|` is alternation: *either side*. It has very low precedence, so `cat|dog` means "cat" or "dog", but `^cat|dog$` probably does not mean what you expect; it reads as "starts with cat" or "ends with dog". Wrap the alternatives in a group, `^(cat|dog)$`, to bound them. Test these live in the [Regex Toolkit](/tools/regex), and when a clever pattern starts running slowly, read why in [catastrophic backtracking](/learn/regex-catastrophic-backtracking).
