A network survives a failure only when a second way to do the same job exists, notices that the first one has gone, takes over in time, and was not broken by the same cause. Each of those four conditions has a price and a way to fail, and the published reports on large outages show that the fourth one, the shared cause, is where redundancy most often stops working. This article opens a series of ten. It defines redundancy in the words of the standards, maps the failure domains from a power supply to a cloud region, walks the layers in which networks add redundancy with the time each layer takes to notice a failure, and reads twelve outages through the operators' own post-mortems and regulators' reports. After reading, you can place any redundancy mechanism in its layer and its failure domain, estimate the time a failover takes from its timers, and ask the questions that find a shared fate before an outage does.

## What redundancy is, in the standards' words

The vocabulary that the International Organization for Standardization (ISO), the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) and the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) publish together, ISO/IEC/IEEE 24765:2017, defines redundancy, "in fault tolerance", as "the presence of auxiliary components in a system to perform the same or similar functions as other elements for the purpose of preventing or recovering from failures" ([IEEE Computer Society and ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 7, SEVOCAB, redundancy](https://pascal.computer.org/sev_display/index.action)). The same vocabulary separates the ways of doing it. Active redundancy runs the redundant elements simultaneously; standby redundancy leaves them inoperative until a primary element fails; homogeneous redundancy realizes the same function with identical means, such as two identical processors; and diversity realizes it by different means, such as different processors, storage media, programming languages, algorithms or development teams ([SEVOCAB, redundancy, active redundancy, standby redundancy, homogeneous redundancy, diversity](https://pascal.computer.org/sev_display/index.action)).

The International Telecommunication Union's Telecommunication Standardization Sector (ITU-T) defines what redundancy is for. Recommendation E.800 calls availability the ability of an item "to be in a state to perform a required function at a given instant of time or at any instant of time within a given time interval, assuming that the external resources, if required, are provided", and reliability "The probability that an item can perform a required function under stated conditions for a given time interval" ([ITU-T E.800 (09/2008), sections 3.1.1.5.4 and 3.1.1.5.5](https://www.itu.int/rec/dologin_pub.asp?lang=e&id=T-REC-E.800-200809-I!!PDF-E&type=items)). The clause about external resources matters: an availability figure for a router says nothing about the power feed it assumes.

Transmission engineers name the arrangements by how many spares protect how many working paths. Request for Comments (RFC) 4427 of the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) defines 1+1 protection as one working and one protection path with "a permanent bridge", the traffic sent down both and the better copy selected at the far end; 1:N as N working paths sharing one protection path that may carry extra traffic while idle; and M:N as N working paths sharing M protection paths ([RFC 4427, April 2006, sections 6.1 to 6.3](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4427.txt)). The data-centre shorthand of N+1 and 2N reads the same way, and [the next part of this series](/learn/the-arithmetic-of-redundancy-availability-mtbf-and-shared-fate) works through what each buys.

The Internet itself began with a deliberate choice about where not to put redundancy. David Clark's account of the design philosophy of the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) protocols explains that state about a connection could have been replicated inside the network's switching nodes, but the architecture gathered it at the endpoints instead, an approach he called "fate-sharing": "it is acceptable to lose the state information associated with an entity if, at the same time, the entity itself is lost" ([Clark, The Design Philosophy of the DARPA Internet Protocols, SIGCOMM 1988, reprinted 1995](http://ccr.sigcomm.org/archive/1995/jan95/ccr-9501-clark.pdf)). He gave two reasons: fate-sharing protects against any number of intermediate failures where replication protects against fewer than the number of copies, and "fate-sharing is much easier to engineer than replication" (same paper). Stateless routers are the reason a routed core can lose a router and keep every Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) connection that crossed it, and the stateful devices of the later layers are where that property ends.

## What redundancy buys, and what it costs

Google's site reliability engineers describe the cost curve from their own operations: "an incremental improvement in reliability may cost 100x more than the previous increment", paid in redundant machines and in the opportunity cost of engineers building reliability instead of features ([Google, Site Reliability Engineering, chapter 3, Embracing Risk](https://sre.google/sre-book/embracing-risk/)). The same chapter sets a ceiling on what users notice: "a user on a 99% reliable smartphone cannot tell the difference between 99.99% and 99.999% service reliability!", and treats an availability target "as both a minimum and a maximum".

The second cost is complexity. RFC 3439 states the Simplicity Principle, that "complexity is the primary mechanism which impedes efficient scaling", and quotes a warning that applies to every redundancy mechanism: "complexity added for robustness also adds new fragilities, which in turn leads to new and thus spiraling complexities" ([RFC 3439, December 2002, sections 2 and 9](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc3439.txt)). Its Coupling Principle adds that "as things get larger, they often exhibit increased interdependence between components" (section 2.2.2), which is the arithmetic's independence assumption failing at scale.

The measurements agree that redundancy helps and that it helps less than its diagrams suggest:

| Study | What was measured | What redundancy did |
|---|---|---|
| [Gill, Jain and Nagappan, SIGCOMM 2011](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2011/papers/sigcomm/p350.pdf) | Over a year of network error logs from thousands of devices in a large operator's data centres (two authors from Microsoft Research) | Median traffic carried during a link failure was 93% across the redundancy group against 65% on the failed link, so redundancy is "only 40% effective in reducing the median impact of failure" |
| [Oppenheimer, Ganapathi and Patterson, USITS 2003](https://www.usenix.org/legacy/events/usits03/tech/full_papers/oppenheimer/oppenheimer.pdf) | Failures of three large Internet services | Redundancy masked most hardware, software and network component failures, but "Fully half of the 36 operator errors resulted in service failure" |
| [Meza and others, IMC 2018](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/imc/2018/papers/imc18-final179.pdf) | Seven years of Facebook's data centre network incidents | Most failures that software could not repair involved maintenance, faulty hardware and misconfiguration, with "2× more human errors than hardware errors" |
| [Govindan and others, SIGCOMM 2016](https://research.google/pubs/evolve-or-die-high-availability-design-principles-drawn-from-googles-network-infrastructure/) | Over 100 high-impact failure events in Google's network | Failures were spread across data, control and management planes, and "a large number of failures happen when a network management operation is in progress" |

Gill and his co-authors give the reasons in their own words: bugs in failover mechanisms when it is unclear which link is the backup, redundant components that were not configured correctly, and once "the same configuration error made on both the primary and back up of a network connection because of a typo in the configuration script" ([Gill and others, section 5.1](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2011/papers/sigcomm/p350.pdf)). That sentence is the whole subject of this series in miniature: two boxes, one mistake.

## Failure domains, from a power supply to a region

A failure domain is the set of things that one fault takes down together. The cloud providers publish theirs. Amazon Web Services (AWS) describes an Availability Zone (AZ) as one or more data centres "with independent and redundant power infrastructure, networking, and connectivity", placed up to 60 miles (about 100 km) from the others "to prevent correlated failures", and designed to avoid "shared fate scenarios such as utility power, water disruption, fiber isolation, earthquakes, fires, tornadoes, or floods"; generators and cooling are not shared, zones are fed from different substations, and deployments to zones in one region are "separated in time to prevent correlated failure" ([AWS, AWS Fault Isolation Boundaries, Availability Zones](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/whitepapers/latest/aws-fault-isolation-boundaries/availability-zones.html)). Microsoft says its zones are "typically separated by several kilometers, and usually are within 100 kilometers of each other" ([Microsoft Learn, What are availability zones?, 11 February 2026](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/reliability/availability-zones-overview)). Google tells its customers that resources in different zones reduce the risk of one infrastructure outage taking all of them, and that regions provide "an even higher degree of failure independence" across "different failure domains" ([Google Cloud, Regions and zones, last updated 10 November 2026](https://cloud.google.com/compute/docs/regions-zones)).

The IETF's survivability vocabulary has the same idea for transport networks. RFC 3386 defines a shared risk group as "a set of network elements that are collectively impacted by a specific fault or fault type", including shared conduit, shared fibre cable, shared right of way, shared optical ring and shared office ([RFC 3386, November 2002, section 2.2.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc3386.txt)); RFC 4202 turns it into a routing attribute, the shared risk link group (SRLG), with the example that "two fibers in the same conduit would be in the same SRLG" ([RFC 4202, October 2005, section 2.3](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4202.txt)).

## How a failover happens, and how long it takes

RFC 5714 breaks a routed network's recovery into five steps, and the breakdown holds for most redundancy mechanisms: detecting the failure, "of the order of a few milliseconds when it can be detected at the physical layer, up to several tens of seconds when a routing protocol Hello is employed"; the local router reacting; passing the news to other routers, "typically between 10 milliseconds and 100 milliseconds per hop"; recomputing, "typically a few milliseconds for a link state protocol"; and loading the new forwarding tables into hardware, which "may be several hundred milliseconds" ([RFC 5714, January 2010, section 4](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5714.txt)). The packets sent towards the failed component until detection are "unavoidably lost" (same section).

Detection is the step each layer configures, and its defaults differ by three orders of magnitude:

| Layer | Mechanism | Default or suggested timers | Time to declare the failure | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Layer 2 | Link Aggregation Control Protocol (LACP), slow rate | One message every 30 s, three missed | 90 s | [Extreme Networks, SLX-OS, LACP timeout](https://documentation.extremenetworks.com/slxos/SW/20xx/l2config/GUID-67DF739B-2115-4055-92BE-D1DB02F72B70.shtml); Linux's default is slow ([Linux kernel, bonding](https://docs.kernel.org/networking/bonding.html)) |
| Layer 2 | LACP, fast rate | One message a second, three missed | 3 s | same pages |
| Layer 2 | Spanning Tree Protocol (STP), IEEE 802.1D | Forward delay 15 s, twice | 30 s of disruption in Cisco's example | [Cisco, Understand Rapid Spanning Tree Protocol (802.1w), updated 9 February 2023](https://www.cisco.com/c/en/us/support/docs/lan-switching/spanning-tree-protocol/24062-146.html) |

| First hop | Hot Standby Router Protocol (HSRP) | Hellotime 3 s recommended; Holdtime at least three times the Hellotime | 9 s or more | [RFC 2281, March 1998, section 5.1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2281.txt) |
| Layer 3 | Bidirectional Forwarding Detection (BFD), FRRouting defaults | 300 ms intervals, multiplier 3 | 900 ms | [FRRouting, BFD](https://docs.frrouting.org/en/latest/bfd.html) |
| Layer 3 | BFD, RFC 5880's aggressive example | 16.7 ms, multiplier 3 | 50 ms | [RFC 5880, June 2010, section 7](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5880.txt) |

| Stateful | FortiGate Clustering Protocol (FGCP) heartbeat | 200 ms between heartbeats by default; 20 lost heartbeats by default (hb-lost-threshold), configurable | 2 × 100 ms × 20 = **4 s** by default | [Fortinet, Technical Tip: Interval between HA heartbeats, updated 7 September 2026](https://community.fortinet.com/fortigate-3/technical-tip-interval-between-ha-heartbeats-97919); [Fortinet, FortiOS 7.6.6 Administration Guide, HA heartbeat interface](https://docs.fortinet.com/document/fortigate/7.6.6/administration-guide/849059/ha-heartbeat-interface) |

The VRRP row is computed from RFC 9568's own formulas, `Skew_Time = ((256 - Priority) * Active_Adver_Interval) / 256` and `Active_Down_Interval = (3 * Active_Adver_Interval) + Skew_Time`, in centiseconds: a Backup at priority 100 waits 300 + 60.9375 = 360.9375 centiseconds, which is 3.6094 seconds, inside the RFC's promise of convergence in under 4 seconds by default and under 1/25 of a second when configured for it ([RFC 9568, sections 3 and 6.1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9568.txt)). The [VRRP timer calculator](/tools/vrrp-timer-calculator) and the [BFD timer calculator](/tools/bfd-timers) do the same arithmetic for any configuration, and the BFD articles explain [why a link can be up and dead](/learn/bfd-when-a-link-is-up-and-dead) and [how the detection time is negotiated](/learn/bfd-timers-the-arithmetic-of-detection).

Two readings of the table matter for design. Detection is only the first of RFC 5714's five steps, so a 50 ms BFD session does not make a 50 ms failover if the forwarding table takes hundreds of milliseconds to reload. And every failover spends some of the availability budget: at OSPF's sample timers, twelve failovers a year cost 480 seconds of loss before anything else happens, which the [arithmetic part](/learn/the-arithmetic-of-redundancy-availability-mtbf-and-shared-fate) turns into nines.

## The layers, in order

Each layer below has a part of its own in this series; the sections here say what fails at that layer, what answers it, how long the answer takes, and which existing articles go deeper.

### Physical and power

Everything above depends on electricity, cooling and glass. The AWS description of its zones lists the shared fates it designs against, from utility power and water to fibre isolation, fire and flood ([AWS, Availability Zones](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/whitepapers/latest/aws-fault-isolation-boundaries/availability-zones.html)). Brazil's telecommunications regulator, the Agência Nacional de Telecomunicações (Anatel), added a title on data centres that form part of telecommunications networks to its conformity assessment regulation in August 2025: the operational procedure is to require, among other things, that they "operar continuamente, mesmo em situações de falhas, eventos adversos ou desastres" (operate continuously, even in failures, adverse events or disasters), and the agency's Board suspended the effects of article 6 of the amending resolution on 3 March 2026 while it re-examines the matter ([Anatel, Resolução nº 715/2019, Title VI-A, as amended by Resolução nº 780 of 1 August 2025](https://informacoes.anatel.gov.br/legislacao/resolucoes/2019/1350-resolucao-717)).

The management network belongs to this layer too, because it is how people reach the equipment when everything else is down. Meta's engineers found in October 2021 that "out-of-band network access was down" and had to send engineers to the data centres ([Meta, More details about the October 4 outage, 5 October 2021](https://engineering.fb.com/2021/10/05/networking-traffic/outage-details/)); the Canadian Radio-television and Telecommunications Commission (CRTC) was told that Rogers "had a management network that relied on the Rogers IP core network", the network that had failed ([CRTC, Assessment of Rogers Networks for Resiliency and Reliability Following the 8 July 2022 Outage, Executive Summary, July 2024](https://crtc.gc.ca/eng/publications/reports/xona2024.htm)). Dual power supplies, A and B feeds, uninterruptible power supplies, diverse conduits and carriers, out-of-band management and the facility ratings are the subject of [part 3](/learn/physical-and-power-redundancy-feeds-paths-and-carriers); the seabed is in [Submarine cables: the internet is a rope on the seabed](/learn/submarine-cables-and-the-physical-internet).

### Layer 2

Two switches joined by two cables form a loop, and Ethernet has two answers: bundle the cables or block one. A link aggregation group (LAG) lets a media access control (MAC) client treat parallel links "as if it were a single link", and IEEE 802.1AX-2020 extends the idea to a Distributed Resilient Network Interconnect among "one to three nodes in a network and one to three nodes in another" ([IEEE 802.1AX-2020 project page, approved 30 January 2020](https://1.ieee802.org/tsn/802-1ax-rev/)). Spanning tree keeps the loop by blocking a port until it is needed, at the timers in the table above. Ethernet virtual private network (EVPN) multihoming replaces both inside a fabric: RFC 7432 lets a segment attach to several provider edge devices in Single-Active mode, one forwarding per virtual local area network (VLAN), or in All-Active mode, all of them forwarding known unicast traffic ([RFC 7432, February 2015, section 3](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7432.txt)).

The existing articles cover the mechanisms: [link aggregation and LACP](/learn/link-aggregation-and-lacp), [spanning tree in one article](/learn/spanning-tree-in-one-article), [split multi-link trunking (SMLT) and the virtual inter-switch trunk (vIST) on Extreme's fabric edge](/learn/voss-smlt-and-vist) and [FortiSwitch spanning tree](/learn/fortiswitch-vlans-stp-ports-and-qos). The [LACP member link and state reader](/tools/lacp-hash-and-state-reader) shows which flows move when a member fails, and the [spanning tree election calculator](/tools/stp-election-calculator) which port blocks. Multi-chassis aggregation, stacking and the loop protections are [part 4](/learn/layer-2-redundancy-lag-mlag-and-spanning-tree).

### The first hop

A host sends everything off its subnet to one address, and RFC 9568 states what losing it means: "Loss of the default router results in a catastrophic event, isolating all end-hosts that are unable to detect an available alternate path" ([RFC 9568, section 1.3](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9568.txt)). First-hop redundancy protocols move a virtual address and a virtual MAC address between routers so that no host has to notice, in the 3.6 seconds of VRRPv3's defaults or the 9 seconds and more of HSRP's. [First-hop redundancy: VRRP, HSRP, and the gateway that is a fiction](/learn/first-hop-redundancy-vrrp-and-hsrp) explains the election, the timers and the attacks; [part 5](/learn/default-gateway-redundancy-vrrpv3-glbp-and-anycast-gateways) takes the subject to scale, with load sharing, tracking, IPv6 and the anycast gateways of EVPN fabrics.

### Layer 3

Routing protocols were built to route around failure, and their own Hellos are slow by design: 40 seconds for OSPF at the sample values and 90 for BGP at the suggested hold time. Equal-cost multipath (ECMP) spreads traffic over several next hops by hashing, and RFC 2992 measures the price of a change in the set of paths as "disruption", the fraction of flows that move ([RFC 2992, November 2000, sections 1 and 2.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2992.txt)). BFD shortens detection, and IP fast reroute shortens the rest: backup routes computed in advance let the router that detects the failure repair it locally "without the immediate need to inform other routers", so that "the disruption time can be limited to the small time taken to detect the adjacent failure and invoke the backup routes" ([RFC 5714, section 1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5714.txt)); loop-free alternates are the basic form of those backup routes ([RFC 5286, September 2008](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5286.txt)). The primers on [OSPF](/learn/ospf-primer) and [BGP](/learn/bgp-primer), the two BFD articles and [Anycast: one address in many places](/learn/anycast) are the background; [part 6](/learn/layer-3-redundancy-ecmp-bfd-and-fast-reroute) covers ECMP hashing, graceful restart, loop-free alternates and BGP's convergence.

### Stateful devices

Firewalls, load balancers and address translators keep a table of connections, so the stateless router's free failover ends here: a standby that does not have the table drops the connections, and two units that each believe they are active forward the same addresses at once. Fortinet sends heartbeats every 200 milliseconds by default and leaves the failure verdict to a configurable number of missed heartbeats ([Fortinet, Interval between HA heartbeats](https://community.fortinet.com/fortigate-3/technical-tip-interval-between-ha-heartbeats-97919)), 20 by default, so a member is declared dead after 4 seconds ([Fortinet, FortiOS 7.6.6 Administration Guide, HA heartbeat interface](https://docs.fortinet.com/document/fortigate/7.6.6/administration-guide/849059/ha-heartbeat-interface)). The vendor articles describe each implementation: [FortiGate FGCP high availability](/learn/fortigate-fgcp-ha-clustering), [BIG-IP device trust, device groups and traffic groups](/learn/bigip-ha-concepts-device-trust-groups), [BIG-IP failover states](/learn/bigip-failover-states-and-operations), [persistence mirroring across a BIG-IP pair](/learn/persistence-mirroring-and-ha), [Check Point ElasticXL](/learn/checkpoint-elasticxl-cluster) and [Check Point management high availability](/learn/checkpoint-management-high-availability). The [FortiGate HA failover simulator](/tools/fortigate-ha-failover-simulator) plays an election. [Part 7](/learn/stateful-clusters-active-standby-active-active-and-split-brain) compares them, with what is and is not synchronized, asymmetric traffic and [split brain](/glossary/split-brain).

### Application delivery and global load balancing

Above the network, an application delivery controller (ADC) decides which servers are alive by probing them, and global server load balancing (GSLB) decides which site a client reaches, usually by answering DNS queries. The probe is the whole system: [How BIG-IP Local Traffic Manager health monitors decide up or down](/learn/ltm-health-monitors) and [Load balancing: what actually decides where a request goes](/learn/load-balancing-what-actually-decides-where-a-request-goes) explain why. DNS-based failover waits for caches, because a resolver may keep an answer for its time to live, up to 2,147,483,647 seconds under RFC 2181 ([RFC 2181, section 8](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2181.txt)); AWS's account of October 2025 shows that once a corrected record existed, customers reconnected "as cached DNS records expired" ([AWS, Summary of the Amazon DynamoDB Service Disruption in the Northern Virginia (US-EAST-1) Region, October 2025](https://aws.amazon.com/message/101925/)). [GSLB in two tiers](/learn/gslb-two-tier-pool-then-member) and the [BIG-IP DNS GSLB simulator](/tools/bigip-dns-gslb-simulator) show the decision; [part 8](/learn/adc-and-gslb-redundancy-health-monitors-pools-and-site-failover) covers monitors' false verdicts, priority groups, draining and anycast against DNS.

### Data and services

Copies of data are redundancy with a consistency problem. Consensus protocols such as Raft stay available "as long as any majority of the servers are operational and can communicate", so "a typical cluster of five servers can tolerate the failure of any two servers" ([Ongaro and Ousterhout, In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm, extended version, 2014, section 1](https://raft.github.io/raft.pdf)). Replication can also turn a small failure into a large one: in April 2011 a mistaken network change left many of Amazon's Elastic Block Store (EBS) nodes without either network, and when connectivity returned they "rapidly began searching the EBS cluster for available server space where they could re-mirror data", a "re-mirroring storm" that left about 13% of the volumes in the zone stuck ([AWS, Summary of the Amazon EC2 and Amazon RDS Service Disruption in the US East Region, 29 April 2011](https://aws.amazon.com/message/65648/)). A replica copies a deletion as faithfully as a write, which is why [backups are a separate control](/learn/backups-and-ransomware-recovery). [Part 9](/learn/data-and-service-redundancy-replication-quorum-rpo-and-rto) covers synchronous and asynchronous replication, [quorum](/glossary/quorum), and the [recovery point objective (RPO) and the recovery time objective (RTO)](/glossary/rto-rpo).

### Operations

The last layer is the people and the tools that change everything else, and the outage table shows it is where most of the large failures began. Google's design principles drawn from its own failures include "defense in depth, maintaining consistency across planes, failing open on large failures, carefully preventing and avoiding failures, and assessing root cause quickly" ([Govindan and others, Evolve or Die, SIGCOMM 2016](https://research.google/pubs/evolve-or-die-high-availability-design-principles-drawn-from-googles-network-infrastructure/)). The existing operations articles are the practical side: [blast-radius thinking before a change](/learn/blast-radius-thinking-before-you-change-anything), [change windows that do not become incidents](/learn/change-windows-that-do-not-become-incidents), [the first hour of fault isolation](/learn/fault-isolation-first-hour), [reviewing a network architecture before approving it](/learn/review-a-network-architecture-before-approving-it), [the hidden assumptions in an architecture diagram](/learn/hidden-assumptions-in-an-architecture-diagram) and [what belongs in a post-incident technical review](/learn/what-belongs-in-a-post-incident-technical-review). The [change blast-radius mapper](/tools/change-blast-radius-mapper) and the [flow path reasoner](/tools/flow-path-reasoner) structure the same questions. Each row below rests on one report written by the operator or by a regulator or parliament. The lesson in the last column is the one the report itself draws or the remedy it commits to, not a verdict of this article. Times are as each report gives them.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| 21 April 2011 | AWS, US East, one zone | A change to upgrade the primary network's capacity shifted traffic onto the lower-capacity redundant EBS network instead of the other primary router; "this change disconnected both the primary and secondary network simultaneously"; 0.07% of the zone's volumes could not be restored | A redundant path sized for less than the primary's load is not a backup for it, and an automatic recovery (re-mirroring) can multiply the load that broke the system | [AWS, 29 April 2011](https://aws.amazon.com/message/65648/) |
| 28 February 2017 | AWS, Simple Storage Service (S3), US-EAST-1 | A playbook command with one input "entered incorrectly" removed more servers than intended; index and placement subsystems needed full restarts that had not been done in the larger regions "for many years"; 09:37 to 13:54 Pacific Standard Time (PST) | The tool now removes capacity more slowly and refuses to take a subsystem below its minimum capacity; recovery itself must be exercised | [AWS, S3 summary](https://aws.amazon.com/message/41926/) |
| 10 March 2021 | OVHcloud, Strasbourg | A fire at the Strasbourg site, whose buildings OVHcloud numbers SBG1 to SBG4; SBG2 powered down and secured, the SBG1 building being dismantled, SBG3 back in operation from 18 March; replacement servers offered in other data centres | A campus is one failure domain for fire; the company's third priority was "Implementing all DRP (Disaster Recovery Plan) mechanisms with our customers" | [OVHcloud, Fire at our Strasbourg Site, update of 2 April 2021](https://us.ovhcloud.com/press/press-releases/2021/fire-our-strasbourg-site) |
| 8 June 2021 | Fastly | A software deployment of 12 May carried a bug that a valid customer configuration change triggered, causing 85% of the network to return errors; detected within one minute, 95% normal within 49 minutes | The same software on every node makes every node one failure domain, however many there are | [Fastly, Summary of June 8 outage](https://www.fastly.com/blog/summary-of-june-8-outage) |
| 4 October 2021 | Meta (Facebook) | A command meant to assess backbone capacity took down all backbone connections; a bug in the audit tool failed to stop it; DNS servers withdrew their BGP routes; out-of-band access was down | The safety check was a single point of failure, and the management path shared the fate of the network it managed (BGP detail in [BGP and the routing chokepoint](/learn/bgp-and-the-routing-chokepoint)) | [Meta, 5 October 2021](https://engineering.fb.com/2021/10/05/networking-traffic/outage-details/) |

| 15 August 2023 | Brazil's interconnected grid, Operador Nacional do Sistema Elétrico (ONS) | The 500 kV line Quixadá to Fortaleza II tripped by protection action without a short circuit; the loss of a single line was followed by an abrupt voltage drop because wind and solar plants performed far below what ONS studies, run on the models the plants' owners supplied, had predicted; about 23,368 megawatts (MW) of load interrupted, full restoration authorized at 14:49 | A design that survives the loss of one element on paper depends on models that match the field | [ONS, RAP-ONS 00012/2023, final version of 18 October 2023](https://www.ons.org.br/AcervoDigitalDocumentosEPublicacoes/RAP%202023.08.15%2008h30min%20vers%c3%a3o%20final%20com%20anexos%20de%20diverg%c3%aancia_Final.pdf) |

| 19 July 2024 | CrowdStrike, Windows hosts worldwide | A content update for a new inter-process communication Template Type defined 21 input fields while the code supplied 20; Microsoft estimated 8.5 million Windows devices affected | One security agent on every redundant server is one software version across all of them | [CrowdStrike, Channel File 291 root cause analysis, 6 August 2024](https://www.crowdstrike.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/Channel-File-291-Incident-Root-Cause-Analysis-08.06.2024.pdf); [Microsoft, 20 July 2024](https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2024/07/20/helping-our-customers-through-the-crowdstrike-outage/) |

| 19 and 20 October 2025 | AWS, US-EAST-1 | A latent race between two of the DNS Enactors that run "redundantly and fully independently in three different Availability Zones" left an empty DNS record for DynamoDB's regional endpoint, 23:48 to 02:40 Pacific Daylight Time (PDT); Elastic Compute Cloud (EC2) instance launches were impaired until 13:50 | Redundant automation can race itself; independence of instances does not make their shared output independent | [AWS, October 2025](https://aws.amazon.com/message/101925/) |

Read down the third column and a pattern appears. Only one row, the fire, is the kind of physical loss that a second device or a second building is designed to absorb; the Brazilian row is a single physical loss that the design was supposed to absorb and did not. Nine of the other ten began with a change, a command, a configuration file or a software version that reached every redundant copy at once, and the tenth with redundant automation that raced itself; that is exactly the shared fate that the arithmetic of independent failures leaves out. Computed from the reports' own times, recovery ran from 49 minutes for 95% of Fastly's network to 26 hours 2 minutes for Rogers, and to 3 days 11 hours 43 minutes, from 00:47 on 21 April to 12:30 on 24 April 2011, before all but 1.04% of the affected EBS volumes had been recovered.

## Questions that find a shared fate

These questions are drawn from the cases above; each one names the report that prompted it.

- **What do the two copies share?** Power, conduit, chassis, software version, configuration source, credentials, management path. AWS lists its own answers for zones ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/whitepapers/latest/aws-fault-isolation-boundaries/availability-zones.html)); Rogers's management network shared the core ([CRTC](https://crtc.gc.ca/eng/publications/reports/xona2024.htm)).
- **Can one change reach both?** Staggered deployment is the defence that AWS applies to zones and that Google committed to for replicated data ([Google Cloud](https://status.cloud.google.com/incidents/ow5i3PPK96RduMcb1SsW)).
- **Can the standby carry the load?** The redundant EBS network could not ([AWS, 2011](https://aws.amazon.com/message/65648/)).
- **How long does detection take, and is that in the availability budget?** The timer table above answers the first half; [part 2](/learn/the-arithmetic-of-redundancy-availability-mtbf-and-shared-fate) answers the second.
- **Does recovery itself work at today's scale?** S3's subsystems had not been fully restarted in years ([AWS, 2017](https://aws.amazon.com/message/41926/)).
- **Do the protective mechanisms act alone or together?** Optus's routers each protected themselves, all at once ([Senate committee](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Senate/Environment_and_Communications/OptusNetworkOutage/Report/Chapter_2_-_The_Optus_network_outage)).
- **When was the failover last exercised?** That is [part 10](/learn/testing-redundancy-failure-drills-chaos-and-the-failover-never-tried).

## What to remember

- Redundancy is an auxiliary means of performing a function; it is active, standby, homogeneous or diverse, and only diversity protects against a fault that every identical copy shares.
- A failure domain is what one fault takes down together, from a power supply to a region; the cloud providers' zone definitions list the shared fates they design against.
- A failover is detection, reaction, propagation, recomputation and reprogramming; detection defaults range from 50 ms for an aggressive BFD session to 90 s for BGP and slow LACP.
- Stateless layers fail over for free; stateful devices need their state synchronized and can end up with two active units.
- Redundancy measurably helps, and measurably less than the diagrams promise: about 40% of the median impact removed in one study, half of operator errors unmasked in another.
- In twelve landmark outages, nine began with a change, a command, a file or a software version that reached every copy at once, and one with redundant automation racing itself; the other two were physical losses, a fire and a single transmission line.

<ReaderQuestions>

- If identical copies protect only against independent faults, and diverse copies double the engineering and testing, where should a network spend its diversity: hardware, software, operators or providers?
- Out-of-band management protects against the network failing, but it is also a second way into every device: how separate should it be, and who should be able to change both?
- Optus's routers each protected themselves correctly and together removed every path; should protective limits be staggered across redundant devices on purpose?
- A post-mortem that names a typo and a post-mortem that names the tool that accepted it describe the same outage: which description leads to the better fix, and why do operators so often publish the first?
- Should regulators set availability figures for networks, as Anatel once did for fixed broadband, or require published post-mortems, as Canada's regulator obtained from Rogers, and which would change designs more?
