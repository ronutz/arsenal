Two switches and two cables give a network something to fall back on, and at layer 2 they also give it a loop. There are only two ways out: block the extra path, which is what spanning tree does, or bundle the parallel links into one logical link, which is what link aggregation does. This part of the series assumes the mechanics of both, explained in [link aggregation and the Link Aggregation Control Protocol (LACP)](/learn/link-aggregation-and-lacp) and [spanning tree in one article](/learn/spanning-tree-in-one-article), and looks at them as redundancy: how long each takes to recover, what happens when the bundle has to end on two different switches, and what the standards bodies have offered in place of the vendors' pairs. After reading, you can predict how a design fails, compute its timers from the documents, and list what to test before trusting it.

## The two jobs, and the four designs

Every layer 2 design for redundancy has to do two things at once: keep a spare path, and stop that spare path from carrying a frame round in a circle. The Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) standards and the vendors' features answer the two jobs in four ways.

| Design | What keeps the spare path | What stops the loop | Who defines it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spanning Tree Protocol (STP), Rapid Spanning Tree Protocol (RSTP), Multiple Spanning Tree Protocol (MSTP) | A blocked port that can be unblocked | The tree itself | IEEE 802.1Q |
| Link aggregation group (LAG) to one switch | The other members of the bundle | The bundle is one logical port | IEEE 802.1AX |
| Multi-chassis link aggregation (MLAG) or a stack | A second switch that answers as the same partner | A forwarding rule on the link between the pair | Each vendor; IEEE 802.1AX defines its own version, DRNI |
| Ethernet virtual private network (EVPN) multihoming | Several provider edges on one Ethernet segment | Designated forwarder election and split-horizon filtering | Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF), Request for Comments (RFC) 7432 and its successors |

A LAG to a single switch protects against a cable or a port, not against the switch: Juniper's MC-LAG documentation puts the limit plainly, "The problem with standard link aggregation is that the connections are point to point" ([Juniper, understanding MC-LAG](https://www.juniper.net/documentation/us/en/software/junos/mc-lag/topics/concept/mc-lag-feature-summary-best-practices.html)). The rest of this article is about the designs that take the switch out of the list of single points of failure, and about the price each one charges for it.

## Spanning tree as a redundancy mechanism: the numbers

The timers that decide how fast spanning tree recovers are set in IEEE 802.1Q, and the IEEE's own data model for the protocol publishes the defaults with their ranges: a bridge maximum age of 20 seconds (6 to 40), a forward delay of 15 seconds (4 to 30) and a transmit hold count of 6 (1 to 10), while the hello time is "Set to 2 seconds if the Bridge is currently acting as the Root Bridge" ([IEEE 802.1, ieee802-dot1q-rstp YANG module, revision 2025-02-04](https://grouper.ieee.org/groups/802/1/files/public/YANGs/ieee802-dot1q-rstp.yang)). For MSTP, the same family of models sets MaxHops to 20 by default, in a range of 6 to 100 ([IEEE 802.1, ieee802-dot1q-mstp YANG module](https://grouper.ieee.org/groups/802/1/files/public/YANGs/ieee802-dot1q-mstp.yang)).

Those defaults are not arbitrary. Cisco's guide to the timers explains that "The IEEE recommendation is to consider a maximum diameter of seven bridges for the default STP timers", and gives two formulas that tie the timers to the diameter: max age = (4 × hello) + (2 × diameter) − 2, and forward delay = ((4 × hello) + (3 × diameter)) / 2 ([Cisco, understanding and tuning STP timers, document 19120, 2 February 2023](https://www.cisco.com/c/en/us/support/docs/lan-switching/spanning-tree-protocol/19120-122.html)). Worked with a hello of 2 seconds and a diameter of 7:

- max age = 4 × 2 + 2 × 7 − 2 = 8 + 14 − 2 = **20 seconds**, the default;
- forward delay = (4 × 2 + 3 × 7) / 2 = (8 + 21) / 2 = **14.5 seconds**, which the standard rounds up to the default of 15.

The same guide works a diameter of 4: a hello of 2 seconds gives a max age of 14 and a forward delay of 10, and a hello of 1 second gives 10 and 8 (Cisco, as above). Shorter timers are possible, but the guide warns that the conservative values "exist in order to prevent STP loops that can occur in stress conditions", such as a busy processor or an overloaded port, and that "you must consider these parameters as fixed values" unless they are recomputed from the formulas and set on the root and the backup root (Cisco, as above).

### What a failure costs in each version

| Failure | Classic STP (802.1D-1998 timers) | RSTP |
|---|---|---|
| The switch's own root port goes down and the link light goes out | 2 × forward delay = **30 s** of listening and learning | A proposal and agreement handshake on point-to-point links |
| A failure elsewhere, noticed only when information ages out | max age + 2 × forward delay = 20 + 30 = **50 s** | Three missed hellos: 3 × 2 s = **6 s** |
| A topology change | Address tables age out fast, then flood | Address tables are flushed, then flood |

The 30 and 50 seconds are explained, with Cisco's sources, in [spanning tree in one article](/learn/spanning-tree-in-one-article). The 6 seconds for a silent failure under RSTP follow from two sentences in Juniper's guide, where switches exchange bridge protocol data units (BPDUs): "the root bridge continues to send BPDUs at a hello time interval (default 2 seconds)", and "When a port does not receive a hello BPDU three times, and if the port is the root port, a complete rework of the spanning tree occurs" ([Juniper, Spanning-Tree Protocols User Guide, published 11 December 2025](https://www.juniper.net/documentation/us/en/software/junos/stp-l2/stp-l2.pdf)). A failure that turns the light off is noticed at once; a failure that leaves the light on, a stuck line card or a half-dead optic, costs three hellos.

Recovery is not over when the tree has converged, because the switches have forgotten where the hosts are. Juniper's guide spells out the second cost, the relearning of media access control (MAC) addresses: "Because a link failure causes all associated ports to flush their MAC address table, the network might be slower as it floods to relearn the MAC addresses" (Juniper, as above). A large flat VLAN pays it on every topology change, which is one reason to keep layer 2 domains small.

Which version a switch runs out of the box differs by vendor. Arista's manual states that "By default, Arista switches use MSTP" ([Arista, EOS 4.37.0F, spanning tree protocol](https://www.arista.com/en/um-eos/eos-spanning-tree-protocol)); Juniper's guide describes RSTP as the default switch configuration, recommended for most networks because it converges faster than STP after a failure (Juniper, as above); and Cisco's Catalyst 9300 defaults to Rapid PVST+ (per-VLAN spanning tree plus), Cisco's per-VLAN RSTP, as the [spanning tree article](/learn/spanning-tree-in-one-article) records. The [spanning tree election calculator](/tools/stp-election-calculator) shows which ports a given topology blocks, which is where any recovery starts.

## Where loops come from, and the guards against them

Spanning tree assumes that every bridge speaks it and that every link carries frames in both directions. Most layer 2 outages begin where one of those assumptions fails, and every vendor ships the same small set of guards against it, each off by default or limited in scope.

**A switch where a host should be.** Juniper's default is open: "By default, all BPDUs are accepted and processed on all interfaces", and BPDU protection changes that, so that "If a BPDU is received on a protected interface, the interface is disabled and stops forwarding frames" (Juniper, Spanning-Tree Protocols User Guide, as above). Arista's BPDU guard does the same and adds the operational detail that matters at three in the morning: "Disabled ports differ from blocked ports in that they are re-enabled only through manual intervention" (Arista, as above).

**A switch that wants to be root.** Arista's root guard "stops a port from becoming a root port, which stops connected switches from becoming root bridges" (Arista, as above), the same control the IEEE model calls restrictedRole, set "to prevent selection of the port as Root Port for the CIST or any MSTI", the common and internal spanning tree or any multiple spanning tree instance, even if it hears the best priority (IEEE 802.1, ieee802-dot1q-rstp YANG module, as above).

**A blocked port that stops hearing BPDUs.** This is the quietest failure and the most dangerous. Juniper describes it exactly: "a blocking interface can transition to the forwarding state in error if the interface stops receiving BPDUs from its designated port on the segment", and the default is the dangerous one, an interface that stops receiving BPDUs "will transition to the designated port (forwarding) state, creating a potential loop" (Juniper, as above). Loop protection (Juniper) or loop guard (Arista, Cisco) holds the port in a loop-inconsistent state instead; Arista sums up the purpose as "loop guard prevents loops resulting from unidirectional failure of point-to-point links" (Arista, as above).

**A link that works in one direction only.** The Unidirectional Link Detection protocol (UDLD) was published by the IETF as RFC 5171 in April 2008 with Informational status, a description of a Cisco protocol rather than a standard ([RFC Editor, RFC 5171 metadata](https://www.rfc-editor.org/info/rfc5171)). On the Catalyst 9300, Cisco notes that "Unidirectional links can cause a variety of problems, including spanning-tree topology loops", sets the message time to a default of 15 seconds in a range of 1 to 90, and leaves the protocol "Globally disabled" by default, on fibre and copper alike ([Cisco, Catalyst 9300 IOS XE 17.12, configuring UDLD, 28 July 2023](https://www.cisco.com/c/en/us/td/docs/switches/lan/catalyst9300/software/release/17-12/configuration_guide/lyr2/b_1712_lyr2_9300_cg/configuring_unidirectional_link_detection.html)).

**A guard that makes it worse.** Filtering BPDUs looks like a protection and is the opposite: Arista warns that "Enabling BPDU filtering on a port not connected to a host can result in loops" (Arista, as above).

**When the loop has already started.** Storm control does not prevent a loop; it limits what a flood can do. Cisco's Catalyst 9300 measures broadcast, multicast or unicast traffic as a percentage of the port's bandwidth, in packets per second or in bits per second, counting packets "within the 1-second time interval", blocking when the rising threshold is reached and, by default, filtering the excess without sending traps; "By default, storm control is disabled" ([Cisco, Catalyst 9300 IOS XE 17.12, configuring port-based traffic control, 28 July 2023](https://www.cisco.com/c/en/us/td/docs/switches/lan/catalyst9300/software/release/17-12/configuration_guide/sec/b_1712_sec_9300_cg/configuring_port_based_traffic_control.html)). Juniper's default goes the other way on switches running its Enhanced Layer 2 Software (ELS) configuration style: "Storm control is enabled by default on ELS platforms and disabled by default on non-ELS platforms. If storm control is enabled, the default level is 80 percent of the available bandwidth for ingress traffic" ([Juniper, understanding storm control](https://juniper.net/documentation/us/en/software/junos/security-services/topics/concept/rate-limiting-storm-control-understanding.html)). On a 10 Gb/s port, 80 percent still lets 8 Gb/s of flooded traffic through before anything is dropped, which saves the uplink but not a server's processor; the level is a choice, and Juniper's advice is to measure the normal broadcast share first and set it from that benchmark (Juniper, as above).

The guards side by side, each from its vendor's document:

| Risk | Cisco | Juniper | Arista | Default |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BPDU on an edge port | BPDU Guard (error-disables) | BPDU protection, `bpdu-block-on-edge` (disables) | BPDU guard (error-disables, manual re-enable) | Off: Juniper accepts BPDUs on all interfaces |
| Superior BPDU from the wrong place | Root Guard | Root protection | Root guard | Off |
| Blocked port stops hearing BPDUs | Loop Guard | Loop protection | Loop guard | Off: the port moves to forwarding |
| One-way fibre or optic | UDLD, 15 s messages | (not covered here) | (not covered here) | Off on Cisco |
| Flood already under way | Storm control, 1 s interval | Storm control, 80 % level | Storm control | Cisco off; Juniper ELS on at 80 % |

The FortiSwitch case, where spanning tree is on by default and these protections are not, is covered in [FortiSwitch VLANs, spanning tree, ports and quality of service](/learn/fortiswitch-vlans-stp-ports-and-qos).

### A hospital that went back to paper

The best-documented layer 2 collapse is the one its chief information officer chose to write about. John Halamka, then at CareGroup, recorded that "On November 13, 2002 at 1:45pm, Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center went from the hospital of 2002 to the hospital of 1972", that a researcher's file-sharing application exchanging "hundreds of gigabytes of data via multicast" saturated a switched core, and, among his ten lessons, "I did not know that a switched core was a point of failure" ([John Halamka, The CareGroup Network Outage, 4 March 2008](https://geekdoctor.blogspot.com/2008/03/caregroup-network-outage.html)). He also wrote that during the outage he "approved configuration changes that actually made the situation worse by causing spanning tree propagations, flooding the network with even more traffic", and that "we required the network to be functional to diagnose problems with the network" (Halamka, as above). The magazine account published three months after the event found "a spanning tree protocol loop", explained that "spanning tree will look only as far out as seven intersections", the diameter of seven bridges above, and reported that "Over four days, Halamka's network crashed repeatedly, forcing the hospital to revert to the paper patient-records system" ([Scott Berinato, CIO magazine, All Systems Down, 25 February 2003, republished by Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1346623/all-systems-down.html)). Halamka's lessons name the design the network lacked, "routed/redundant network cores, routed distribution layers and switched access layers isolated into vlans", and record that fixing "many basic structural problems in the network" took two days (Halamka, as above).

## Multi-chassis link aggregation: one partner, two chassis

A LAG whose members end on two switches removes the switch as a single point of failure, but only if the two switches look like one to the device at the other end. The far end runs ordinary LACP and checks that every member reports the same partner system ID, the rule explained in [link aggregation and LACP](/learn/link-aggregation-and-lacp); the pair has to forge that identity and keep it consistent. Every vendor's design has the same four parts:

1. **A link between the pair** that carries control traffic and, when needed, data: Cisco's peer link, Arista's peer link, Juniper's interchassis link (ICL), Extreme's inter-switch connection (ISC), FortiSwitch's ICL, and in Extreme's Fabric Engine a virtual inter-switch trunk (vIST) that serves Split MultiLink Trunking (SMLT).
2. **A second, independent heartbeat** so that a dead peer can be told from a dead peer link: Cisco's peer keepalive, Arista's heartbeat to the peer address, Juniper's backup liveness detection, Extreme's alternate health-check path.
3. **A forwarding rule on the link between the pair**, so that a frame from a dual-homed device is not sent back to it by the other switch. Cisco's design guide states the rule: "traffic coming from vPC member port, then crossing vPC peer-link is NOT allowed to egress any vPC member port", with "The only exception to this rule occurs when vPC member port goes down" ([Cisco, Design and Configuration Guide: Best Practices for Virtual Port Channels (vPC) on Cisco Nexus Series Switches, revised March 2021](https://www.cisco.com/c/dam/en/us/td/docs/switches/datacenter/sw/design/vpc_design/vpc_best_practices_design_guide.pdf)). Juniper uses "a block mask" to stop traffic "received on the ICL toward the multichassis aggregated Ethernet interface", cleared "if all of the local members of the MC-LAG link go down on the peer" ([Juniper, MC-LAG concepts](https://www.juniper.net/documentation/us/en/software/junos/mc-lag/topics/concept/mc-lag-feature-concepts.html)). Extreme's Fabric Engine writes it as "If a packet is received from a vIST trunk port, it is not forwarded to an active SMLT group in order to prevent network loops" ([Extreme Networks, Fabric Engine 9.1 User Guide, SMLT](https://documentation.extremenetworks.com/Fabric%20Engine%20v9.1%20User%20Guide/GUID-2CA1562F-51DC-45ED-9F3D-76BD2BD592AA.shtml)).
4. **A role**, primary and secondary, so that one of the two knows it must stand down when the pair cannot agree.

Only one of the control protocols is a published standard. The IETF's Inter-Chassis Communication Protocol, RFC 7275 of June 2014, written for provider edge routers (PEs), runs among "a set of two or more PEs, forming a Redundancy Group, for the purpose of synchronizing data among the systems", and is "built as a secondary protocol running within LDP", the Label Distribution Protocol, over its TCP session; its multi-chassis LACP application has the peers exchange their system configuration, aggregator and port state ([RFC 7275, Inter-Chassis Communication Protocol for Layer 2 Virtual Private Network (L2VPN) Provider Edge (PE) Redundancy, June 2014, Proposed Standard](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7275.txt)). The protocols inside Cisco's, Arista's, Extreme's and Fortinet's pairs are their own, and two switches from different vendors do not form a pair.

## When the link between the pair fails

The design is judged by what it does in four failures, and the vendors agree on three of them.

| What fails | What the pair sees | What the documents say happens |
|---|---|---|
| One member link to the far device | The local member is down | The pair lets the other switch forward to that device across the link between them: Cisco's exception to the vPC rule, Juniper's block mask cleared, Extreme's ISC filter changed |
| The other switch | Peer link and heartbeat both lost | The survivor takes over the shared identity |
| The peer link, with the heartbeat still answering | The peer is alive but cannot be synchronized | The secondary stands down |
| Both peer link and heartbeat, with both switches alive | Each believes the other is dead | Dual-active: both act alone, and the far device sees two partners |

**The peer link fails and the heartbeat still answers.** Cisco's guide is explicit: "When vPC peer-link fails down and vPC peer-keepalive link is still up, the vPC secondary peer device performs the following operations", namely it "Suspends its vPC member ports" and "Shuts down the SVI associated to the vPC VLAN", the switched virtual interface (Cisco, design guide, as above). H3C's DRNI does the same and names the condition: "A multi-active collision occurs if the IPL goes down while the keepalive link is up", and the secondary device "sets all network interfaces to DRNI MAD DOWN state", MAD standing for multi-active detection, except for the interfaces excluded from that action (H3C, as above). Arista leaves the action to configuration: a "dual primary detection delay with an optional action to errdisable all interfaces on secondary MLAG peer" (Arista, as above). Extreme's Switch Engine shows why the heartbeat must travel another way: "If the ISC link alone goes down when the remote peer is alive, both the MLAG peers forward the south-bound traffic, resulting in duplication of traffic. However, this does not result in traffic loops"; with an alternate health-check path, "When the MLAG switch misses 3 consecutive health check messages from the peer, it declares that the MLAG peer is not reachable on the ISC link", and "The MLAG switch having the lower IP address for the alternate path VLAN disables its ports" (Extreme, Switch Engine inter-switch communication, as above).

The cost of standing down falls on anything connected to one switch only. Cisco's guide warns that "access devices connected to operational secondary peer device become fully isolated in case of peer-link failure", and recommends that a single-attached device on a vPC VLAN "always connect it to vPC primary peer device" (Cisco, design guide, as above).

**Both links fail with both switches alive.** This is the layer 2 version of split brain, and the far device is the one that has to cope. Juniper describes the mechanism: "A split-brain occurs when ICCP adjacency is lost between the MC-LAG peers", "During a split-brain state, both active and standby peers change LACP system IDs", and "the customer edge (CE) device accepts the LACP system ID of the first link that comes up and brings down other links carrying different LACP system IDs" (Juniper, MC-LAG concepts, as above). LACP, the protocol in the middle, does the last line of defence: two partner IDs cannot share one bundle. Juniper's own mitigation is physical, an ICCP adjacency over an aggregated interface whose member links sit on several line cards, which it says "mitigates the possibility of a split-brain state" (Juniper, as above). Split brain in stateful clusters, where both halves can write state, is the subject of [part 7 of this series](/learn/stateful-clusters-active-standby-active-active-and-split-brain).

**Coming back is a failure mode too.** A switch that rejoins too early forwards before it has learnt addresses, multicast state or routes. Arista's reload delay keeps links disabled "after an MLAG peer reboots", and its non-MLAG version exists because "This interval allows non peer links to learn multicast and OSPF states before the ports start handling traffic" (Arista, as above). Extreme's switch "waits for 2 seconds before enabling the previously disabled ports", to "prevent frequent enabling and disabling of MLAG ports due to a faulty ISC link up event" (Extreme, as above). Cisco's technote shows the matching vPC timers in its sample output, "Delay-restore status : Timer is off.(timeout = 30s)" and "Auto-recovery status : Enabled (timeout = 240 seconds)" (Cisco, document 218333, as above).

**Configuration must match.** Cisco classifies mismatches between the two peers: a Type 1 inconsistency "Puts peer device or interface into a suspended state to prevent invalid packet forwarding behavior", while with a Type 2 the "Peer device or Interface still forward traffic"; for a global Type 1 inconsistency "all vPC member ports are set to down state", and with the graceful consistency check "only vPC member ports on secondary peer device are set to down state" (Cisco, design guide, as above). Juniper requires the same service identifier on both peers; without it, "multichassis link aggregation will not work" (Juniper, understanding MC-LAG, as above). FortiSwitch requires the same trunk name on both switches (Fortinet, as above).

**Spanning tree does not go away.** The pair still has to run it towards everything that is not dual-homed. In Arista's words, "The global STP configuration is derived from the primary peer device while the secondary device parameters are ignored", and spanning tree "views the MLAG Domain as a single switch and each MLAG as a single port" (Arista, as above). Without Cisco's peer-switch feature, a vPC "BPDU is processed only by primary peer device"; with it, "a vPC-attached access device will receive 2 BPDU: 1 per vPC peer device", and Bridge Assurance "is enabled automatically on vPC peer-link" (Cisco, design guide, as above). FortiSwitch warns against presenting the pair to the tree in two ways at once, as a dual-homed virtual switch on one side and as two ordinary bridges on the other (Fortinet, as above).

### Case: an MLAG pair that failed over the wrong way

GitHub published two post-mortems in December 2012 that show both kinds of layer 2 trouble in one network. In the first, on 30 November, a misconfiguration caused a bridge loop, the switches disabled the links as designed, and then a setting "intended to detect partial link failure between two links", unsupported between the access and aggregation models, disabled redundant links whenever one was shut; underneath it all, a bug left the aggregation switches "missing a number of MAC addresses from their tables", so they flooded traffic to every access switch ([GitHub, Network problems last Friday, 5 December 2012](https://github.blog/2012-12-05-network-problems-last-friday)). The team wrote that it "fixated for a very long time on the idea of a bridge loop and it blinded us to other possible causes" (GitHub, as above).

The second, on 22 December, came from the software update meant to fix the first. The aggregation switches ran "a feature called MLAG to appear as a single switch to the access switches". During a diagnostic step, the vendor terminated an agent on one switch; "the peer has a 5 second timeout period where it waits to hear from it again. If it does not hear from the peer, but still sees active links between them, it assumes that the other switch is still running but in an inconsistent state", and falls back to behaving as a standalone switch, whereas when links go down "they wait 2 seconds for the link to come back up" and then perform "a stateful takeover" that "does not trigger any layer two changes" ([GitHub, Downtime last Saturday, 26 December 2012](https://github.blog/2012-12-26-downtime-last-saturday)). The agent could not reset the links while it was writing its state, so the five seconds ran out with the links still up; aggregated links re-formed, spanning tree re-converged, and "all traffic between access switches" was blocked "for roughly a minute and a half" (GitHub, as above). That minute and a half was enough for file servers in different racks to exceed their heartbeat timeouts, try to fence each other and end with both nodes stopped in a number of pairs; restoring them "took over five hours" (GitHub, as above). Among the changes GitHub listed: placing the file servers' high-availability software in maintenance mode before any switching change, and a staging copy of the network to test upgrades on (GitHub, as above).

The timers in that report are worth reading against each other: a 5 second heartbeat timeout and a 2 second link wait are both reasonable alone, and the failure happened in the narrow case where the heartbeat stopped and the links did not.

## Stacks and virtual chassis: one control plane

Stacking takes the opposite approach to MLAG: instead of two switches that coordinate, it makes one switch out of several. Cisco's StackWise Virtual "allows two directly connected physical switches to operate as a single logical virtual switch", and "Since the control plane, management plane, and data plane are integrated, the system behaves as a single switch"; "The active switch is responsible for all the management, bridging and routing protocols, and software data path", and the pair runs stateful switchover between the active and the standby ([Cisco, Catalyst 9500 IOS XE 17.12, configuring Cisco StackWise Virtual, 28 July 2023](https://www.cisco.com/c/en/us/td/docs/switches/lan/catalyst9500/software/release/17-12/configuration_guide/ha/b_1712_ha_9500_cg/configuring_cisco_stackwise_virtual.html)). The StackWise Virtual link (SVL) "carries all the control and data traffic between the switching units", up to eight of them can be configured, and a separate dual-active detection (DAD) link, with up to four links for the fast hello method or enhanced Port Aggregation Protocol (ePAgP) messages through a neighbour, catches the split; the former active then "shuts down all of its non-SVL or non-DAD interfaces" and "waits in recovery mode until the SVLs recover" (Cisco, as above).

Juniper's Virtual Chassis goes further in size and states the split rule in full. Members take the primary Routing Engine role, the backup role or the linecard role ([Juniper, Virtual Chassis components](https://juniper.net/documentation/us/en/software/junos/virtual-chassis/topics/concept/virtual-chassis-components.html)); an EX4400 Virtual Chassis takes "Up to 10" members from Junos OS 21.1R1 (Juniper, as above). When it splits, one part stays active if it "contains both the stable primary and the stable backup", or "contains the stable primary and the configuration is greater than half the Virtual Chassis size", or "contains the stable backup and is at least half the Virtual Chassis size"; the other part's members "remain in a linecard role" ([Juniper, understanding split and merge in a Virtual Chassis](https://juniper.net/documentation/us/en/software/junos/virtual-chassis/topics/concept/virtual-chassis-split-merge.html)). Worked:

| Virtual Chassis | Split | Where the primary and backup were | Part that stays active |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 members | 5 and 5 | Primary in one half, backup in the other | The backup's half: 5 is "at least half", while the primary's 5 is not "greater than half" |
| 6 members | 2 and 4 | Primary in the 2, backup in the 4 | The 4: it holds the backup and is at least half |
| 6 members | 4 and 2 | Both in the 4 | The 4: it holds both |
| 3 members | 1 and 2 | Primary alone, backup in the 2 | The 2 |

Juniper's documentation adds the reason for the rule: two active halves "can cause disruptions in the network if the two resulting Virtual Chassis share common resources such as global IP addresses" (Juniper, as above). The rule is a majority rule in all but name, the same idea that [part 9 of this series](/learn/data-and-service-redundancy-replication-quorum-rpo-and-rto) treats as quorum.

The trade between the two families is about what they share. A stack or a virtual chassis has one configuration and one running control plane, so an operator manages one device; MLAG keeps two control planes, so the pair needs consistency checks and a split-brain policy, and an upgrade can take one switch at a time, which is how GitHub planned its December change (GitHub, as above). The [FortiSwitch topologies article](/learn/fortiswitch-fortilink-provisioning-and-topologies) puts the stacking trade in one phrase: saving ports at the cost of dependency.

## The standard's version: DRNI in IEEE 802.1AX

The IEEE has defined its own multi-chassis aggregation since 2014. The project that added it, 802.1AXbq, "Link Aggregation Amendment: Distributed Resilient Network Interconnect", had its project authorization request approved on 10 September 2011 and was superseded by the revision of the whole standard ([IEEE 802.1, 802.1AXbq](https://www.ieee802.org/1/pages/802.1axbq.html)), approved as 802.1AX-2014 on 10 December 2014 ([IEEE 802.1, 802.1AX-2014](https://www.ieee802.org/1/pages/802.1AX-2014.html)). The current edition, approved on 30 January 2020, describes the Distributed Resilient Network Interconnect (DRNI) as "A resilient interconnect using multiple full-duplex point-to-point links among one to three nodes in a network and one to three nodes in another, separately administered, network", and its project request gave the reason for the revision: "There is a need to correct and clarify Link Aggregation specifications and in particular the Distributed Resilient Network Interface specifications, in the light of implementation experience to ensure interoperability" ([IEEE 802.1, 802.1AX-2020 project page](https://1.ieee802.org/tsn/802-1ax-rev/)).

The vendor that uses the IEEE's name most visibly is explicit about what it implements. H3C's configuration guide describes DRNI as virtualizing "two physical devices into one system through multichassis link aggregation", lists as its standard "IEEE P802.1AX-REV™/D4.4c, Draft Standard for Local and Metropolitan Area Networks", and then states: "DRNI uses H3C proprietary Distributed Relay Control Protocol (DRCP) for multichassis link aggregation" and "DRNI is an H3C proprietary protocol. You cannot use DR interfaces for communicating with third-party devices" (H3C, as above). The standard exists; in the documents read for this article, the pairs that ship are still single-vendor.

## EVPN multihoming: the standard answer at scale

The IETF's answer moved the problem out of the pair and into the routing protocol. RFC 7432, "BGP MPLS-Based Ethernet VPN", a Proposed Standard of February 2015 (MPLS being Multiprotocol Label Switching), defines an Ethernet virtual private network whose provider edges (PEs) learn MAC addresses through BGP rather than by flooding, and it has since been updated by RFC 8584, RFC 9161, RFC 9572, RFC 9573 and RFC 9746 ([RFC Editor, RFC 7432 metadata](https://www.rfc-editor.org/info/rfc7432)). Its one verified erratum, number 5554 of 2018, is a reminder of how standards meet implementations: the RFC did not say where a 20-bit MPLS label sits in a 3-octet field, and the erratum notes that "This lead to ambiguity in different implementations" ([RFC Editor, errata for RFC 7432](https://www.rfc-editor.org/errata/rfc7432)). The [MPLS primer](/learn/mpls-primer) explains the labels.

### The Ethernet segment

The unit of redundancy is the link bundle, renamed: "When a customer site (device or network) is connected to one or more PEs via a set of Ethernet links, then that set of links is referred to as an 'Ethernet segment'", identified by a non-zero Ethernet Segment Identifier (ESI) that "is encoded as a 10-octet integer" ([RFC 7432, sections 3 and 5](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7432.txt)). One of the six ESI types is built from the attached device's own LACP identity, its "CE LACP System MAC address (6 octets)" and "CE LACP Port Key (2 octets)", so that "As far as the CE is concerned, it would treat the multiple PEs that it is connected to as the same switch" (RFC 7432, section 5). That is MLAG's trick, done by a standard.

Two modes decide how the PEs share the segment. In Single-Active mode "only a single PE, among all the PEs attached to an Ethernet segment, is allowed to forward traffic to/from that Ethernet segment for a given VLAN"; in All-Active mode "all PEs attached to an Ethernet segment are allowed to forward known unicast traffic" (RFC 7432, section 3). A bridged network attached without a LAG has to use single-active, because only one of its links may be active per VLAN (RFC 7432, section 8.5).

### Four mechanisms in place of a peer link

- **Fast convergence.** Without help, convergence would be "a function of the number of MAC/IP Advertisement routes that must be withdrawn by the PE encountering a failure"; instead, each PE advertises Ethernet auto-discovery (A-D) routes per Ethernet segment (ES), and "Upon a failure in connectivity to the attached segment, the PE withdraws the corresponding set of Ethernet A-D per ES routes", which makes every remote PE update the next hop for all the segment's MAC addresses at once (RFC 7432, section 8.2). One withdrawal replaces thousands.
- **Aliasing.** A device hashes its flows over its LAG, so one PE may learn all its MAC addresses; with an EVPN instance written EVI, aliasing is "the ability of a PE to signal that it has reachability to an EVPN instance on a given ES even when it has learned no MAC addresses from that EVI/ES", so remote PEs can still balance across all of them (RFC 7432, section 8.4).
- **Designated forwarder.** Only one PE per VLAN, the designated forwarder (DF), sends broadcast, unknown unicast and multicast (BUM) traffic to the segment (RFC 7432, section 8.5).
- **Split horizon.** A BUM frame that a non-DF PE received from the device and forwarded to the others must not come back: "the DF PE to which the CE is multihomed MUST drop the packet and not forward back to the CE" (RFC 7432, section 8.3). Over MPLS this uses an ESI label; over Virtual Extensible LAN (VXLAN) and Network Virtualization using Generic Routing Encapsulation (NVGRE), which have no room for a label stack, RFC 8365 of March 2018 recommends "Local Bias", in which a PE drops flooded frames from a PE it shares the segment with, using the source address of the tunnel ([RFC 8365, A Network Virtualization Overlay Solution Using Ethernet VPN (EVPN), section 8.3.1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8365.txt)). RFC 9746 of March 2025 updates both documents with extensions for split-horizon filtering ([RFC Editor, RFC 9746 metadata](https://www.rfc-editor.org/info/rfc9746)).

### The election, worked

The default election in RFC 7432 is "service carving". Each PE advertises a route for the segment and "starts a timer (default value = 3 seconds)"; when it expires, each PE sorts the originating IP addresses of all PEs on the segment "in increasing numeric value", numbers them from 0, and the PE with ordinal i is the DF for a given segment and VLAN V "when (V mod N) = i" (RFC 7432, section 8.5). RFC 8584 of April 2019 works an example and shows the weakness: three PEs on one segment carry VLANs with tags 999, 1000 and 1001 ([RFC 8584, Framework for Ethernet VPN Designated Forwarder Election Extensibility, section 1.3.1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8584.txt)).

| VLAN tag V | V mod 3, with PE1, PE2, PE3 up | DF | V mod 2, after PE3 fails | New DF | Moved although its DF was healthy? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 999 | 0 | PE1 | 1 | PE2 | Yes |
| 1000 | 1 | PE2 | 0 | PE1 | Yes |
| 1001 | 2 | PE3 | 1 | PE2 | No, PE3 failed |

That is the result RFC 8584 states: "when PE3 goes down, PE2 will become the DF for V1 and PE1 will become the DF for V2", a "needless churn" in which "all the objects (VLAN V) will be remapped except those for which V mod N and V mod (N-1) refer to the same PE" (RFC 8584, section 1.3.1). The same section names the other weakness: when the tags "are all even or all odd" and two PEs share the segment, "one of the PEs will be elected as the DF for all of the VLANs"; tags 100, 102, 104 and 106 all give 0 modulo 2, so the PE with the lowest address carries every VLAN's flooded traffic (RFC 8584, as above). RFC 8584 adds a Highest Random Weight algorithm, a form of consistent hashing, so that a failure moves only the VLANs of the failed PE (RFC 8584, section 3); RFC 9722 of June 2025 adds fast recovery after a failed link or node comes back, and RFC 9785 of the same month a preference-based election ([RFC Editor, RFC 9722 metadata](https://www.rfc-editor.org/info/rfc9722); [RFC Editor, RFC 9785 metadata](https://www.rfc-editor.org/info/rfc9785)).

### In the vendors' documents

Juniper calls the result an EVPN LAG or ESI LAG and describes what it removes: "An EVPN-VXLAN overlay offers link and node level redundancy and an active-active Layer 2 Ethernet extension between the leaf devices, without the need to implement a Spanning Tree Protocol (STP) to avoid loops" ([Juniper, introduction to EVPN LAG multihoming](https://www.juniper.net/documentation/us/en/software/nce/evpn-lag-multihoming-guide/topics/concept/evpn-lag-guide-introduction.html)). Nokia's SR OS "supports EVPN VXLAN multihoming as specified in RFC8365", names the same three components, "Designated Forwarder (DF) election", "split-horizon" and "aliasing", and applies local bias in VXLAN services ([Nokia, 7750 SR Layer 2 Services and EVPN Guide, release 22.5.R1, EVPN VXLAN multihoming](https://infocenter.nokia.com/public/7750SR225R1A/topic/com.nokia.L2_Services_and_EVPN_Guide/evpn_vxlan_mult-ai9enrmqd0.html)); its preference-based election, from the draft that became RFC 9785, advertises a 2-byte preference "32767 by default" and lets an operator force a new DF for maintenance by changing the value "without shutting down the ES" ([Nokia, 7750 SR Layer 2 Services and EVPN Guide, release 21.10.R1, preference-based and non-revertive DF election](https://infocenter.nokia.com/public/7750SR2110R1A/topic/com.nokia.L2_Services_and_EVPN_Guide/preference-base-ai9enrmqgi.html)). Because the signalling is BGP and the behaviour is in RFCs, two vendors' PEs can share a fabric: Juniper published in February 2025 a guide to running Arista EOS and Junos OS in one EVPN-VXLAN fabric, with an Arista pair and a Juniper pair each multihoming a host with ESI LAG, while noting that "Multi-vendor fabrics are less common but do happen" ([Juniper Networks, EVPN VXLAN Interoperability Between Arista EOS and Junos OS, 18 February 2025](https://juniper.net/documentation/us/en/software/nce/evpn-vxlan-interoperability-arista-eos-junos-os/evpn-vxlan-interoperability-arista-eos-junos-os.pdf)).

What EVPN multihoming gives up is simplicity. It needs a BGP control plane, an underlay and, over VXLAN, the local bias rules, and an operator debugging it reads route types where an MLAG operator reads a peer-link status. The gateway that sits on top of such a fabric, the distributed anycast gateway, is the subject of [part 5 of this series](/learn/default-gateway-redundancy-vrrpv3-glbp-and-anycast-gateways).

## How the failures are found, and what to test

Each design has a short list of things to look at, and every one of them is in the vendors' own commands and logs.

- **The far end's view.** LACP partner system ID and key on the dual-homed device: if a pair splits, the partner IDs differ and the device drops members (Juniper, MC-LAG concepts, as above). The [LACP member link and state reader](/tools/lacp-hash-and-state-reader) decodes the state flags a show command prints.
- **The pair's view.** Arista's `show mlag` prints three blocks, configuration, status (including peer-link and local-interface status) and a count of ports by state, from disabled to active-full (Arista, as above); Cisco's `show vpc consistency-parameters` gives "the consistency reason of down vpc" (Cisco, document 218333, as above).
- **A storm in progress.** Broadcast and multicast counters climbing on every port of a VLAN at once, processors at their limit, and storm-control drops; GitHub's case adds a subtler signature, unknown-unicast flooding because addresses were missing from the table (GitHub, 5 December 2012, as above).
- **Where to capture.** The [packet capture plan builder](/tools/packet-capture-plan-builder) helps decide where to put a capture point when the symptom is flooding or duplication.

The tests that matter are the four failures in the table above, run in a window with the far devices watched: pull one member, pull the peer link alone, cut the heartbeat alone, and reload one switch, timing each. GitHub's lesson applies before the first one: put anything that fails over on its own, such as storage or firewall clusters, in maintenance mode, or the test will exercise their redundancy too (GitHub, 26 December 2012, as above). How to plan such a window so that it does not become the incident is covered in [change windows that do not become incidents](/learn/change-windows-that-do-not-become-incidents). Testing a failover that has never been tried is the subject of [the last part of this series](/learn/testing-redundancy-failure-drills-chaos-and-the-failover-never-tried).

## What to remember

- A LAG to one switch protects the link, not the switch; MLAG, stacks, DRNI and EVPN multihoming each make two or more switches answer as one partner.
- Spanning tree's defaults assume a diameter of seven bridges; classic STP takes 30 to 50 seconds, RSTP reacts at once to a lost link and in 3 × 2 = 6 seconds to a silent one, and every topology change floods while tables are relearnt.
- The guards (BPDU guard, root guard, loop guard, UDLD, storm control) are mostly off by default, and BPDU filtering on a switch-facing port creates the loop it seems to prevent.
- Every MLAG has a link between the pair, an independent heartbeat, a rule that stops frames returning to a dual-homed device, and a secondary that stands down when the peer link fails; dual-active, with both links lost, leaves the far device to choose between two partners.
- Stacks and virtual chassis trade two control planes for one; Juniper's split rule keeps active the part with the backup and at least half the members, or the primary and more than half.
- DRNI is in IEEE 802.1AX since 2014, but the implementation documented here calls itself proprietary; EVPN multihoming (RFC 7432, RFC 8365) does the same job with BGP, any number of PEs and several vendors in one fabric, at the cost of a routing control plane.
- The default DF election, V mod N, moves VLANs whose DF never failed; RFC 8584's consistent hashing and RFC 9785's preferences exist for that reason.
