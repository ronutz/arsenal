High-frequency trading is the part of electronic markets that most people have heard of and few can define: firms trading their own money through computers that react in millionths of a second, praised for narrowing the gap between buying and selling prices and blamed for crashes, hidden costs and a race for speed that some economists call wasteful. This part of the series opened by [the map from the pit to the microsecond](/learn/electronic-trading-from-the-pit-to-the-microsecond) follows [the screens](/learn/the-screens-arrive-electronic-exchanges-from-nasdaq-to-globex) and [the rules that rewired markets](/learn/the-rules-that-rewired-markets-reg-ats-reg-nms-mifid-and-brazil), and comes before [the infrastructure of speed](/learn/colocation-microwaves-and-the-race-for-latency). After reading, you can say how the United States (US) and European regulators define the activity, describe its strategies, read the main studies on liquidity and costs within their limits, retell the Flash Crash of 6 May 2010 and the Knight Capital failure of 1 August 2012 from the official records, weigh the estimates of the arms race against the industry's replies, and place the Brazilian figures that B3 (Brasil, Bolsa, Balcão) publishes.

## What counts as high-frequency trading

### A working description, 2010

The US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) wrote in its concept release on equity market structure, dated 14 January 2010, that high-frequency trading (HFT) "is relatively new and is not yet clearly defined" and "typically is used to refer to professional traders acting in a proprietary capacity that engage in strategies that generate a large number of trades on a daily basis" ([SEC, Release 34-61358, 14 January 2010, Federal Register 21 January 2010](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-01-21/pdf/2010-1045.pdf)). It said such traders might be a proprietary trading firm, the proprietary desk of a broker-dealer or a hedge fund, and listed five characteristics often attributed to them: extraordinarily high-speed and sophisticated programs for generating, routing and executing orders; co-location services and individual data feeds to minimize latencies; very short time-frames for establishing and liquidating positions; numerous orders cancelled shortly after submission; and ending the day as close to flat as possible ([SEC, 2010](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-01-21/pdf/2010-1045.pdf)).

The same release recorded that estimates of HFT volume "vary widely, though they typically are 50% of total volume or higher", and chose not to define the term at all: "Rather than attempt any single, precise definition of HFT, this release will focus on particular strategies and tools" ([SEC, 2010](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-01-21/pdf/2010-1045.pdf)). Ten years later the SEC's staff report on algorithmic trading, required by section 502 of the Economic Growth, Regulatory Relief, and Consumer Protection Act of 2018 and dated 5 August 2020, still noted that there is "no statutory or regulatory definition of this term" ([SEC staff, 5 August 2020](https://www.sec.gov/files/algo_trading_report_2020.pdf)).

### The European legal definition

The European Union (EU) did write a definition into law. The revised Markets in Financial Instruments Directive (MiFID II), Directive 2014/65/EU of 15 May 2014, defines algorithmic trading as trading in which "a computer algorithm automatically determines individual parameters of orders" with "limited or no human intervention", excluding systems that only route orders or process them after execution ([Directive 2014/65/EU, article 4(1)(39)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32014L0065)). A high-frequency algorithmic trading technique is an algorithmic technique characterized by three things together ([article 4(1)(40)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32014L0065)):

1. infrastructure intended to minimize latencies, including at least one of co-location, proximity hosting or high-speed direct electronic access;
2. system determination of order initiation, generation, routing or execution without human intervention for individual trades or orders;
3. high message intraday rates which constitute orders, quotes or cancellations.

The Commission's Delegated Regulation (EU) 2017/565 turned the third test into numbers: on average at least 2 messages per second in any single instrument traded on a venue, or at least 4 messages per second across all instruments on a venue, counting messages in liquid instruments sent when dealing on own account ([Delegated Regulation (EU) 2017/565, article 19](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32017R0565)). Recital 61 of the directive adds the point that runs through the whole debate: high-frequency trading "rather than being a strategy in itself is usually the use of sophisticated technology to implement more traditional trading strategies such as market making or arbitrage" ([Directive 2014/65/EU, recital 61](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32014L0065)).

The definition has consequences. A firm dealing only on its own account normally falls outside MiFID II, but not if it applies a high-frequency technique ([article 2(1)(d)(iii)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32014L0065)); every algorithmic trader must have risk controls that "prevent the sending of erroneous orders", and a high-frequency firm must keep "accurate and time sequenced records of all its placed orders, including cancellations" ([article 17(1) and (2)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32014L0065)). Its business clocks may not diverge from Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) by more than 100 microseconds, with timestamps of 1 microsecond or finer, from 3 January 2018 ([Delegated Regulation (EU) 2017/574, annex, table 2](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32017R0574)); [the next part](/learn/colocation-microwaves-and-the-race-for-latency) explains how that is achieved.

|---|---|---|
| Status | A description, explicitly not a definition | A legal definition with consequences |
| Who | Professional traders acting in a proprietary capacity | Any firm applying the technique, including on own account |
| Speed | Extraordinarily high-speed programs; co-location and individual data feeds | Infrastructure to minimize latency: co-location, proximity hosting or high-speed direct electronic access |

### How much of the market it is, and why the answers differ

Because the definition is fuzzy, so is the measurement. The European Securities and Markets Authority (ESMA) studied 100 stocks from nine EU countries in May 2013 and gave a lower and an upper bound: identifying HFT firms by their primary business gave 24% of value traded, 30% of trades and 58% of orders, while flagging any participant whose orders lived for a very short time gave 43%, 49% and 76% ([ESMA, Economic Report No 1, 2014](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/library/2015/11/esma20141_-_hft_activity_in_eu_equity_markets.pdf)). ESMA explained that the difference is mainly the high-frequency activity of investment banks, which the second method captures and the first does not ([ESMA, 2014](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/library/2015/11/esma20141_-_hft_activity_in_eu_equity_markets.pdf)). Any single share figure for HFT therefore carries its method with it.

## The strategies

The SEC's 2010 release grouped the strategies of proprietary firms into four broad types and noted that "many of the trading strategies discussed below are not new. What is new is the technology" ([SEC, 2010](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-01-21/pdf/2010-1045.pdf)).

**Passive market making.** The firm rests bids and offers in the order book and earns the spread by buying at the bid and selling at the offer, plus any rebates venues pay to orders that supply liquidity; layering many orders at several prices can produce "high cancellation rates of 90% of more" and orders that live "a second or less" ([SEC, 2010](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-01-21/pdf/2010-1045.pdf)). The SEC observed that "Proprietary firms largely have replaced" exchange specialists on manual floors and over-the-counter market makers, but without the time and place privileges, and without the affirmative and negative obligations, that came with those roles ([SEC, 2010](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-01-21/pdf/2010-1045.pdf)). Albert Menkveld's study of one large high-frequency trader active on an incumbent exchange and a new entrant venue found that it lost money on its inventory, earned it on the bid-ask spread, and was passive in four out of five of its trades ([Menkveld, Journal of Financial Markets, 2013](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:finmar:v:16:y:2013:i:4:p:712-740)).

**Arbitrage.** The firm captures price differences between related instruments or markets, for example between an exchange-traded fund (ETF) and the basket of stocks inside it, usually by taking liquidity and holding hedged positions ([SEC, 2010](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-01-21/pdf/2010-1045.pdf)).

**Structural strategies, including latency arbitrage.** The SEC described firms that, by obtaining market data fastest through co-location and direct feeds, "theoretically could profit by identifying market participants who are offering executions at stale prices" ([SEC, 2010](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-01-21/pdf/2010-1045.pdf)). Economists later named the practice "sniping" stale quotes, or latency arbitrage, and called it the harmful side of speed, as against useful liquidity provision ([Aquilina, Budish and O'Neill, FCA Occasional Paper 50, January 2020](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/occasional-papers/occasional-paper-50.pdf)).

**Directional strategies.** The SEC singled out two. In order anticipation, a firm detects a large buyer or seller from public information or by "pinging" venues and trades ahead of it; the release quoted a 2003 market-structure treatise: "Order anticipators are parasitic traders. They profit only when they can prey on other traders" ([SEC, 2010](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-01-21/pdf/2010-1045.pdf)). In momentum ignition, a firm submits and cancels many orders to "spoof" other algorithms into a price move it has positioned for; the SEC noted that manipulation is already prohibited and asked whether more tools were needed ([SEC, 2010](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-01-21/pdf/2010-1045.pdf)).

### A latency arbitrage race, step by step

The study by Matteo Aquilina, Eric Budish and Peter O'Neill for the United Kingdom's (UK) Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) used London Stock Exchange (LSE) message data, which, unlike order book data, records the attempts to trade or cancel that fail, so both winners and losers of a race are visible ([FCA Occasional Paper 50, 2020](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/occasional-papers/occasional-paper-50.pdf)). The sequence below follows the paper's definitions; the timings are its reported values.

| Step | What happens | What the FCA paper measured |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | New information arrives: a related price moves elsewhere | Races occur about once per minute per FTSE 100 symbol |
| 2 | Several fast firms send orders to take the now-stale quote; its owner sends a cancellation | The participants are concentrated: the top 3 firms win about 55% of races and lose about 66% |
| 3 | The exchange processes messages one at a time; the first take succeeds, the cancellation and the slower takes fail | In the modal race the winner beats the first loser by 5 to 10 microseconds; about 4% of the time the winner's message arrives slightly later but is processed first |
| 4 | The winner gains a little; the quote's owner loses the same | The average race is worth a bit more than half a tick, about 2 pounds |
| 5 | Added up over the day | About 22% of FTSE 100 trading volume is in races |

Source for every row: [FCA Occasional Paper 50, January 2020](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/occasional-papers/occasional-paper-50.pdf).

## What the evidence says about liquidity and costs

The studies most often cited in defence of fast trading are causal or near-causal ones. Terrence Hendershott, Charles Jones and Albert Menkveld used the New York Stock Exchange's (NYSE) automation of quote dissemination in 2003 as a natural experiment and found that algorithmic trading "narrows spreads, reduces adverse selection" for large stocks and "improves liquidity and enhances the informativeness of quotes" ([Hendershott, Jones and Menkveld, Journal of Finance, February 2011](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/hender/Algo.pdf)). Jonathan Brogaard, Terrence Hendershott and Ryan Riordan, with a Nasdaq dataset "that identifies a subset of HFTs", found that they "facilitate price efficiency by trading in the direction of permanent price changes", through their liquidity-demanding orders, while their liquidity-supplying orders "are adversely selected" ([Brogaard, Hendershott and Riordan, European Central Bank Working Paper 1602, November 2013](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/pub/pdf/scpwps/ecbwp1602.pdf)).

The broadest official review, the UK Government Office for Science's Foresight project of 2012, concluded that computer-based trading had several beneficial effects: liquidity measured by spreads "has improved", transaction costs "have fallen for both retail and institutional traders", and prices "have become more efficient" ([Foresight, The Future of Computer Trading in Financial Markets, final report, 2012](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/289431/12-1086-future-of-computer-trading-in-financial-markets-report.pdf)). It added a "particular concern": high-frequency traders now provided most market making but, "unlike designated specialists", typically "operate with little capital, hold small inventory positions and have no obligations to provide liquidity during periods of market stress", which creates "the potential for periodic illiquidity" ([Foresight, 2012](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/289431/12-1086-future-of-computer-trading-in-financial-markets-report.pdf)). It found "no direct evidence that computer-based HFT has increased volatility", and no direct evidence that it had increased market abuse, while reporting that qualitative evidence from investors "consistently indicates high levels of concern" about manipulation ([Foresight, 2012](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/289431/12-1086-future-of-computer-trading-in-financial-markets-report.pdf)).

The SEC staff's 2020 summary reads the same way: algorithmic trading "has improved many measures of market quality and liquidity provision during normal market conditions", though "some types of algorithmic trading may exacerbate periods of unusual market stress or volatility" ([SEC staff, 2020](https://www.sec.gov/files/algo_trading_report_2020.pdf)). In June 2014, after Michael Lewis's Flash Boys had made the subject a number one best seller ([FCA Occasional Paper 50, 2020](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/occasional-papers/occasional-paper-50.pdf)), the SEC's chair, Mary Jo White, cited execution costs for large institutional orders "more than 10 percent lower in 2013 than in 2006" and spreads "as narrow as they have ever been", and concluded that the market structure "is not fundamentally broken, let alone rigged"; in the same speech she asked "whether the increasingly expensive search for speed has passed the point of diminishing returns" ([Mary Jo White, SEC, 5 June 2014](https://www.sec.gov/news/speech/2014-spch060514mjw)).

The limits matter. ESMA's correlations between HFT activity and market features are, in its own words, "mostly based on correlations" ([ESMA, Report on Trends, Risks and Vulnerabilities No. 1, 2014](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/library/2015/11/2014-188_trv_no.1_2014.pdf)); the Foresight review warned that its improvements "should not be overstated" ([Foresight, 2012](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/289431/12-1086-future-of-computer-trading-in-financial-markets-report.pdf)); and the normal-conditions findings say little about the rare minutes in which liquidity disappears, which is where the next case begins.

## 6 May 2010: the Flash Crash

### What the regulators found

The staffs of the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) and the SEC found that on the afternoon of 6 May 2010 major US equity indices in futures and securities markets, already down more than 4%, "suddenly plummeted a further 5-6% in a matter of minutes before rebounding almost as quickly", and more than 20,000 trades in over 300 securities were executed at prices more than 60% away from their values moments before, some at a penny or less and some as high as $100,000 ([CFTC and SEC staff, Findings Regarding the Market Events of May 6, 2010, 30 September 2010](https://www.cftc.gov/sites/default/files/idc/groups/public/@otherif/documents/ifdocs/staff-findings050610.pdf)). Their joint report reconstructs the minutes as follows (Central Time, as the report's figures note).

| Time | Event in the joint report |
|---|---|
| About 1:00 p.m. | Negative sentiment over the European debt crisis; volatility pauses on the NYSE rise above average |
| 2:30 p.m. | Buy-side depth in the E-Mini S&P 500 futures contract (E-Mini) has fallen from nearly $6 billion in the morning to $2.65 billion |
| 2:32 p.m. | A large fundamental trader, a mutual fund complex, starts selling 75,000 E-Mini contracts, about $4.1 billion, through an algorithm targeting 9% of the previous minute's volume "without regard to price or time" |
| 2:41 to 2:44 p.m. | High-frequency traders, net long about 3,300 contracts, sell about 2,000 to cut their positions; the E-Mini falls about 3% in four minutes |
| 2:45:13 to 2:45:27 | High-frequency traders trade over 27,000 contracts, about 49% of volume, while buying only about 200 net: the "hot-potato" effect; buy-side depth falls to about $58 million |
| 2:45:28 | The Chicago Mercantile Exchange's (CME) Stop Logic Functionality pauses E-Mini trading for five seconds |
| 2:45:33 | Trading resumes; prices stabilize and begin to recover |
| 2:40 to 3:00 p.m. | Liquidity providers pause or withdraw; over 20,000 trades in more than 300 securities execute 60% or more away from their 2:40 p.m. prices, many against "stub quotes" |
| After the close | Exchanges and the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA) break all such trades as clearly erroneous |

Source for every row: [CFTC and SEC staff, 30 September 2010](https://www.cftc.gov/sites/default/files/idc/groups/public/@otherif/documents/ifdocs/staff-findings050610.pdf).

The report's first lesson was that "under stressed market conditions, the automated execution of a large sell order can trigger extreme price movements, especially if the automated execution algorithm does not take prices into account"; the second, that the interaction between execution programs and algorithmic strategies "can quickly erode liquidity", and that "high trading volume is not necessarily a reliable indicator of market liquidity" ([CFTC and SEC staff, 2010](https://www.cftc.gov/sites/default/files/idc/groups/public/@otherif/documents/ifdocs/staff-findings050610.pdf)). It noted that on the previous occasion the same trader took more than 5 hours to sell its first 75,000 contracts, and on 6 May about 20 minutes ([CFTC and SEC staff, 2010](https://www.cftc.gov/sites/default/files/idc/groups/public/@otherif/documents/ifdocs/staff-findings050610.pdf)). The responses it recorded were single-stock circuit breakers, a five-minute pause after a 10% move in five minutes, approved for S&P 500 stocks on 10 June 2010 and extended on 10 September; clearer rules for breaking erroneous trades; and study of a "limit up/limit down" mechanism ([CFTC and SEC staff, 2010](https://www.cftc.gov/sites/default/files/idc/groups/public/@otherif/documents/ifdocs/staff-findings050610.pdf)). Stub quotes, the report explained, were quotes far from the market that market makers posted to meet continuous two-sided quoting obligations "even when a market maker has withdrawn from active trading" ([CFTC and SEC staff, 2010](https://www.cftc.gov/sites/default/files/idc/groups/public/@otherif/documents/ifdocs/staff-findings050610.pdf)).

### What the academic reconstruction added

Andrei Kirilenko, Albert Kyle, Mehrdad Samadi and Tugkan Tuzun, using the CFTC's account-level audit trail for the E-Mini, found that the trading pattern of the most active intermediaries, which they classified as high-frequency traders, "did not change when prices fell during the Flash Crash" ([Kirilenko, Kyle, Samadi and Tuzun, The Flash Crash: High-Frequency Trading in an Electronic Market, Journal of Finance, 2017, Cambridge repository copy](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/bitstream/1810/304244/1/KirilenkoFlashCrash.pdf); [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:72:y:2017:i:3:p:967-998)). The combined net inventories of all intermediaries never exceeded 6,000 contracts, "an order of magnitude smaller than the large sell program of 75,000 contracts"; and on ordinary days, when high-frequency traders were net buyers in a given second, prices rose in the following second, consistent with "stale quote sniping" ([Kirilenko et al., 2017](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/bitstream/1810/304244/1/KirilenkoFlashCrash.pdf)). The authors note that the joint report's narrative of the E-Mini was based in part on an earlier version of their paper ([Kirilenko et al., 2017](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/bitstream/1810/304244/1/KirilenkoFlashCrash.pdf)).

### The spoofer in London

Five years later a different cause entered the record. On 21 April 2015 the CFTC announced a civil complaint alleging that a UK resident, Navinder Singh Sarao, and his company had manipulated the E-Mini with a modified off-the-shelf platform that layered four to six exceptionally large sell orders above the best offer and cancelled most of them, and that on 6 May 2010 <Claim status="alleged" by="by the CFTC in 2015">his "manipulative activities contributed to an extreme E-mini S&P order book imbalance that contributed to market conditions that led to the Flash Crash"</Claim> ([CFTC, release 7156-15, 21 April 2015](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/7156-15)). In a consent order announced on 17 November 2016 Sarao admitted the complaint's allegations, with a headline in which he <Claim status="established" by="in a federal court consent order">admits to contributing to extreme order book imbalance</Claim>, and was ordered to pay more than $38 million in penalty and disgorgement ([CFTC, release 7486-16, 17 November 2016](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/7486-16)). His lawyers reported that on 28 January 2020 a federal judge in Chicago sentenced him to time served with one year of home confinement, after his guilty plea to wire fraud and spoofing ([Dechert, January 2020](https://www.dechert.com/knowledge/news/2020/1/dechert-represents-london-based-trader-navinder-singh-sarao.html)). The 2010 joint report, written before any of this, names neither him nor spoofing among the causes it analysed ([CFTC and SEC staff, 2010](https://www.cftc.gov/sites/default/files/idc/groups/public/@otherif/documents/ifdocs/staff-findings050610.pdf)); the two accounts address different questions, what drained liquidity and who added pressure to the order book, and neither record says that high-frequency traders started the fall.

## Knight Capital, 1 August 2012

Two years after the Flash Crash a single firm's failure showed what an unchecked algorithm can do in minutes. The SEC's order of 16 October 2013 found that on 1 August 2012 Knight Capital Americas' automated router "routed millions of orders into the market over a 45-minute period" and "obtained over 4 million executions in 154 stocks for more than 397 million shares", leaving a net long position of about $3.5 billion in 80 stocks and a net short position of about $3.15 billion in 74; "Ultimately, Knight lost over $460 million from these unwanted positions" ([SEC, Release 34-70694, 16 October 2013](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)).

The order traces the cause to deployment. Code for the NYSE's Retail Liquidity Program, "scheduled to commence on August 1, 2012", reused a flag that once activated an old function called Power Peg, "which Knight had discontinued using many years earlier"; from 27 July Knight deployed the new code, but "one of Knight's technicians did not copy the new code to one of the eight SMARS computer servers" ([SEC, 2013](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)). Because a 2005 change had moved the function that counted filled shares, the old code on that server, for 212 incoming parent orders, "continuously sent child orders, in rapid sequence" ([SEC, 2013](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)). Before the 9:30 a.m. open Knight's system had sent 97 e-mails naming an error described as "Power Peg disabled" ([SEC, 2013](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)). Knight "did not have written code development and deployment procedures" for the router and "did not require a second technician to review code deployment" ([SEC, 2013](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)).

The SEC found that Knight "willfully violated" Rule 15c3-5 under the Securities Exchange Act, the market access rule, and imposed a civil penalty of $12 million ([SEC, 2013](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)). The system at fault was "an automated, high speed, algorithmic router that sends orders into the market for execution", not a trading strategy ([SEC, 2013](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)); the case belongs here because it shows the speed of automated failure, and the engineering story is told in [Knight Capital: forty-five minutes, and a rollback that spread the fault](/learn/knight-capital-and-the-rollback-that-made-it-worse). A later part of this series, on pre-trade risk and market access, returns to Rule 15c3-5 and kill switches.

## The arms race

### Budish, Cramton and Shim

In 2010 Spread Networks finished a fibre cable dug in a nearly straight line between Chicago and New York, at an estimated cost of $300 million, cutting round-trip time from 16 to 13 milliseconds; microwave links soon took it to 10, 9 and then 8.5 milliseconds. Eric Budish, Peter Cramton and John Shim opened their paper with that story ([Budish, Cramton and Shim, National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) conference draft, 17 September 2013](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f69665.pdf)); [the next part](/learn/colocation-microwaves-and-the-race-for-latency) follows those links in detail. Their published argument, in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2015, is that "The high-frequency trading arms race is a symptom of flawed market design" ([Budish, Cramton and Shim, 2015, EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:130:y:2015:i:4:p:1547-1621)).

The reasoning has three steps. In a continuous limit order book, correlations between closely related instruments "completely break down" at millisecond horizons, which creates "obvious mechanical arbitrage opportunities", and competition "has not affected the size or frequency of the arbitrage opportunities, it has only raised the bar for how fast one has to be to capture them" ([Budish, Cramton and Shim, 2015](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:130:y:2015:i:4:p:1547-1621)). In the working version, the median duration of arbitrage opportunities between the E-Mini in Chicago and the S&P 500 ETF in New York fell from 97 milliseconds in 2005 to 7 milliseconds in 2011, while there were about a thousand such opportunities a day worth "on the order of $75 million per year" ([Budish, Cramton and Shim, 2013 draft](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f69665.pdf)). Because "even symmetrically observed public information creates arbitrage rents", the rents harm liquidity provision and "induce a never-ending socially wasteful arms race for speed"; their remedy is frequent batch auctions, "uniform price double auctions conducted, for example, every tenth of a second" ([Budish, Cramton and Shim, 2015](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:130:y:2015:i:4:p:1547-1621)). A later part of this series, on fairness by design, covers batch auctions and speed bumps.

### The FCA's estimate, and the industry's objections

The FCA paper of January 2020 put numbers on the harm from real races. On 43 trading days from August to October 2015 for the FTSE 350, about 2.2 billion messages recorded by an optical tap outside the exchange's firewall, it found that eliminating latency arbitrage would reduce investors' cost of liquidity by 17%, that the "latency-arbitrage tax" was 0.42 basis points of all volume, that the annual sums at stake were about 60 million pounds in the UK and, extrapolated, "about $5 billion per year" across global equity markets ([FCA Occasional Paper 50, 2020](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/occasional-papers/occasional-paper-50.pdf)). The authors framed their result between two quotations, Michael Lewis's <Claim status="attributed" by="by Michael Lewis in Flash Boys (2014)">"The market is rigged."</Claim> and an industry lobbyist's <Claim status="attributed" by="by Bill Harts of the Modern Markets Initiative (2016)">"Widespread latency arbitrage is a myth."</Claim>, and concluded that their numbers "are consistent with aspects of both" ([FCA Occasional Paper 50, 2020](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/occasional-papers/occasional-paper-50.pdf)). The FCA's disclaimer says occasional papers "may not necessarily represent the position of the FCA" ([FCA, 2020](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/occasional-papers/occasional-paper-50.pdf)).

The Futures Industry Association's (FIA) European Principal Traders Association (EPTA), which represents firms trading their own capital, answered in April 2020 with seven objections ([FIA EPTA, comments on FCA Occasional Paper 50, 30 April 2020](https://www.fia.org/sites/default/files/2020-05/20200430_FIA%20EPTA%20Review_FCA%20Occasional%20Paper_0.pdf)):

| FCA paper | FIA EPTA's objection |
|---|---|
| A race is a take of a stale quote that its owner tries to cancel | In most measured races the owner never tried to cancel; counting only those cuts the races by about 70% |
| 2015 LSE data | "Stale" data: the LSE later changed its systems so public execution data arrives at least as soon as private data |
| Extrapolation from the LSE to the UK and the world | The LSE is about 2.6% of global electronic order book trading; races cannot occur in auctions, and closing auctions are about 25% of daily volume; corrected, the UK figure falls to about 13.5 million pounds |
| Profits measured against the mid-price | The mid-price is not tradable and trading costs are ignored |
| An information horizon averaging 202 microseconds | Too long; at 50 microseconds the UK figure falls to about 3.1 million pounds |
| Investors and trading firms as two stylized types | Execution algorithms also act passively and trading firms also use them; the types cannot be separated |
| A "tax" on investors | Not zero-sum; the same few firms win and lose; electronic liquidity provision cut average costs by more than 50% in ten years |

Source for every row: [FIA EPTA, 30 April 2020](https://www.fia.org/sites/default/files/2020-05/20200430_FIA%20EPTA%20Review_FCA%20Occasional%20Paper_0.pdf); the FCA paper's own figures are in [FCA Occasional Paper 50](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/occasional-papers/occasional-paper-50.pdf). The FIA's 50% figure cites a TABB Group chart, not its own data ([FIA EPTA, 2020](https://www.fia.org/sites/default/files/2020-05/20200430_FIA%20EPTA%20Review_FCA%20Occasional%20Paper_0.pdf)).

A third kind of evidence comes from the weather. Andriy Shkilko and Konstantin Sokolov studied episodes in which rain and storms disrupted the microwave networks of the fastest traders and found that <Claim status="reported" by="by Shkilko and Sokolov in the Journal of Finance (2020)">the disruptions are associated with lower adverse selection and lower trading costs</Claim> ([Shkilko and Sokolov, Every Cloud Has a Silver Lining, Journal of Finance, 2020, EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:75:y:2020:i:6:p:2899-2927)).

## High-frequency trading on B3

Brazil's exchange has courted high-frequency traders for longer than many observers realize. The BM&FBovespa's Programa de Investidores de Alta Frequência, the high-frequency investor programme, was set up by Ofício Circular 028/2010-DP of 2 August 2010 to give fee incentives to investors that continuously meet its requirements; Ofício Circular 071/2017-DP of 8 November 2017 updated it, aiming to encourage day trades "em grandes volumes e, consequentemente, aumentar a liquidez", in large volumes and so increase liquidity, and was itself revoked by Ofício Circular 044/2022-PRE of 5 April 2022 ([B3, Ofício Circular 071/2017-DP, 8 November 2017](https://www.b3.com.br/data/files/A4/64/89/8B/6EC9F510BD3EA5F5790D8AA8/OC%20071-2017-DP%20-%20Nova%20Pol%C3%ADtica%20de%20Tarifa%C3%A7%C3%A3o%20de%20Opera%C3%A7%C3%B5es%20para%20Investidores%20de%20Alta%20Frequ%C3%AAncia%20-%20HFT.pdf)). In 2017 a broker had to request an investor's enrolment, the discount applied only to day trades in enrolled accounts, and B3 could reject or remove investors who did not meet its parameters ([B3, 2017](https://www.b3.com.br/data/files/A4/64/89/8B/6EC9F510BD3EA5F5790D8AA8/OC%20071-2017-DP%20-%20Nova%20Pol%C3%ADtica%20de%20Tarifa%C3%A7%C3%A3o%20de%20Opera%C3%A7%C3%B5es%20para%20Investidores%20de%20Alta%20Frequ%C3%AAncia%20-%20HFT.pdf)).

The supervisory side followed. In May 2012 the magazine Capital Aberto reported that high-frequency trading had gained ground at the BM&FBovespa about three years earlier, that it was limited, "em torno de 10% dos negócios", around 10% of trades, and that the exchange's self-regulator, BM&FBovespa Supervisão de Mercados (BSM), had begun assessing it at the end of 2011 with a surveillance system supplied by Nasdaq OMX and had seen more "casos atípicos", atypical cases, while discarding every suspicion analysed so far ([Capital Aberto, 1 May 2012](https://capitalaberto.com.br/empresas/2012/05/bsm-ve-indicios-de-infracoes-com-operacoes-de-alta-frequencia)). The exchange gave its own figure about two months later, on another basis. At a debate held on 29 June 2012 by the Comissão de Valores Mobiliários (CVM), Brazil's securities regulator, on a consultancy report about competition between exchanges, BM&FBovespa said that "entre 8 e 10% do volume negociado na BVMF corresponde a operações de alta frequência", between 8 and 10% of the volume traded on the exchange came from high-frequency trading. It said it recognized the contribution of high-frequency traders but worried about "dominância de HFTs", their dominance, in fragmented markets, where their strategies were designed to profit from imperfections in price formation "que se impõem como um pedágio aos demais investidores", which act as a toll on other investors ([BM&FBovespa, debate promoted by the CVM on the Oxera report, 29 June 2012, slides 23 and 24](https://www.gov.br/cvm/pt-br/centrais-de-conteudo/publicacoes/apresentacoes/apresentacoes-realizadas-pelo-mercado/2012/anexos-coloquio-29-6.2012/bmf-bovespa)). The argument came from the incumbent exchange in a debate about letting rivals in, which is part of reading it.

The figures since then come from B3 itself. Reuters reported in April 2021, quoting B3, that orders originated from B3's colocation facility were 39% of average daily volume in the cash equity market in the first quarter of 2021, against 35% a year earlier, and 59% in derivatives, against 53% ([Forbes Brasil with Reuters, 19 April 2021](https://forbes.com.br/forbes-money/2021/04/b3-oferta-dispositivos-mais-potentes-em-meio-a-alta-das-operacoes-de-alta-frequencia/)). On 4 April 2025 B3 said that high-frequency traders "respondem atualmente por cerca de 40% dos negócios realizados no mercado de ações brasileiro", account for about 40% of trades in the Brazilian equity market, and that the latency of its trading system had fallen from 1.2 milliseconds in 2023 to about 350 microseconds, with a target below 300 microseconds by 2026 ([B3, Avanço em tecnologia, 4 April 2025](https://www.b3.com.br/pt_br/noticias/avanco-em-tecnologia.htm)). Colocation share and share of trades are different measures and should not be compared as if they were the same.

B3's current HFT programme for the cash equity market, which its page says was due to start on 1 September (the page's transition clause runs to September 2026), requires an average daily traded volume of at least R$150 million with at least 85% day trading, plus at least 10 instruments with R$10 million each, and charges enrolled investors who meet them 0.009% of volume on passive (maker) orders and 0.013% on taker and other orders ([B3, Programa HFT de Mercado a Vista de Renda Variável, read 15 November 2026](https://b3.com.br/pt_br/produtos-e-servicos/tarifas/listados-a-vista-e-derivativos/programas-de-incentivo/programa-hft-de-mercado-a-vista-de-renda-variavel/)). Volumes from market maker programmes do not count towards the requirements ([B3, 2026](https://b3.com.br/pt_br/produtos-e-servicos/tarifas/listados-a-vista-e-derivativos/programas-de-incentivo/programa-hft-de-mercado-a-vista-de-renda-variavel/)). No study by the CVM measuring the effects of high-frequency trading on Brazilian market quality was found for this article. [Brazil: BM&F, Bovespa, B3 and electronic access](/learn/brazil-bmf-bovespa-b3-and-electronic-access) covers B3's access models, and [the next part](/learn/colocation-microwaves-and-the-race-for-latency) its colocation service.

## What to remember

- The SEC described high-frequency trading in 2010 by five traits and refused to define it; MiFID II defines a high-frequency technique by low-latency infrastructure, automation and at least 2 messages per second in one instrument or 4 across a venue.
- High-frequency trading is technology applied to old strategies: market making, arbitrage, the sniping of stale quotes and, at the abusive end, order anticipation and spoofing.
- Causal studies and official reviews find narrower spreads, lower costs and more efficient prices in normal conditions, and a risk of liquidity vanishing under stress.
- On 6 May 2010 a 75,000-contract sell algorithm triggered the fall; high-frequency traders passed contracts back and forth without absorbing them; a spoofer later admitted contributing to the order book imbalance.
- Knight Capital lost over $460 million in 45 minutes because new code reached seven of eight servers; the SEC fined it $12 million under the market access rule.
- The FCA paper estimated about $5 billion a year in latency arbitrage worldwide; the principal traders' association called the method flawed and recalculated the UK figure at 13.5 or 3.1 million pounds.
- B3 has offered fee incentives to high-frequency traders since 2010 and says they account for about 40% of trades in Brazilian equities.

<ReaderQuestions>

- If high-frequency firms supply most of the liquidity on an ordinary day but have no obligation to stay on a bad one, should the right to fast access carry a duty to quote, as the floor specialists' privileges once did?
- Which figure better describes the cost of the race for speed: the 17% reduction in liquidity costs that the FCA paper estimates, or the much smaller UK totals that its critics recalculate, and what data would settle the question?
- When an exchange designs fee programmes that reward volume and day trading, as B3 has since 2010, is it buying liquidity for every investor or selling an advantage to a few?
- The 2010 joint report and the later spoofing case describe the same afternoon differently: what does it mean for a cause to "contribute" to a crash that a sell algorithm triggered and a liquidity withdrawal deepened?
- If a few firms both win and lose most latency races, as the FCA paper found, who ultimately bears the cost of the race?

</ReaderQuestions>
