Every episode in this series ended. Witch trials stopped, schoolgirls recovered, rumours faded, runs on banks were halted, hoaxes were exposed. The records say how each ended, though rarely why, and the people who did the ending are usually the ones who wrote the record. Four things appear again and again: separating the people affected, reassuring them, investigating, and letting time pass. Around them has grown a body of advice: health authorities' guidance on mass psychogenic illness (MPI), the risk communication manuals of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) of the United States (US) and of the World Health Organization (WHO), the Debunking Handbook of 2020, journalism codes and court rulings, and laws against false alarms. This part sets the endings side by side, reads that advice, and says what kind of evidence stands behind each piece. After reading, you can say what each remedy is meant to do, how strong its support is, where two pieces of advice pull in opposite directions, and which questions the evidence leaves open.

Three patterns stand out. Some episodes ended when an authority changed the rules that fed them: a court closed, a guarantee replaced a queue. Some ended when a trusted voice spoke, and in Singapore the first voice to speak made things worse. And some did not end when officials spoke at all: in Kashmir clarifications were issued at once and the queues stayed. The sections below take the remedies one at a time.

## Separation

### What the guidance says

Separating the people affected is the oldest and most consistent advice. Simon Wessely's 1987 review, whose two syndromes are set out in [the part on mass psychogenic illness](/learn/mass-psychogenic-illness-when-symptoms-spread), tied separation to the fast, anxiety-driven form; for the slower form, in which abnormal movements spread after a period of tension, his advice was different: "Treatment should be directed towards the underlying stressors" ([Wessely, Psychological Medicine 17(1), February 1987, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=3575566)). Timothy Jones, a medical epidemiologist with the Tennessee Department of Health and first author of the study of the 1998 school outbreak, wrote that the symptoms "frequently resolve after patients are separated from each other and removed from the environment in which the outbreak began", and his recommended approach for physicians begins "Attempt to separate persons with illness associated with the outbreak" and continues "Minimize unnecessary exposure to medical procedures, emergency personnel, media or other potential anxiety-stimulating situations" ([Jones, American Family Physician 62(12), 15 December 2000](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2000/1215/p2649.html)).

The WHO's 2019 manual on immunization stress-related responses (ISRR), the term it uses for fainting, hyperventilation and similar reactions around vaccination, gives the same instruction for vaccination campaigns: "Once a cluster of cases is identified, the affected individuals should be separated from each other and from healthy vaccine recipients" ([WHO, Immunization stress-related responses: a manual for program managers and health professionals, 2019](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/8c4a911c-e176-431b-8144-da5cddec4757/content)). It asks staff to isolate the first person affected tactfully, "to prevent transmission of fear and anxiety to others and to reduce stress", and lists among the conditions that feed clusters "an overheated, crowded waiting area, prolonged standing, lack of privacy for immunization" and access to text messaging and social media while waiting (same).

### What the evidence is

The support is observational. A 1985 review of outbreaks in workplaces listed "recurrences in affected individuals when they congregate" among their typical features ([Boxer, Journal of Occupational Medicine 27(12), December 1985, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=3910765)). The neurologists who treated the Le Roy students noted, in their words "anecdotally", that symptoms improved with home schooling and worsened "when they were around other children who exhibited vocal and motor tics" ([McVige and colleagues, Journal of Child Neurology, 2026](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC13018255/fullTextXML)). No trial has tested separation against its absence, and the record may flatter it: the WHO manual notes "general reluctance to publish reports on such events in the peer-reviewed literature once the situation is resolved and the immunization programme resumed" ([WHO, 2019](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/8c4a911c-e176-431b-8144-da5cddec4757/content)).

Separation also has a cost the guidance names: it can hide a real cause in one patient. Jones warned that "any large group involved in an outbreak may include individual persons with unrelated organic causes of illness" and that "Persons with severe, persistent or unusual symptoms should be carefully evaluated for other possible etiologies" ([Jones, 2000](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2000/1215/p2649.html)). The cases in which a psychogenic diagnosis later gave way to a toxin are in [the part on mass psychogenic illness](/learn/mass-psychogenic-illness-when-symptoms-spread).

## Investigation, and the dilemma of the label

### Investigate, but not too much

The Tennessee study ended with a sentence often repeated since: "Alleviation of the anxiety surrounding an episode of mass psychogenic illness requires prompt recognition and a detailed investigation" ([Jones and colleagues, New England Journal of Medicine 342(2), 13 January 2000, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=10631279)). The same author warned in the same year that investigation can feed the fear it is meant to settle: "extensive testing can fuel perceptions that a physiologic or toxic cause of the illness is suspected and may be interpreted as inconsistent with reassuring messages" ([Jones, 2000](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2000/1215/p2649.html)). The WHO manual adds that in published vaccination clusters "The clinical management of cases in clusters that involved invasive testing or treatment was reported in some cases to be harmful" ([WHO, 2019](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/8c4a911c-e176-431b-8144-da5cddec4757/content)). The Le Roy neurologists put the bind in one sentence: "Providers face a conundrum navigating diagnostic pathways and treatment without over- or underattention because both can worsen symptoms" ([McVige and colleagues, 2026](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC13018255/fullTextXML)).

### Naming it

The hardest decision is whether, when and to whom to say that an outbreak is psychogenic. Jones set out both options: "Labeling an outbreak as psychogenic may minimize unnecessary testing and halt spread, but the perception of a less than thorough investigation can lead to mistrust and anger"; the alternative, reassuring patients while avoiding a name, gives no diagnosis, and "the absence of an identified source may lead to continued concern, symptom spread and increased testing" ([Jones, 2000](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2000/1215/p2649.html)). The WHO manual avoids the old names: outbreaks of this kind are often called "mass psychogenic illness" or "mass hysteria" in the literature, "however, patients may find the term demeaning, which may exacerbate the problem"; its advice to communicators is "Be tactful. Remember that ISRR are not the patient's 'fault', nor are they 'crazy'" ([WHO, 2019](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/8c4a911c-e176-431b-8144-da5cddec4757/content)).

Who says it, and to whom first, mattered in the cases on record. In El Carmen de Bolívar, the researchers who studied the response wrote that "Parents and the population saw the MPI diagnosis as dismissing the girls' physiological distress and caused public outrage" ([Simas, Munoz, Arregoces and Larson, Human Vaccines & Immunotherapeutics 15(1), 2019](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6363158/fullTextXML)). In Le Roy the treating clinic found that "Acknowledging and affirming the patient's illness and struggle was found to aid in patient recovery as opposed to adversarial speech or attitude" ([McVige and colleagues, 2026](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC13018255/fullTextXML)). Writing from the WHO's Department of Vaccines and Immunisation in 2003, John Clements argued that "a dismissive approach may actually be harmful. Unless the spokesperson has already earned a high level of trust, the public are not likely to be convinced easily that nothing was wrong with the vaccine until it has been tested" ([Clements, Drug Safety 26(9), 2003, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=12814329)).

| Choice | What it may gain | What it may cost | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Name the outbreak as psychogenic early | less testing, a halt to spread | "mistrust and anger" if the investigation looks thin | [Jones, 2000](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2000/1215/p2649.html) |
| Reassure without naming | avoids the stigma of the name | "continued concern, symptom spread and increased testing" | [Jones, 2000](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2000/1215/p2649.html) |
| Test widely | rules out a real cause | tests read as a sign that a toxin is suspected; harm from invasive procedures | [Jones, 2000](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2000/1215/p2649.html); [WHO, 2019](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/8c4a911c-e176-431b-8144-da5cddec4757/content) |
| Test narrowly and say so | fewer procedures | a dismissive look, unless the spokesperson is already trusted | [Clements, 2003](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=12814329) |
| Tell the families before the press | the diagnosis arrives with explanation | none named in the sources read | [Simas and colleagues, 2019](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6363158/fullTextXML) |

## Reassurance: when it calms and when it backfires

### In the clinic

For a diagnosed outbreak of psychogenic illness, reassurance is the treatment. Jones wrote that "Once the diagnosis is determined, reassuring patients is the primary therapy", and his list ends with two reassurances: that long-term effects are not expected and, "As appropriate", that "thorough clinical, epidemiologic and environmental investigations have identified no toxic cause for the outbreak or reason for further concern" ([Jones, 2000](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2000/1215/p2649.html)). The same list tells doctors to "Acknowledge that symptoms experienced by the patient are real" and to remind patients that rumours of suspected causes "are not equivalent to confirmed results" (same).

### In public

Risk communicators speaking to whole populations warn against the same word. The CDC's manual on crisis and emergency risk communication (CERC), in its chapter on the psychology of a crisis (2019 update), says "It is important to remember that an over-reassured public isn't the goal", lists "Don't over-reassure" among its advice, and argues that "more harm is done by officials trying to avoid panic by withholding information or over-reassuring the public" ([CDC, CERC: Psychology of a Crisis, 2019](https://www.cdc.gov/cerc/media/pdfs/CERC_Psychology_of_a_Crisis.pdf)). It also questions the fear behind over-reassurance: "Contrary to what you may see in the movies, people seldom act completely irrationally during a crisis" (same). The WHO's outbreak communication guidelines of 2005 say the same: "Evidence shows that public panic is rare and most rare when people have been candidly informed"; "the longer officials withhold worrisome information, the more frightening the information will seem when it is revealed, especially if it is revealed by an outside source"; and trust-building measures are "often counter-intuitive (such as acknowledging uncertainty or avoiding excessive reassurance)" ([WHO, Outbreak communication guidelines, 2005](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/5b99e481-dcb3-43a5-88ab-895e1cf97ebb/content)). A public's mistaken view, the guidelines add, "should still be acknowledged publicly and corrected, not ignored, patronized or ridiculed" (same).

### The British beef inquiry

The case most often cited for over-reassurance is bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE). The public inquiry chaired by Lord Phillips of Worth Matravers concluded in October 2000 that <Claim status="established" by="by the BSE Inquiry, whose report the government welcomed">"The Government did not lie to the public about BSE. It believed that the risks posed by BSE to humans were remote. The Government was preoccupied with preventing an alarmist over-reaction to BSE because it believed that the risk was remote. It is now clear that this campaign of reassurance was a mistake"</Claim>, and that when the government announced on 20 March 1996 that BSE had probably been transmitted to humans, "the public felt that they had been betrayed. Confidence in government pronouncements about risk was a further casualty of BSE" ([The BSE Inquiry Report, volume 1, executive summary, October 2000, as filed with the Infected Blood Inquiry](https://www.infectedbloodinquiry.org.uk/sites/default/files/documents/CABO0000141_004%20-%20The%20BSE%20Inquiry%20Report%20Volume%201%20Findings%20and%20Conclusions%20-%2003%20Nov%202000.pdf)).

The responses in the House of Commons on 26 October 2000 are on record. The agriculture minister, Nick Brown, read those words to the House and said "The Government welcome the report". Tim Yeo, the member for South Suffolk and first to reply, said that the report made clear "that civil servants, other advisers and Ministers acted honourably and in good faith", quoted the inquiry's paragraph 1292, "the lessons to be learned are based on hindsight and relate to the overall approach of reassurance that was adopted. We do not consider that individuals should be criticised for following that approach", and added "I am truly sorry for what has happened". Douglas Hogg said he was "deeply sorry" for the victims' suffering and welcomed the inquiry's findings that the abattoir controls he had put in place in 1995 were effective ([Hansard, House of Commons, BSE Inquiry Report, 26 October 2000](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/2000/oct/26/bse-inquiry-report)). The bank run that followed a statement meant to reassure, at Northern Rock in 2007, is told in [the part on rumour and panic](/learn/rumour-panic-and-lynching).

### Two kinds of reassurance

The clinic and the public advice can look contradictory. The sources themselves draw a line of timing and evidence: Jones's reassurance comes "As appropriate", after investigations have found nothing, and is paired with saying that the symptoms are real; the BSE Inquiry faulted reassurance given while "the possibility of a risk to humans was not communicated to the public"; and the CERC chapter asks officials to say "What you know", "What you don't know" and "What process you are using to get answers" ([Jones, 2000](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2000/1215/p2649.html); [BSE Inquiry, 2000](https://www.infectedbloodinquiry.org.uk/sites/default/files/documents/CABO0000141_004%20-%20The%20BSE%20Inquiry%20Report%20Volume%201%20Findings%20and%20Conclusions%20-%2003%20Nov%202000.pdf); [CDC, 2019](https://www.cdc.gov/cerc/media/pdfs/CERC_Psychology_of_a_Crisis.pdf)). The Singapore koro statement of 1967, in the table above, shows that even accurate reassurance from a government can fail where the next voice, a medical association, succeeds; why is not explained in the account that records it.

## Risk communication: the manuals and their evidence

### The CDC's crisis manual

The CDC describes CERC as a framework "built around psychological and communication sciences," and "studies in the field of issues management, and lessons learned from emergency responses" ([CDC, CERC: Introduction, 2018 update](https://www.cdc.gov/cerc/media/pdfs/CERC_Introduction.pdf)). Its chapters carry different dates, from 2014 for the chapters on plans, spokespersons and the media to 2019 for the psychology chapter, and the manual's index page was last updated and reviewed on 20 November 2024 ([CDC, CERC Manual](https://www.cdc.gov/cerc/php/cerc-manual/index.html)). Six principles sit at its centre.

| Principle | The manual's words | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Be first | "Crises are time-sensitive. Communicating information quickly is crucial." | [CDC, 2018](https://www.cdc.gov/cerc/media/pdfs/CERC_Introduction.pdf) |
| Be right | "Accuracy establishes credibility. Information can include what is known, what is not known, and what is being done to fill in the gaps." | [CDC, 2018](https://www.cdc.gov/cerc/media/pdfs/CERC_Introduction.pdf) |
| Be credible | "Honesty and truthfulness should not be compromised during crises." | [CDC, 2018](https://www.cdc.gov/cerc/media/pdfs/CERC_Introduction.pdf) |
| Express empathy | "Crises create harm, and the suffering should be acknowledged in words." | [CDC, 2018](https://www.cdc.gov/cerc/media/pdfs/CERC_Introduction.pdf) |
| Promote action | "Giving people meaningful things to do calms anxiety, helps restore order, and promotes some sense of control." | [CDC, 2018](https://www.cdc.gov/cerc/media/pdfs/CERC_Introduction.pdf) |
| Show respect | "Respectful communication is particularly important when people feel vulnerable." | [CDC, 2018](https://www.cdc.gov/cerc/media/pdfs/CERC_Introduction.pdf) |

The reason for being first is the one that runs through this series: "For members of the public, the first source of information often becomes the preferred source" ([CDC, 2018](https://www.cdc.gov/cerc/media/pdfs/CERC_Introduction.pdf)). The psychology chapter explains the gap rumours fill: "The first message to reach us may be the accepted message, even though more accurate information may follow", and "In the absence of information, we may begin to speculate and fill in the blanks. This often results in rumors." It also notes, without using the term mass psychogenic illness, that "People in a crisis tend to have more unexplained physical symptoms", and that people "sometimes referred to as the 'worried well,' may heavily tax response resources by requesting medical treatment they do not need" ([CDC, 2019](https://www.cdc.gov/cerc/media/pdfs/CERC_Psychology_of_a_Crisis.pdf)).

### The WHO guideline and how it graded its evidence

The WHO's 2017 guideline on emergency risk communication is the most explicit about its evidence. It grades each recommendation by strength, "strong" or "conditional", and by the quality of the evidence, and it explains a choice that matters for every recommendation below: "Even though the underpinning evidence was assessed as 'moderate' or 'low' for many of the recommendations, the panel decided to make a strong recommendation because the reported experience (grey literature) and case examples indicated that the recommended action would yield clear benefits, was feasible and satisfied equity considerations" ([WHO, Communicating risk in public health emergencies: a WHO guideline for emergency risk communication (ERC) policy and practice, 2017](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/27ef4bb6-61ab-4870-b609-c09b2710633a/content)). A Portuguese edition was published in 2018 ([WHO, Comunicação de riscos em emergências de saúde pública, 2018](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/a35f2347-f077-4127-a43b-29eb871486af/content)).

| Recommendation, in the guideline's words | Strength | Quality of evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A.1: risk communication "should be linked to functioning and accessible services, be transparent, timely, easy-to-understand, acknowledge uncertainty" | strong | moderate |
| A.2: communication "should include explicit information about uncertainties associated with risks, events and interventions, and indicate what is known and not known at a given time" | strong | moderate |
| A.3: "Identify people that the community trusts and build relationships with them" | strong | moderate |
| B.4: risk communication "requires a defined and sustained budget" | strong | very low |
| C3.1: social media "may be used to engage the public, facilitate peer-to-peer communication, create situational awareness, monitor and respond to rumours" | conditional | moderate |
| C4.1: "Risk should not be explained in technical terms" | strong | moderate |
| C4.2: "Consistent messages should come from different information sources and emerge early in the outbreak" | strong | moderate |
| C4.3: "Messages should promote specific actions people can realistically take to protect their health" | strong | moderate |

Source for every row: [WHO, 2017](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/27ef4bb6-61ab-4870-b609-c09b2710633a/content).

On one question the guideline found nothing: "The systematic review found no evidence indicating which tools/approaches to monitoring and evaluation are most effective for refining and adjusting interventions", and it made that a priority for research (same). The guideline, in other words, says what to do, rates how sure it is, and admits that whether a given campaign worked is hard to measure.

### The infodemic

During the COVID-19 pandemic the WHO gave the information side of an outbreak a name. "An infodemic is too much information including false or misleading information in digital and physical environments during a disease outbreak. It causes confusion and risk-taking behaviours that can harm health" ([WHO, Infodemic, health topic page](https://www.who.int/health-topics/infodemic)). In May 2020 the World Health Assembly passed Resolution WHA73.1, which, according to a joint statement of the WHO and other United Nations (UN) agencies, "recognizes that managing the infodemic is a critical part of controlling the COVID-19 pandemic" ([WHO and others, Managing the COVID-19 infodemic, joint statement, 23 September 2020](https://www.who.int/news/item/23-09-2020-managing-the-covid-19-infodemic-promoting-healthy-behaviours-and-mitigating-the-harm-from-misinformation-and-disinformation)). The WHO's own page says that "more efforts are needed to better understand the scale of the infodemic, and impact of strategies used to manage it" ([WHO, Infodemic](https://www.who.int/health-topics/infodemic)).

## Debunking: the handbook, and the tests of its formats

### What the handbook advises about when to debunk

The Debunking Handbook 2020, written by 22 researchers, is used in [Why misbeliefs persist](/learn/why-misbeliefs-persist) for what a correction should contain: a factual alternative, the fact-myth-fallacy-fact order, and the evidence on backfire. Its advice on whether to correct at all is less often quoted. "Everyone has limited time and resources, so you need to pick your battles. If a myth is not spreading widely, or does not have the potential to cause harm now or in the future, there may be no point in debunking it", and "the less said about an unknown myth the better"; the authors ask "If no one has heard of the myth that earwax can dissolve concrete, why correct it in public?" ([Lewandowsky, Cook, Ecker and colleagues, The Debunking Handbook 2020](https://sciencelearning.umd.edu/files/2020/10/DebunkingHandbook2020.pdf)). They add that any correction "necessarily reinforces a rhetorical frame" created by someone else, since "You cannot correct someone else's myth without talking about it", and suggest that "highlighting the enormous success and safety of a vaccine might create a more positive set of talking points than debunking a vaccine-related myth" (same).

The handbook draws the choice as a chart, summarized here.

| The situation | The handbook's advice |
| --- | --- |
| The myth is largely unknown | "Keep monitoring, withhold debunking, but be prepared." |
| The myth has traction, but framing is still fluid | "Provide accurate information, withhold debunking so you set agenda." |
| Misinformation is expected | prebunk or inoculate: "Provide warning." and "Explain misleading techniques." |
| The framing is fixed | "Debunk often and properly" |

Source for every row: [The Debunking Handbook 2020](https://sciencelearning.umd.edu/files/2020/10/DebunkingHandbook2020.pdf). Prebunking is the subject of [Prebunking and inoculation](/learn/prebunking-and-inoculation).

On who should correct, the handbook says that "perceived trustworthiness of a debunking source may matter more than its perceived expertise", that "people with negative attitudes toward vaccines distrust formal sources of vaccine-related information (including generally-trusted health organizations)", and advises: "Tailor the message to the audience and use a messenger trusted by the target group" (same). The WHO's vaccination manual makes the same move in the field: "The presence of local community leaders and local health workers who are familiar to those being immunized can reassure them and support the immunization team" ([WHO, 2019](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/8c4a911c-e176-431b-8144-da5cddec4757/content)).

### What experiments found about format

Practical advice often recommends a "truth sandwich": a fact, then the myth labelled as false, then the fact again. Several experiments since 2021, including some by the handbook's own authors, have tested whether the order of a correction matters.

| Study | Participants | What was compared | Finding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Swire-Thompson, Cook, Butler, Sanderson, Lewandowsky and Ecker, 2021 | four experiments | myth-first, fact-first, fact-only and myth-only corrections | "the impact of a correction on beliefs and inferential reasoning was largely independent of the specific format used"; myth-first did better than fact-first after a delay in one experiment |
| König, 2023 | 302 | three truth-sandwich texts against a control text on healthy eating | lower agreement with the specific myth targeted |
| Kotz, Giese and König, 2023 | 4,906, representative of Germany | truth sandwich against a "bottom-heavy" structure; statement against question headlines | beliefs "unaffected by the text structures and headline formats"; corrections worked less well with people holding "strong pre-existing, incongruent attitudes and distrust in science" |
| Swire-Thompson, Butler and Rapp, 2025 | 1,046 in two experiments | truth sandwich against a "bottom-loaded" refutation, retested after two weeks | "both correction formats were highly effective", with "no evidence that the truth sandwich format enhanced the effectiveness of corrections" |
| Blase and Dohle, 2026 | 2,684, representative by gender and age | truth sandwiches from a general practitioner, a health institution or a company | no direct difference by source for nutrition myths; an indirect effect through trustworthiness and psychological reactance; vaccine myths too rare in the sample to test well |

Sources: [Swire-Thompson and colleagues, Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications 6, 2021](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=34964924); [König, British Journal of Health Psychology 28(4), 2023](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=37186138); [Kotz, Giese and König, British Journal of Health Psychology 28(4), 2023](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=37263771); [Swire-Thompson, Butler and Rapp, Journal of Educational Psychology 117(6), 2025](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=40860910); [Blase and Dohle, Health Communication 41(13), 2026](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=41674185).

The 2021 paper concluded that "simply providing corrective information, regardless of format, is far more important than how the correction is presented"; the 2025 paper that "advocacy for the truth sandwich correction above other simpler formats are unwarranted"; and Kotz and colleagues that "writers may need to pay attention to the text being comprehensive, trustworthy and persuasive" (same). <Claim status="disputed" by="by the 2023 to 2025 format experiments against earlier practical advice">Whether the order of a correction matters</Claim> is the open point; that a clear correction lowers belief in the myth it targets is common to all five. None of these studies tested a correction during a panic or an outbreak.

## Journalism and the courts after Escola Base

### What the case left

The 1994 accusation against the owners of the Escola Base in São Paulo, the closed inquiry and the damages awarded against the State of São Paulo and the press are told in [The Satanic panic, false memories and the Escola Base case](/learn/satanic-panic-false-memories-and-the-escola-base-case), with a table of what Brazil's journalism code and laws now say. That part also records the journalist Alex Ribeiro's conclusion that a simple retraction does not repair the moral damage done by false reporting. Two later legal changes bear on that point. No source read for this part shows that either was adopted because of Escola Base.

### The right of reply

Law 13,188 of 11 November 2015 regulates the right of reply. It gives anyone offended by a report "o direito de resposta ou retificação, gratuito e proporcional ao agravo" (the right of reply or rectification, free of charge and in proportion to the offence), and it states that a spontaneous retraction, even with the same prominence as the offence, does not prevent a reply "nem prejudicam a ação de reparação por dano moral" (nor prejudice a claim for moral damages) ([Lei nº 13.188, de 11 de novembro de 2015, arts. 2 and 2 §3, Senado Federal](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/585000/publicacao/15638070)). The request must be made within 60 days; if the outlet does not publish the reply within seven days the offended person may sue, the judge has 24 hours after the summons to decide on urgent publication, and the sentence is due within 30 days (arts. 3, 5, 7 and 9, same).

The Supremo Tribunal Federal (STF), Brazil's supreme court, upheld the core of the law on 11 March 2021, ruling on two direct actions of unconstitutionality (ações diretas de inconstitucionalidade, ADIs 5415 and 5436). It declared constitutional the rule that a retraction does not cancel the reply and the short deadlines, and struck from article 10 only the words "em juízo colegiado prévio" (by prior collegiate decision), so that a single appeal judge can suspend a reply order ([STF, ADI 5.436, judgment of 11 March 2021, Senado Federal](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/33351822/publicacao/34138059); [STF, ADI 5.415, Senado Federal](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/33351748/publicacao/34138031)). The court's summary explains the reasoning: "O direito de resposta possibilita que a liberdade de expressão seja exercida em sua plenitude, pois é acionado apenas após a livre e irrestrita manifestação do pensamento" (the right of reply allows freedom of expression to be exercised in full, since it is triggered only after thought has been freely expressed), and "O direito de resposta não se confunde com direito de retificação ou retratação" (the right of reply is not the same as a right to rectification or retraction) ([ADI 5.436](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/33351822/publicacao/34138059)). Justice Marco Aurélio dissented in both cases (same).

### Liability for repeating a false accusation

The second change concerns the press repeating an accusation made by someone else, as in 1994. In Recurso Extraordinário (RE) 1075412, an appeal by the newspaper Diário de Pernambuco against damages for publishing, in May 1995, an interview in which the interviewee blamed the former congressman Ricardo Zaratini for the 1966 bombing at the Guararapes airport in Pernambuco, the STF fixed a thesis of general application (Tema 995) on 29 November 2023 ([STF, news item, 29 November 2023](https://noticias.stf.jus.br/postsnoticias/stf-fixa-criterios-para-responsabilizar-empresas-jornalisticas-por-divulgacao-de-acusacoes-falsas/)). The newspaper had argued that the lower court's decision violated press freedom; the majority, led by Justice Edson Fachin, held that a press company could be liable only if "havia indícios concretos da falsidade da imputação" (there were concrete signs that the accusation was false) at the time and it failed in "o dever de cuidado na verificação da veracidade dos fatos" (the duty of care in checking the facts); Justices Marco Aurélio and Rosa Weber dissented (same).

The press objected. <Claim status="attributed" by="by the Associação Nacional de Jornais (ANJ), the national newspaper association, in a filing to the court">The thesis's "vagueza dos termos empregados potencializa práticas persecutórias e cria o famigerado 'chilling effect' sobre os veículos de comunicação, tornando inevitável a autocensura"</Claim> (the vagueness of its terms encourages persecution and creates the notorious chilling effect, making self-censorship inevitable), argued the ANJ on 15 April 2024, asking that liability require "dolo ou erro grosseiro" (intent or gross error) ([ANJ, filing in RE 1.075.412/PE, 15 April 2024](https://www.anj.org.br/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/30-RE-1075412-manifestacao-ANJ.pdf)). On 20 March 2025, ruling unanimously on appeals by the newspaper and by the Brazilian Association of Investigative Journalism (Associação Brasileira de Jornalismo Investigativo, Abraji), the court rewrote the thesis.

| Point | Thesis of 20 March 2025, in the court's words |
| --- | --- |
| When a press company is liable for an interviewee's false accusation of a crime | only if "comprovada sua má-fé" (its bad faith is proved), shown by intent from prior knowledge of the falsehood or by "culpa grave decorrente da evidente negligência na apuração da veracidade do fato" (gross fault from evident negligence in checking the fact) and publication without the reply of the person accused or at least an attempt to hear them |
| Live interviews | the outlet is not liable for the interviewee's words, but must ensure "o exercício do direito de resposta em iguais condições, espaço e destaque" (a right of reply on equal terms, space and prominence) |
| Content left online | once the falsehood is established, removal "de ofício ou por notificação da vítima" (on the outlet's own initiative or on the victim's notice) |

Source: [STF, news item, 20 March 2025](https://noticias.stf.jus.br/postsnoticias/stf-fixa-criterios-para-responsabilizacao-de-empresas-jornalisticas-que-divulgarem-acusacoes-falsas/). No source read for this part measures whether the right of reply or the new liability rule reduces the harm of false accusations, or how often either is used.

## Laws against false alarms, and their risks

### A 1941 offence

Brazil's Law of Misdemeanours (Lei das Contravenções Penais, LCP) of 1941 punishes, in article 41, anyone who "Provocar alarma, anunciando desastre ou perigo inexistente, ou praticar qualquer ato capaz de produzir pânico ou tumulto" (causes alarm by announcing a non-existent disaster or danger, or does any act capable of producing panic or tumult), with "prisão simples, de quinze dias a seis meses, ou multa" (simple imprisonment of fifteen days to six months, or a fine) ([Decreto-Lei nº 3.688, de 3 de outubro de 1941, Senado Federal](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/528775/publicacao/15771787)). In April 2020 <Claim status="reported" by="by A Gazeta">authorities had used it against false COVID-19 alarms "em ao menos três capitais" (in at least three state capitals)</Claim>: in Recife, where, when the city had five suspected cases, a man presenting himself as a health worker claimed there were 61 infected and the city asked the police to investigate; and in Vitória, where the police charged a man who had posted that he was infected and would use his gun to defend himself, and who later learned that his test was negative ([A Gazeta, Lei Penal é usada contra fake news relacionada à covid-19, 13 April 2020](https://www.agazeta.com.br/brasil/lei-penal-e-usada-contra-fake-news-relacionada-a-covid-19-0420)).

The same report recorded the doubts. Recife's chief municipal attorney, Rafael Figueiredo, acknowledged that the deterrent effect was low: "Embora seja uma punição de menor potencial, elas podem perder a primariedade penal, além de ter que gastar horas indo se explicar à Justiça" (although it is a minor penalty, they can lose their clean record, besides spending hours explaining themselves in court). Ivar Hartmann, coordinator of the Centre for Technology and Society at the law school of the Fundação Getulio Vargas (FGV) in Rio de Janeiro, said "Não adianta colocar uma proibição na lei e esperar que o Judiciário julgue todos os casos. O volume de posts torna inviável" (it is no use putting a ban in the law and expecting the courts to judge every case; the volume of posts makes it unworkable), and Sérgio Lütdke, coordinator of Projeto Comprova, an initiative that brings Brazilian newspapers together against disinformation, that "Atitudes mais reativas tendem a ser melhores do que as que podem levar à censura" (more reactive approaches tend to be better than those that can lead to censorship) (same).

### The European Union's crisis mechanism

The European Union (EU) took a different route: it regulates platforms, not speakers. Article 36 of the Digital Services Act (DSA) lets the European Commission, on a recommendation of the European Board for Digital Services, require very large online platforms and search engines to assess whether their services contribute to a crisis and to apply "specific, effective and proportionate measures"; a crisis exists "where extraordinary circumstances lead to a serious threat to public security or public health in the Union or in significant parts of it" ([Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, Digital Services Act, 19 October 2022, Article 36](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065)). The article limits the measures to "a period not exceeding three months", extendable by three months, leaves the choice of measures to the platforms, and requires the Commission to weigh "the possible failure of the measures to respect the fundamental rights enshrined in the Charter" (same). The sources read for this part do not show whether the mechanism has been used. The wider dispute over platform law is in [Platform law: Section 230, the DSA and the Marco Civil](/learn/platform-law-section-230-the-dsa-and-the-marco-civil) and [Content moderation at scale](/learn/content-moderation-at-scale).

Other legal answers to panic are told where they arose: deposit insurance against bank runs and India's Supreme Court on lynchings after rumours in [Rumour, panic and lynching](/learn/rumour-panic-and-lynching), and the prosecutions of people accused of spreading rumours about the schoolgirl illnesses in Iran in [Mass psychogenic illness](/learn/mass-psychogenic-illness-when-symptoms-spread).

## What ended the steered panics

When someone was steering, the records show a different kind of ending: exposure and pressure on the person steering. The East Anglian witch-finders withdrew after opponents sued one of them, appealed to Parliament, which sent a commission, and questioned them through the justices; the Soviet AIDS campaign was curtailed when the US made it an issue for the summits of 1987; the Columbian Chemicals hoax collapsed when a local official telephoned the plant; and the coronavirus share promotions of 2020 met trading suspensions and prosecutions. Each case, with its evidence, is in [How manipulators harness collective panic](/learn/how-manipulators-harness-collective-panic).

## Time, and what it leaves behind

Many outbreaks end on their own. The WHO manual says of vaccination clusters that "Usually, the spread of such situations and their resolution are relatively rapid" ([WHO, 2019](https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/8c4a911c-e176-431b-8144-da5cddec4757/content)), and in Le Roy every patient recovered as coverage faded ([McVige and colleagues, 2026](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC13018255/fullTextXML)). That is why the endings in the first table cannot be credited to the remedy that came last.

Denmark shows what an ending can cost when a scare touches a public programme. Uptake of the first HPV vaccine dose had been around 90% for girls born from 1998 to 2000 and fell to 54% for girls born in 2003; a study of 8,524 news items found no link between coverage and uptake until July 2013 and a negative correlation of -0.52 afterwards, a change that coincided with a rise in Google searches for HPV side effects and with "media coverage with negative content" ([Suppli and colleagues, BMC Public Health 18, 10 December 2018](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=30526589)). A cohort study of 328,779 girls found uptake at 83.6% of its baseline in the period of some negative coverage and 49.6% in the period of extensive negative coverage; "After the information campaign, HPV vaccine uptake recovered to its baseline level (109.2%, 95% CI:90.1%-132.4%) due in part to catch-up doses", but "an estimated 26,000 fewer girls initiated the vaccine than if uptake had not declined" ([Hansen, Schmidtblaicher and Brewer, Vaccine 38(7), February 2020, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=31918860)). One author declared that he "has served as a paid advisor to and received research grants from Merck & Co., Inc." (same). A second study by the same group found that the second dose of the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine, which 91% of the girls who received it had received in the same week as their first HPV dose, fell with it ([Gørtz, Brewer, Hansen and Ejrnæs, Vaccine 38(28), June 2020, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=32418796)). The recovery came after a campaign, years later; whether the campaign caused it, and how much of the recovery was catch-up, the abstract does not fully separate. The broader history of vaccine scares is in [Health scares](/learn/health-scares-from-the-revolta-da-vacina-to-the-autism-myth).

## What to remember

- The records show endings, not causes: courts closed, groups separated, investigations reported, guarantees given, coverage faded, and most episodes would have faded with time.
- Separation is the most consistent advice for outbreaks of psychogenic illness, resting on case reports and clinical experience rather than trials.
- Investigation must be prompt and detailed, yet extensive testing can signal that a toxin is suspected; naming an outbreak psychogenic can halt it or anger the families, depending on how and to whom it is said.
- Reassurance is the treatment once an investigation is complete, and a mistake when it runs ahead of the evidence, as the BSE Inquiry found in 2000.
- The CDC's CERC principles and the WHO's 2017 guideline agree on speed, candour, consistency and giving people something to do; the WHO rated most of its recommendations on moderate evidence and found none on how to evaluate them.
- The Debunking Handbook advises choosing battles and trusted messengers; experiments from 2021 to 2026 found that a clear correction works and that its order matters little.
- Brazil's right of reply (2015, upheld in 2021) and the STF's 2025 thesis on false accusations, like the 1941 false-alarm offence and the EU's crisis mechanism, carry risks to free expression that their critics named on the record.
