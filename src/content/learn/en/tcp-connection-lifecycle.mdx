The Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) turns an unreliable packet network into a reliable byte stream, and it does so with ceremony: connections are explicitly created and explicitly ended. The retired fundamentals exam asked for the possible reasons a connection terminates and the possible causes when one fails to establish - questions whose answers are the core literacy of reading any packet capture.

## Establishment: the three-way handshake

A client proposes with a SYN, the server accepts with a SYN-ACK, the client confirms with an ACK - three segments, after which both sides hold a connection and data may flow. The handshake is also where the two sides exchange their starting sequence numbers and options; everything reliable about TCP is bootstrapped in these three packets, which is why a capture filter for SYNs is the fastest census of who is trying to talk to whom.

## Why establishment fails

When the handshake does not complete, the capture shows one of a short list. SYN, then silence: the SYN never arrived, or the SYN-ACK died on the way back - a routing gap, a silent drop at a firewall, or a dead host. SYN answered by RST: something reached the destination network and was actively refused - most often no listener on that port, sometimes a policy device resetting on purpose; either way, refusal is information, and it arrives fast. SYN answered by an [ICMP unreachable](/learn/icmp-ping-and-traceroute): a router or host named the failure explicitly. And SYN retransmitted in a slowing rhythm before giving up: the classic signature of a silent drop, distinguishable from refusal precisely by how long it takes to fail.

## The middle act: how much can be in flight

Once the handshake is done, the question becomes how fast the stream can go, and the first ceiling is arithmetic. A sender may have only so much unacknowledged data on the path at once: no more than the smaller of the receiver's advertised window and its own congestion window ([RFC 5681, section 2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5681.html#section-2)). At most one window can be delivered per round trip, so the window divided by the round-trip time is a ceiling on throughput whatever the link rate, and filling a path takes a window at least as large as its bandwidth-delay product, the link rate multiplied by the round-trip time. The TCP header's window field is 16 bits, so without the window scale option the largest window is 64 KiB ([RFC 7323, section 1.1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7323.html#section-1.1)): at a 100 ms round trip that caps a connection at about 5.24 Mbit/s however fast the link is, while a 100 Mbit/s path at the same round trip needs 1,250,000 bytes in flight. Window scaling, offered in the SYN and answered in the SYN-ACK, is what lifts the limit ([RFC 7323, section 2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7323.html#section-2)), up to a window of 1 GiB, because the scale exponent is limited to 14 ([RFC 7323, section 2.3](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7323.html#section-2.3)).

Loss meets the same arithmetic but is read differently. A lost segment does not always cost a full retransmission timeout: duplicate acknowledgements let the sender resend early and recover (fast retransmit and fast recovery, [RFC 5681, section 3.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5681.html#section-3.2)), while a timeout stalls the flow for much longer. A capture that shows the acknowledgements around the loss tells you which of the two happened; an aggregate retransmission counter does not.

The congestion window those two signals cut follows four algorithms ([RFC 5681, section 3](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5681.html#section-3)). It starts at the initial window: at most four segments of the sender maximum segment size (SMSS) when the SMSS is 1,095 bytes or less, three up to 2,190 bytes and two above that, or a single segment if the SYN or the SYN-ACK was lost ([RFC 5681, section 3.1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5681.html#section-3.1)). RFC 6928 allows up to min(10 × MSS, max(2 × MSS, 14,600 bytes)), ten segments of 1,460 bytes, as an Experimental RFC, its status at the RFC Editor on 26 October 2026 ([RFC 6928, section 2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6928.html#section-2)). Below the slow start threshold, ssthresh, which starts arbitrarily high, slow start lets each ACK for new data add up to one SMSS, so a window acknowledged segment by segment doubles every round trip; above it, congestion avoidance adds about one SMSS per round trip and never more, and when the two are equal either may run (section 3.1). A loss sets ssthresh to no more than max(FlightSize / 2, 2 × SMSS), FlightSize being the data sent and not yet acknowledged. Found by three duplicate ACKs, the missing segment is resent at once, cwnd becomes ssthresh plus 3 × SMSS and grows by one SMSS for each further duplicate ACK, then falls back to ssthresh when the next ACK for new data arrives ([RFC 5681, section 3.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5681.html#section-3.2)); found by the retransmission timer, cwnd falls to the loss window of one full-sized segment, whatever the initial window was, and slow start begins again (section 3.1). These are the Reno-style rules of RFC 5681, a Draft Standard that RFC 9438 updates. RFC 9438, a Proposed Standard, specifies CUBIC, which grows the window with a cubic function rather than a linear one and which, it says, the Linux, Windows and Apple stacks have adopted as their default ([RFC 9438, abstract](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9438.html)). ## Termination: the polite close and the abort

Connections end two ways. The graceful close is a FIN exchange - each side says it is done sending, each acknowledges, and the stream drains completely; this is the normal end of a finished conversation. Each FIN closes only its sender's direction: the side that has not yet sent its own FIN may go on sending, and the connection stays half-closed until it does ([RFC 9293, section 3.6.1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9293.html#section-3.6.1)). The abort is a RST: an immediate, unilateral teardown that discards anything in flight. RSTs are legitimate - a process crashed, a host received data for a connection it no longer knows, an idle-timeout fired on a middlebox, an application chose abort over lingering - but every RST has a reason, and finding it is often the whole investigation. The practical reading: FINs at the end of a complete exchange are health; a RST in the middle of one is the event to explain, and its direction tells you who lost patience.

Each end moves through a fixed set of states while this happens: LISTEN, SYN-SENT, SYN-RECEIVED, ESTABLISHED, FIN-WAIT-1, FIN-WAIT-2, CLOSE-WAIT, CLOSING, LAST-ACK, TIME-WAIT and the fictional CLOSED, which stands for no connection at all ([RFC 9293, section 3.3.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9293.html#section-3.3.2)). In the handshake the client goes from SYN-SENT to ESTABLISHED when the SYN-ACK arrives, and the server from LISTEN through SYN-RECEIVED to ESTABLISHED when the last ACK arrives ([RFC 9293, section 3.5](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9293.html#section-3.5)); a SYN and a FIN each take one sequence number and an ACK takes none, which is why the acknowledgement of a FIN is its sequence number plus one ([RFC 9293, section 3.4](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9293.html#section-3.4)). The end that closes first goes through FIN-WAIT-1 and FIN-WAIT-2, the other through CLOSE-WAIT and LAST-ACK, and when both close at once each goes through CLOSING ([RFC 9293, section 3.6](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9293.html#section-3.6)). Every segment after the handshake carries an acknowledgement number ([RFC 9293, section 3.1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9293.html#section-3.1)), so the second FIN can arrive already acknowledging the first; the first end then goes straight from FIN-WAIT-1 to TIME-WAIT, a transition note 2 of the state diagram adds (section 3.3.2), and the close takes three segments instead of four. TIME-WAIT is there to be sure the other end received the last acknowledgement and to keep delayed segments away from a new connection (section 3.3.2): the end that closed first MUST stay in it for twice the Maximum Segment Lifetime (MSL) ([RFC 9293, section 3.6.1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9293.html#section-3.6.1)), so after a simultaneous close both ends wait (section 3.6). The specification takes the MSL to be 2 minutes, an engineering choice it says may change ([RFC 9293, section 3.4.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9293.html#section-3.4.2)); how long an operating system actually waits is its own setting and is not covered here. An abort skips all of it: the ABORT call sends a RST, deletes the connection and enters CLOSED ([RFC 9293, section 3.10.5](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9293.html#section-3.10.5)), and a valid RST moves its receiver to CLOSED ([RFC 9293, section 3.5.3](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9293.html#section-3.5.3)), although section 3.5.2 adds that the side issuing a reset should enter TIME-WAIT, to lighten the load on busy servers.

Between proxies the picture doubles: a full-proxy device like a BIG-IP terminates the client's connection and originates its own to the server, so one user transaction is two lifecycles with independent handshakes, timeouts, and resets. Correlating them - and noticing which of the two failed - is the daily work the [capture syntax article](/learn/bigip-tcpdump-syntax) equips, and the reason this lifecycle deserves to be reflex before the first capture is ever taken.
