On the morning of 2 May 2022 a trader at Citigroup Global Markets Limited (CGML), Citigroup's London-headquartered broker-dealer, meant to sell a basket of European shares worth $58 million and, in the words of the United Kingdom's Financial Conduct Authority (FCA), "made an inputting error while entering the basket in an order management system"; part of what was typed then went to "a trading algorithm" designed to sell slices through the rest of the day, and $1.4 billion of shares were sold before the trader cancelled ([FCA, 22 May 2024](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-fines-cgml-27-million); [FCA, Final Notice to CGML, 22 May 2024](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/citigroup-global-markets-limited-2024.pdf)). The FCA's final notice names the two systems: an order management system (OMS) called PTE, and an electronic trading system called CitiSmart that took the basket "using a VWAP trading algorithm, where individual parent and child orders were generated", VWAP meaning volume-weighted average price ([FCA, Final Notice to CGML, 22 May 2024](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/citigroup-global-markets-limited-2024.pdf)). The controls that failed that morning belong to [the next part of this series](/learn/pre-trade-risk-and-market-access-controls). This part is about the two systems themselves.

This part of the series opened by [Electronic trading: from the pit to the microsecond](/learn/electronic-trading-from-the-pit-to-the-microsecond) follows the part on how one order's messages and states unfold in the Financial Information eXchange (FIX) protocol, [The life of an order in FIX](/learn/the-life-of-an-order-in-fix). Here the subject is the software that creates those messages before they reach a broker or an exchange, and that keeps the record afterwards. After reading, you will be able to say what an order management system and an execution management system (EMS) each do, and for whom; follow an institutional order from the portfolio manager's screen to the venues and back; read how a broker's algorithm is described and how its parameters travel in a FIX message; compute the benchmarks an execution is judged by on a small worked example; and place the main vendors, their owners and the Brazilian providers by what their own pages and filings say. Claims that companies make about their own products are attributed to them.

## Two systems, two questions

An OMS answers three questions: what the firm decided to trade, for which portfolios, and whether it is allowed. An EMS answers a fourth: how to get it done in the market. The vendors' own product descriptions draw the line in those terms. Bloomberg describes its buy-side Asset and Investment Manager (AIM) as a "Global, multi-asset buy-side solution for portfolio management, trading, compliance and operations", and its sell-side Trade Order Management Solutions (TOMS) as a "Global, multi-asset sell-side solution for front-end inventory, trading, and middle- and back-office operations" ([Bloomberg, Order Management System, undated](https://professional.bloomberg.com/products/trading/order-management-system/)). For execution, Bloomberg says its execution management solutions "focus on end-to-end workflows and allow you to dynamically manage your positions, portfolios and trading risk" ([Bloomberg, Execution Management, undated](https://professional.bloomberg.com/products/trading/execution-management-system/)).

A competition authority looked at the same market from outside. When ION Investment Group agreed in 2018 to buy Fidessa, a London trading software company, the United Kingdom's Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) examined how the products divide: by asset class, by stage of the trade (research and analytics, front-office execution, middle-office clearing and reconciliation, back-office risk and compliance) and by customer type ([CMA, ION and Fidessa decision, 3 August 2018, published 22 August 2018](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5b7d5c8aed915d14db8821fe/ion_fidessa_full_text_decision.pdf)). It defined the buy side as institutions that "manage investments in capital assets", such as pension funds, and the sell side as institutions, such as banks and brokers, that "facilitate trading in capital assets"; it found that "buy-side customers require execution management systems which are different to the tools required by sell-side customers", while noting that some third parties said "both buy-side and sell-side customers typically purchase the same product but use it differently" ([CMA, 2018](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5b7d5c8aed915d14db8821fe/ion_fidessa_full_text_decision.pdf)). Third parties also told the CMA that "middle and back office software is frequently handled in-house, while front office software is typically purchased externally" ([CMA, 2018](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5b7d5c8aed915d14db8821fe/ion_fidessa_full_text_decision.pdf)), which is why the OMS and EMS business is a vendor business.

The line between the two systems is not fixed. Raptor Trading Systems sells OMEX, which it calls "A multi-asset OEMS designed to unify trading workflows in a single, broker-neutral environment", OEMS meaning an order and execution management system ([Raptor Trading Systems, products, undated](https://raptortrading.com/products/)); the London Stock Exchange Group (LSEG) described TORA, which it agreed to buy in 2022, as offering "an order and execution management system (OEMS) and portfolio management system (PMS)" ([LSEG, 22 February 2022](https://lseg.com/en/media-centre/press-releases/2022/lseg-acquire-tora)). The table below sets out the division of labour the sources describe; a given product may cover one column, both, or parts of each.

| Function | Usually in the OMS | Usually in the EMS | Where it is described |
|---|---|---|---|
| Portfolio decision becomes an order | Yes | | Bloomberg AIM, "portfolio management, trading, compliance and operations" |
| Compliance and investment-rule checks before trading | Yes | | Bloomberg AIM; Charles River's "secondary compliance" users |
| Choosing a broker and an algorithm, setting its parameters | Sometimes | Yes | FIXatdl standard; Virtu's Triton EMS |
| Market data, positions and trading risk while the order works | | Yes | Bloomberg EMSX, TSOX and FXEM |

Sources for the table: [Bloomberg, Order Management System](https://professional.bloomberg.com/products/trading/order-management-system/); [Bloomberg, Execution Management](https://professional.bloomberg.com/products/trading/execution-management-system/); [Charles River, home page, figures as of the third quarter of 2026](https://www.crd.com/); [FIX Trading Community, FIXatdl](https://fixtrading.org/standards/fix-algorithmic-trading-definition-language/); [Virtu Financial, Form 10-K for 2025, 20 February 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1592386/000159238626000009/virt-20251231.htm); [FIX Trading Community, Recommended Risk Control Guidelines, 2012](https://fixtrading.org/download/recommended-risk-control-guidelines-2012/); [Raptor Trading Systems, products](https://raptortrading.com/products/); [SEC staff, Report on Algorithmic Trading in U.S. Capital Markets, 5 August 2020](https://www.sec.gov/files/Algo_Trading_Report_2020.pdf).

## Buy side and sell side: the same order seen twice

On the buy side, the order starts with a portfolio manager's decision and ends with shares allocated to the right funds. Raptor's description of what institutional managers need is typical of how vendors frame it: managing "complex, multi-asset portfolios across global markets from a single order management environment", with allocation across several custodians and post-trade integration ([Raptor Trading Systems, buy side, undated](https://raptortrading.com/buy-side/)). Charles River Development, now part of State Street, says that "Over half of the world’s 100 largest investment managers use Charles River technology" and gives the assets its clients manage on the platform both as over $59 trillion and as more than $60 trillion, figures dated to the third quarter of 2026 that include clients using the platform "for purposes of secondary compliance" ([Charles River, about us, read 16 November 2026](https://www.crd.com/company/about-us/); [Charles River, home page](https://www.crd.com/)). These are the company's own figures and are not checked by any source read for this article. BlackRock describes its Aladdin platform as one that "unifies the investment management process through a common data language", and notes that it is "both a user and provider" of it ([BlackRock, Aladdin, undated](https://www.blackrock.com/aladdin)).

On the sell side, the same order arrives as a client message to be accepted, checked and worked. The FIX Trading Community's risk working group for the Americas distinguished two kinds of client order in 2012. Orders sent to a broker's algorithm or straight to a market are "low touch order flow", handled "without human intervention"; worked orders, "for example, cash single stock and/or program trading", "typically rely on the sales trader to make a decision regarding whether to accept or decline a given order" ([FIX Trading Community, Recommended Risk Control Guidelines, 2012](https://fixtrading.org/download/recommended-risk-control-guidelines-2012/)). The same document says that algorithmic orders "are typically delivered through a FIX interface from either a 3rd party vendor Order Management System (OMS) / Execution Management System (EMS) or the client’s proprietary OMS", that the client's order "is routed to a strategy engine, and does not interact directly with the market", and that "The algorithm makes all decisions when to place and execute child order slices" ([FIX Trading Community, 2012](https://fixtrading.org/download/recommended-risk-control-guidelines-2012/)). A broker therefore runs its own OMS for the orders it receives, its own algorithms and routers for working them, and its own controls in front of the exchange, the subject of [Pre-trade risk and market access: limits, sponsored access and kill switches](/learn/pre-trade-risk-and-market-access-controls).

Raptor's sell-side page lists what a broker's system is asked to do in the same breath: route "across all major venues, brokers and algo providers", manage allocations and clearing, and produce reports for the Consolidated Audit Trail (CAT) and the SEC's order routing and execution quality rules, Rules 605 and 606 ([Raptor Trading Systems, sell side, undated](https://raptortrading.com/sell-side/)). Those regulatory reports are treated in [Surveillance and regulatory reporting](/learn/surveillance-and-regulatory-reporting-mifid-ii-cat-and-cvm).

## Where they sit in the flow: one order, end to end

The table follows a buy order for 100,000 shares from an asset manager to the market and back. The steps and message names come from the sources cited under the table; the order, the broker and the numbers are invented for illustration, and the message-level detail of each step is in [The life of an order in FIX](/learn/the-life-of-an-order-in-fix) and [Anatomy of a FIX message and the session layer](/learn/anatomy-of-a-fix-message-and-the-session-layer).

| Step | System | What happens | What crosses the wire |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Asset manager's OMS | The portfolio manager's decision becomes an order for several funds; investment rules are checked | Nothing yet: the order lives in the OMS |
| 2 | OMS to EMS | The order is staged to the trading desk's EMS | An internal message, often FIX |
| 3 | EMS | The trader picks a broker and a strategy on a ticket drawn from the broker's FIXatdl file and fills in its parameters | NewOrderSingle (35=D) with the strategy and its parameters |
| 4 | Broker's OMS and risk checks | The order is checked against the client's limits before it is acknowledged | ExecutionReport (35=8) accepting, rejecting or pausing it |
| 5 | Broker's algorithm | The parent order is cut into child orders over time | Child orders, each a new order to a router |
| 6 | Smart order router | Each child order is sent to a venue chosen by price, size and cost | Orders to exchanges and other venues in their own protocols |
| 7 | Back up the chain | Fills come back to the broker and then to the EMS and OMS | ExecutionReport (35=8) messages, drop copies |
| 8 | OMS | Fills are allocated to the funds and the result is analysed | Allocation messages; transaction cost analysis (TCA) reports |

Sources for the steps: the parent order and child orders ([SEC, Release 34-84528, Disclosure of Order Handling Information, 2 November 2018](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/final/2018/34-84528.pdf)); the strategy engine, the risk checks and the client OMS ([FIX Trading Community, Recommended Risk Control Guidelines, 2012](https://fixtrading.org/download/recommended-risk-control-guidelines-2012/)); the FIXatdl ticket ([FIX Trading Community, FIXatdl 1.1 specification with errata, 21 December 2010](https://fixtrading.org/packages/fixatdl-1-1-with-errata-20101221/)); the allocations and the reports ([Raptor Trading Systems, products](https://raptortrading.com/products/)).

Step 4 carries a detail that shows how closely the buy side's OMS and the sell side's systems must agree. When a broker's checks find an algorithmic order outside the client's limits, the 2012 guidelines proposed that the broker pause it rather than reject it outright, and tell the client's OMS with an ExecutionReport whose order status is pending new (39=A) and whose OrdRejReason (103) carries a new value, 21, "Algorithm risk threshold breached" ([FIX Trading Community, 2012](https://fixtrading.org/download/recommended-risk-control-guidelines-2012/)). The value is now in the standard, defined as a case where "A sell-side broker algorithm has detected that a risk limit has been breached which requires further communication with the client" ([FIX Latest, OrdRejReasonCodeSet](https://orchimate.org/fixtrading/fix-latest/codeSets/OrdRejReasonCodeSet)). The authors admitted in 2012 that "The majority of buy sides are not currently running order management systems that supports the “Pending New” message", and that the fallback would most likely be "a phone conversation" ([FIX Trading Community, 2012](https://fixtrading.org/download/recommended-risk-control-guidelines-2012/)).

## Execution algorithms: what the EMS asks a broker to do

The SEC described in 2018 what had replaced the telephone. In current electronic markets, it wrote, broker-dealers handle institutional orders with "sophisticated institutional order execution algorithms and smart order routing systems that decide the timing, pricing, and quantity of orders routed to a number of various trading centers", dividing a large parent order into many smaller child orders sent over time to different venues "in accordance with a particular strategy" ([SEC, Release 34-84528, 2 November 2018](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/final/2018/34-84528.pdf)). The SEC's staff, in a report Congress required, put a date on it by quoting the Commission's 2016 proposal: the "manual handling of institutional orders is increasingly rare, and has been replaced by sophisticated institutional order execution algorithms and smart order routing systems" ([SEC staff, Report on Algorithmic Trading in U.S. Capital Markets, 5 August 2020](https://www.sec.gov/files/Algo_Trading_Report_2020.pdf)).

The same staff report names the core strategies that "many brokers" offer: a volume-weighted average price (VWAP), a time-weighted average price (TWAP), a minimum implementation shortfall, which it glosses as "minimizing the total costs of trading relative to the market price at the time a trading decision is made", and trading at a specified percentage of market volume, PVOL or POV ([SEC staff, 2020](https://www.sec.gov/files/Algo_Trading_Report_2020.pdf)). It adds that "many of the core types of algorithms are more or less similar across brokers", that brokers "generally allow some degree of customization", and, citing an estimate by Greenwich Associates of April 2019, that "approximately two-thirds of institutional order flow is sent through the three largest broker algorithm suites" ([SEC staff, 2020](https://www.sec.gov/files/Algo_Trading_Report_2020.pdf)).

| Strategy | What it aims at, in the SEC staff's words or by its name | What it leaves to the market |
|---|---|---|
| VWAP | The average price of all trades in the period, weighted by their size | The order tracks the day's volume, wherever the price goes |
| TWAP | The average price over time, each interval counting equally | The order trades evenly through the clock, whether volume is there or not |
| Implementation shortfall | "the total costs of trading relative to the market price at the time a trading decision is made" | Trades faster when delay is costly, slower when impact is costly |
| POV | "a specified percentage of market volume" | The finish time depends on how much the rest of the market trades |

Source for the definitions: [SEC staff, 2020](https://www.sec.gov/files/Algo_Trading_Report_2020.pdf). The CGML incident above used a VWAP algorithm ([FCA, Final Notice, 2024](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/citigroup-global-markets-limited-2024.pdf)), and the sell programme at the start of the Flash Crash of 6 May 2010 was an algorithm that targeted a share of the previous minute's volume, as [High-frequency trading: what it is and the dispute](/learn/high-frequency-trading-what-it-is-and-the-dispute) shows from the regulators' report. An execution algorithm follows its instructions, and the instructions may not include the price.

### What the law calls an algorithm, and what it does not

The second Markets in Financial Instruments Directive (MiFID II) of the European Union (EU) defines algorithmic trading as trading in which "a computer algorithm automatically determines individual parameters of orders", and then excludes a whole class of software: the definition "does not include any system that is only used for the purpose of routing orders to one or more trading venues or for the processing of orders involving no determination of any trading parameters" ([EU, Directive 2014/65/EU, article 4(1)(39), 15 May 2014](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32014L0065)). A pure order router is outside the rules for algorithms; an algorithm that decides timing, price or size is inside them. When the European Securities and Markets Authority (ESMA) reviewed the regime in 2021, it recorded stakeholders asking, among other things, for "Differentiating for pure execution algorithms and automated execution processes (TWAP, VWAP, SOR, Autospreaders, etc)", SOR meaning smart order routing ([ESMA, MiFID II/MiFIR review report on algorithmic trading, ESMA70-156-4572, 28 September 2021](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/library/esma70-156-4572_mifid_ii_final_report_on_algorithmic_trading.pdf)). Which of those systems counts as an algorithm decides which firm controls apply to it, the subject of the next part.

### Worked example: one order, three benchmarks

The numbers below are invented; the arithmetic is the point. A portfolio manager decides to buy 100,000 shares when the price is 20.00. The market trades 1,000,000 shares in four hours, in the pattern of the first three columns. Two algorithms are compared: one that takes 10% of each hour's volume, which is what a POV order does and what a VWAP order aims at, and one that buys 25,000 shares each hour, as a TWAP order would. For simplicity, each is assumed to trade at the hour's average price.

| Hour | Market volume | Hour's average price | POV fills (10% of volume) | TWAP fills (equal per hour) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10:00 to 11:00 | 300,000 | 20.05 | 30,000 | 25,000 |
| 11:00 to 12:00 | 250,000 | 20.08 | 25,000 | 25,000 |
| 12:00 to 13:00 | 250,000 | 20.11 | 25,000 | 25,000 |
| 13:00 to 14:00 | 200,000 | 20.14 | 20,000 | 25,000 |
| Total | 1,000,000 | | 100,000 | 100,000 |

The market's VWAP is the value traded divided by the volume: (300,000 × 20.05 + 250,000 × 20.08 + 250,000 × 20.11 + 200,000 × 20.14) ÷ 1,000,000 = 20,090,500 ÷ 1,000,000 = 20.0905. The POV order's average price is (30,000 × 20.05 + 25,000 × 20.08 + 25,000 × 20.11 + 20,000 × 20.14) ÷ 100,000 = 20.0905, exactly the VWAP, because it bought the same share of every hour. The TWAP order's average is (20.05 + 20.08 + 20.11 + 20.14) ÷ 4 = 20.095.

| Measure | POV order | TWAP order |
|---|---|---|
| Average price paid | 20.0905 | 20.0950 |
| Against the market VWAP of 20.0905 | 0.00 basis points | 2.24 basis points worse |
| Against the decision price of 20.00, per share | 0.0905 | 0.0950 |
| Against the decision price, in basis points | 45.25 | 47.50 |
| Against the decision price, in money for 100,000 shares | 9,050 | 9,500 |

(A basis point is one hundredth of one per cent; 0.0905 ÷ 20.00 = 0.4525%, or 45.25 basis points.) Judged against VWAP, the first algorithm was perfect and the second nearly so. Judged against the price at the moment of decision, the shortfall measure the SEC staff describes, both cost the fund about 45 to 48 basis points, because the price rose all day while they bought. Neither number is wrong: they answer different questions, and the choice of benchmark is part of the instruction the EMS sends.

## Telling a broker's algorithm what to do: FIXatdl

Every broker's VWAP has its own parameters: start and end times, a participation cap, a limit price, whether to finish at any cost. An EMS that offers dozens of brokers must draw a ticket for each, and the FIX Trading Community wrote a standard so that brokers could publish their tickets as data. The FIX Algorithmic Trading Definition Language (FIXatdl) version 1.1 was approved by the Global Technical Committee's governance board on 2 March 2010 and republished with errata on 21 December 2010 ([FIX Trading Community, FIXatdl 1.1 specification with errata, 21 December 2010](https://fixtrading.org/packages/fixatdl-1-1-with-errata-20101221/)). The community describes it as a standard for describing algorithms' user interfaces and parameters, "enabling consistent presentation and interpretation of algo configuration across order and execution management systems" ([FIX Trading Community, FIX Algorithmic Trading Definition Language, undated](https://fixtrading.org/standards/fix-algorithmic-trading-definition-language/)).

The specification splits a FIXatdl file into two halves: a "Data Contract", which "Defines the wire-value interface of an algorithmic order", with each parameter's type, legal range and validation rules, and a graphical user interface (GUI) half that recommends the controls to draw and where ([FIXatdl 1.1, 2010](https://fixtrading.org/packages/fixatdl-1-1-with-errata-20101221/)). The language lives in six Extensible Markup Language (XML) schema files covering the core data, validation rules, layout, flow rules that enable or disable controls, and lists of regions and time zones ([FIXatdl 1.1, 2010](https://fixtrading.org/packages/fixatdl-1-1-with-errata-20101221/)); [Reading XML structure](/learn/reading-xml-structure) and [XML namespaces explained](/learn/xml-namespaces-explained) cover the format itself.

The specification's own example defines a percentage-of-volume strategy with two parameters, a participation rate and a "Force Completion" flag, and shows what an EMS would send when a trader enters 15% and ticks the box ([FIXatdl 1.1, 2010](https://fixtrading.org/packages/fixatdl-1-1-with-errata-20101221/)):

```
. . 35=D|11=1234|55=AXP|. . |7000=v|7001=1|957=2|958=PctVol|959=11|960=0.15|958=FC|959=13|960=Y
```

| Tag | Field | Value | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 35 | MsgType | `D` | NewOrderSingle, a new order |
| 11 | ClOrdID | `1234` | The client's identifier for the order |
| 55 | Symbol | `AXP` | The instrument in the example |
| 7000 | User-defined | `v` | The strategy, here the POV algorithm; the file declares tag 7000 as the strategy identifier |
| 7001 | User-defined | `1` | The strategy's version, declared as tag 7001 |
| 957 | NoStrategyParameters | `2` | Two parameters follow |
| 958 | StrategyParameterName | `PctVol` | First parameter's name |
| 959 | StrategyParameterType | `11` | Percentage |
| 960 | StrategyParameterValue | `0.15` | 15% of volume |
| 958 | StrategyParameterName | `FC` | Second parameter's name |
| 959 | StrategyParameterType | `13` | Boolean |
| 960 | StrategyParameterValue | `Y` | Yes, force completion |

Sources: the message and the strategy declaration ([FIXatdl 1.1, 2010](https://fixtrading.org/packages/fixatdl-1-1-with-errata-20101221/)); the fields 957 to 960, each defined in FIX as the number, name, datatype and value of a strategy parameter in the group StrategyParametersGrp ([FIX Latest, NoStrategyParameters](https://orchimate.org/fixtrading/fix-latest/fields/NoStrategyParameters); [StrategyParameterName](https://orchimate.org/fixtrading/fix-latest/fields/StrategyParameterName); [StrategyParameterType](https://orchimate.org/fixtrading/fix-latest/fields/StrategyParameterType); [StrategyParameterValue](https://orchimate.org/fixtrading/fix-latest/fields/StrategyParameterValue)); the codes 11 for Percentage and 13 for Boolean ([FIX Latest, StrategyParameterTypeCodeSet](https://orchimate.org/fixtrading/fix-latest/codeSets/StrategyParameterTypeCodeSet)).

Two details show how FIX bends to practice. First, the strategy identifier sits in a user-defined tag, not in the standard field: the specification notes that "At one time TargetStrategy (tag 847) was intended to carry this information. However, most providers use a user-defined field for this purpose" ([FIXatdl 1.1, 2010](https://fixtrading.org/packages/fixatdl-1-1-with-errata-20101221/)); TargetStrategy, added in FIX 4.4, reserves values of 1000 and above "for bi-laterally agreed upon user defined values" ([FIX Latest, TargetStrategy](https://orchimate.org/fixtrading/fix-latest/fields/TargetStrategy)). Second, a broker may accept the parameters either in the standard repeating group or in its own user-defined tags, and the file says which: with the group the order carries `957=2|958=PctVol|...`, without it the same order would carry `7002=0.15|7003=Y` ([FIXatdl 1.1, 2010](https://fixtrading.org/packages/fixatdl-1-1-with-errata-20101221/)). Both forms are FIX; which one a given broker expects is part of its rules of engagement, which [The FIX protocol: origins, versions and the community](/learn/the-fix-protocol-origins-versions-and-the-community) describes as the problem FIX Orchestra was built to make machine-readable.

FIX has since added fields to identify the algorithm behind an execution. The FIX Trading Community says that its Extension Pack EP297 extends the ExecutionReport with AlgoCertificateID (3012), AlgoTrialID (3097) and LastAlgoID (3098), "so firms can determine which algorithm/certificate (and, in trial contexts, which configuration) actually generated an execution", and links the work to European rules that require venues to make members certify that their algorithms were tested ([FIX Trading Community, Automated Trading and Risk Management, undated](https://fixtrading.org/guidelines/automated-trading/)).

## Smart order routing, and why it exists

A router decides where each child order goes. In the United States the reason is regulatory as much as commercial: Regulation NMS (National Market System) obliges trading centres to avoid trading through better prices displayed elsewhere, which [The rules that rewired markets](/learn/the-rules-that-rewired-markets-reg-ats-reg-nms-mifid-and-brazil) works through with an example. The FIX Trading Community's 2012 guidelines classed a smart order router (SOR) with direct market access (DMA) rather than with algorithms, because of its "shorter execution horizon, and a lower tolerance for latency", and noted that many brokers route a client's directed DMA order through their SOR, "which acts as a pass-through to the market per the client’s instructions" ([FIX Trading Community, 2012](https://fixtrading.org/download/recommended-risk-control-guidelines-2012/)).

Routing decisions are also where conflicts of interest hide, which is why regulators ask brokers to disclose them. When the SEC amended Rule 606 in 2018, it required brokers, on a customer's request, to report how they routed that customer's "not held" orders in stocks, orders on which the broker has discretion, and it declined to make brokers sort their strategies into categories because "order routing strategies are difficult to place into well-defined categories due to the complex nature of today’s order execution algorithms and smart order routing systems" ([SEC, Release 34-84528, 2 November 2018](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/final/2018/34-84528.pdf)). The same release states that "Best execution is the broker-dealer’s legal obligation for all orders, whether from retail or institutional customers" ([SEC, 2018](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/final/2018/34-84528.pdf)).

In Europe, MiFID II requires investment firms to "take all sufficient steps to obtain, when executing orders, the best possible result for their clients taking into account price, costs, speed, likelihood of execution and settlement, size, nature or any other consideration relevant to the execution of the order" ([EU, Directive 2014/65/EU, article 27, 15 May 2014](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32014L0065)). The reports that firms and venues had to publish under that article did not survive review: the 2024 amending directive says that "those reports are rarely read and do not enable investors or other users of those reports to make meaningful comparisons", and deletes the paragraph of MiFID II that required firms to publish, each year, "the top five execution venues in terms of trading volumes" for each class of instrument ([EU, Directive 2014/65/EU, article 27(6)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32014L0065); [EU, Directive (EU) 2024/790, 28 February 2024](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024L0790)). In Brazil, the securities regulator's best execution rule of 2022 is covered in [The rules that rewired markets](/learn/the-rules-that-rewired-markets-reg-ats-reg-nms-mifid-and-brazil).

## Choosing among brokers: algo wheels and transaction cost analysis

An institution that uses several brokers' algorithms has to decide which to send each order to, and then judge the result. The SEC staff report describes both halves. Institutional investors "generally conduct, either on their own or through a third-party provider, transaction cost analysis in order to assess the quality of executions received from different brokers, different algorithms, or in different market centers"; and, increasingly, they route through "a tool called an “algo wheel.”" ([SEC staff, 2020](https://www.sec.gov/files/Algo_Trading_Report_2020.pdf)). "An algo wheel, which can be operated by an investor or provided by a third-party, connects investors into multiple broker algorithm offerings, and chooses brokers and individual algorithms based on specified constraints or preferences", and it "can enable a firm to switch between different brokers without input from a human trader" ([SEC staff, 2020](https://www.sec.gov/files/Algo_Trading_Report_2020.pdf)). The staff cited two industry surveys of how many buy-side firms used one, about a quarter in one estimate and about 14% in the other ([SEC staff, 2020](https://www.sec.gov/files/Algo_Trading_Report_2020.pdf)); the gap between the two is itself a reason to treat such figures with care.

The wheel is now an EMS feature. Virtu Financial, which bought Investment Technology Group (ITG) on 1 March 2019 for $30.30 a share in cash ([Virtu Financial, Form 8-K, 1 March 2019](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1592386/000110465919012016/a19-5723_18k.htm)), describes the latest release of its broker-neutral Triton EMS as including "the Algo Wheel, an algorithmic way for a portfolio manager to intelligently allocate volume between different providers" ([Virtu Financial, Form 10-K for 2025, 20 February 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1592386/000159238626000009/virt-20251231.htm)). A wheel allocates flow by measured results; measured against which benchmark is the question the worked example above makes concrete.

## What to remember

- An OMS records what was decided, for which portfolios, and whether it is allowed; an EMS works the order in the market through brokers' algorithms and routers. Products increasingly combine the two as an OEMS.
- The CMA, reviewing the ION and Fidessa deal in 2018, found buy-side and sell-side needs different enough to treat them separately, and front-office software usually bought from vendors rather than built.
- Brokers' core algorithms are VWAP, TWAP, implementation shortfall and percentage of volume, by the SEC staff's 2020 account; MiFID II counts as algorithmic trading only systems that decide order parameters, not pure routers.
- The benchmark is part of the instruction: the same execution can match VWAP exactly and still cost 45 basis points against the decision price.
- FIXatdl (2010) lets a broker publish its algorithm's ticket as XML; the parameters travel in StrategyParametersGrp (tags 957 to 960) or in the broker's own tags.
- Ownership moved fast: Charles River to State Street and Eze to SS&C on 1 October 2018, Fidessa to ION cleared in 2018, ITG to Virtu in 2019, TORA to LSEG agreed in 2022. In Brazil, B3's certified list and Sinacor, present in 95% of brokers by B3's count, are the fixed points.
