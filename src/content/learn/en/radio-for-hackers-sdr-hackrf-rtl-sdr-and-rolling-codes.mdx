A car key, a garage remote, a gate opener and an alarm sensor all do the same thing: they put a short burst of radio into the air and trust that only the right receiver will act on it. For decades that trust rested on the cost of radio equipment, because building a transmitter for an arbitrary frequency and modulation took a laboratory. Software-defined radio removed that cost. ## Radio as software

The Wireless Innovation Forum, an industry body for radio technology, defines a software-defined radio as a "Radio in which some or all of the physical layer functions are software defined" ([Wireless Innovation Forum, Introduction to SDR, undated](https://winnforum.org/introduction-to-software-definied-radio-winnforum-sdr-101/)). In a conventional receiver, the tuner, the filters and the demodulator are fixed circuits built for one kind of signal; in an SDR, a wideband front end and an analogue-to-digital converter hand the raw waveform to a computer, and everything after that is code. The GNU Radio project, the free software toolkit most SDR work is built on, says the benefit plainly: because the software can be replaced, "the same hardware can be used to create many kinds of radios for many different communications standards" ([GNU Radio, About GNU Radio, undated](https://www.gnuradio.org/about/)).

Michael Ossmann, who designed the HackRF, put the same idea in hardware terms when he introduced the project on 22 June 2012: a software radio peripheral digitizes radio waveforms the way a sound card digitizes audio, and "It's like a very fast sound card with the speaker and microphone replaced by an antenna" ([Michael Ossmann, Introducing HackRF, 22 June 2012](https://ossmann.blogspot.com/search/label/hardware)). He added that "A single software radio platform can be used to implement virtually any wireless technology (Bluetooth, GSM, ZigBee, etc.)" (same source). The samples such a device delivers are pairs of numbers, the in-phase and quadrature (I/Q) components of the signal at each instant; Great Scott Gadgets lists "8-bit quadrature samples (8-bit I and 8-bit Q)" among the HackRF One's specifications ([Great Scott Gadgets, HackRF One, undated](https://greatscottgadgets.com/hackrf/one/)).

That is the whole security story in one sentence. A receiver that records I/Q samples has captured the signal itself, not a decoded message, so it can store a transmission it does not understand and, if the hardware can transmit, play it back. Nothing in the radio layer distinguishes the original from the copy. Whether a system survives depends entirely on what its protocol does above the radio, which is why the rest of this article is about codes and counters rather than antennas.

## 2012: the television dongle that became a radio

The cheapest SDR in the world began as a mistake in perspective. Mass-produced Digital Video Broadcasting, Terrestrial (DVB-T) television dongles built around Realtek's RTL2832U chip were sold for watching television on a laptop; RTL-SDR Blog, the company that now sells improved versions, says that "With the combined efforts of Antti Palosaari, Eric Fry and Osmocom (in particular Steve Markgraf) it was found that the raw I/Q data on the RTL2832U chipset could be accessed directly", which turned the tuner into "a wideband software defined radio via a custom software driver developed by Steve Markgraf" ([RTL-SDR Blog, About RTL-SDR, read 16 November 2026](https://www.rtl-sdr.com/about-rtl-sdr/)). Alexandru Csete, the amateur radio operator who writes the Gqrx receiver, recorded the moment on 2 April 2012: "It all started" on a kernel mailing list when Antti Palosaari, a developer of Linux television drivers, found that an ezcap dongle "can be used to stream raw I/Q samples to the host computer", which is in fact how the dongle supports FM and DAB (Digital Audio Broadcasting) reception ([Alexandru Csete, RTL2832U based software defined radios, 2 April 2012](https://www.oz9aec.net/radios/gnu-radio/rtl2832u-based-software-defined-radios)). Csete wrote that Steve Markgraf of OsmoSDR then created the rtl-sdr package to tune the dongle and dump I/Q data to a file, that Balint Seeber added a GNU Radio source block a few weeks later, and that he bought his own dongle in the United Kingdom for £9.50 including value added tax (same source).
What the dongle can do is modest and well documented by RTL-SDR Blog: it is "a very cheap ~$30 USB dongle" that, depending on the model, "could receive frequencies from 500 kHz up to 1.75 GHz", and "Note that RTL-SDRs cannot transmit" ([RTL-SDR Blog, About RTL-SDR](https://www.rtl-sdr.com/about-rtl-sdr/)). The maximum sample rate is 3.2 million samples per second, but the dongle is unstable at that rate; the highest rate that does not drop samples is 2.56 million, and the native resolution of 8 bits gives an effective number of bits estimated at about seven (same source). The company insists that "No person or company owns RTL-SDR", credits Palosaari, Fry and Osmocom with the discovery, and describes its own role as one of several firms producing improved dongles (same source).

The improved dongles are what most people buy today. RTL-SDR Blog's store listed, on 16 November 2026, the V3 dongle (R860 tuner, also sold as R820T2) at US$34.95 to US$39.95 and the newer V4L (Lite) dongle with the R828S tuner at US$37.95, each with a 1 part-per-million temperature-compensated crystal oscillator (TCXO), an SMA antenna connector and a software-switchable 4.5 V bias tee for powering amplifiers ([RTL-SDR Blog, Buy RTL-SDR dongles, read 16 November 2026](https://www.rtl-sdr.com/buy-rtl-sdr-dvb-t-dongles/)). The V4 uses an R828D tuner and covers 24 to 1,766 MHz directly, with high frequency (HF) reception below 28.8 MHz by upconversion rather than the V3's direct sampling ([RTL-SDR Blog, V4 users guide, read 16 November 2026](https://www.rtl-sdr.com/v4/); [RTL-SDR Blog, About RTL-SDR](https://www.rtl-sdr.com/about-rtl-sdr/)). The same store page warns that sellers on marketplace sites pass off cheaper dongles under the RTL-SDR Blog name and that blue, green or square silver cases, or units advertised as "Pro", are not the company's ([RTL-SDR Blog, Buy RTL-SDR dongles](https://www.rtl-sdr.com/buy-rtl-sdr-dvb-t-dongles/)).

The uses the hobby settled on are mostly benign and mostly about listening. RTL-SDR Blog's tutorial menu runs from aircraft tracking with Automatic Dependent Surveillance-Broadcast (ADS-B) and ship tracking with the Automatic Identification System (AIS) to weather satellite images, pager decoding, radio astronomy at the hydrogen line, and a tutorial on replay attacks with an RTL-SDR and a Raspberry Pi transmitter ([RTL-SDR Blog, About RTL-SDR](https://www.rtl-sdr.com/about-rtl-sdr/)). The receive-only dongle cannot replay anything by itself; it records, and the record is enough to study a remote's modulation and code before anyone touches a transmitter.

## Great Scott Gadgets and the HackRF

Great Scott Gadgets describes itself in one sentence, "Great Scott Gadgets puts open source tools into the hands of innovative people", and says that everything it produces, hardware designs, software and educational material, is released under open source licences ([Great Scott Gadgets, About, read 16 November 2026](https://greatscottgadgets.com/about/)). Michael Ossmann is its founder and chief technology officer and does most of the hardware design; Liz Hendrex Stephens is its chief executive and chief operating officer (same source). Before the HackRF, the company's best-known tool was the Ubertooth, a 2.4 GHz platform for Bluetooth experiments: Ubertooth Zero was demonstrated at ToorCon 12 on 24 October 2010 and Ubertooth One at ShmooCon 7 on 29 January 2011 ([Ubertooth documentation, History, undated](https://ubertooth.readthedocs.io/en/latest/history.html)), and Ubertooth One is now "a retired product" that the company has no plan to manufacture again ([Great Scott Gadgets, Ubertooth One, read 16 November 2026](https://greatscottgadgets.com/ubertoothone/)). The company also made the 2013 badge for the tenth anniversary of the Hackers to Hackers Conference in Brazil ([H2HC](/glossary/h2hc)), an ARM Cortex-M3 development board meant as a platform for a next-generation GoodFET ([Great Scott Gadgets, H2HC 2013 Badge, undated](https://greatscottgadgets.com/h2hc2013badge/)).

### From a research grant to a Kickstarter

Ossmann's introduction of June 2012 explains the motive: he had used software radio techniques for wireless security research for years, taught a workshop each year at ToorCon San Diego, and wanted a low-cost portable platform that would fit in a laptop bag; "with support from DARPA's CFT program", the Cyber Fast Track programme of the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA), he wrote, he was finally able to build it, and he expected a retail price "in the neighborhood of $300" ([Michael Ossmann, Introducing HackRF, 22 June 2012](https://ossmann.blogspot.com/search/label/hardware)). In October 2012 he and Jared Boone gave the ToorCon 14 keynote, "HackRF: A Low Cost Software Defined Radio Platform", and announced a beta programme that Cyber Fast Track funding made possible (same source, post of 25 October 2012).

The beta hardware was called Jawbreaker. On 9 May 2013 Ossmann wrote that more than 500 Jawbreakers were in production and that he planned to give nearly all of them away, with invitation codes handed out at ToorCon 14 and the 2012 GNU Radio Conference; Jawbreaker could "transmit or receive virtually any radio signal from 30 MHz to 6 GHz", and he thanked Cyber Fast Track for enabling both the development and the free beta units ([Michael Ossmann, Giving Away HackRF, 9 May 2013](https://ossmann.blogspot.com/2013/05/giving-away-hackrf.html)). The project's documentation lists Jawbreaker as the beta test platform, with half-duplex operation from 30 MHz to 6 GHz at 8 to 20 million samples per second ([HackRF documentation, Jawbreaker, read 16 November 2026](https://hackrf.readthedocs.io/en/latest/jawbreaker.html)).

Production funding came from the public. By 12 August 2013, the Kickstarter campaign Ossmann had launched to fund mass production had passed its US$80,000 target and drawn more than US$300,000 in pledges, and a pledge of US$275 bought a HackRF with an enclosure and antenna, with delivery expected in January 2014, according to CNX Software ([CNX Software, $275 HackRF Open Source Software Defined Radio (SDR) Platform, 12 August 2013](https://www.cnx-software.com/2013/08/12/275-hackrf-open-source-software-defined-radio-sdr-platform/)).

### HackRF One

The production board is the HackRF One, "the first production hardware platform for the HackRF project" ([HackRF documentation, HackRF One, read 16 November 2026](https://hackrf.readthedocs.io/en/latest/hackrf_one.html)). Its maker's specifications are these: transmission or reception from 1 MHz to 6 GHz; a half-duplex transceiver, so it transmits or receives but not both at once; up to 20 million samples per second of 8-bit I and Q; compatibility with GNU Radio, SDR# and other software; software-controlled antenna port power of 50 mA at 3.3 V; SMA connectors for the antenna and for clock input and output; Hi-Speed USB 2.0; power from the USB bus; open source hardware ([Great Scott Gadgets, HackRF One](https://greatscottgadgets.com/hackrf/one/)). The documentation adds that supported sample rates run from 2 to 20 million per second, that the maximum safe input power is -5 dBm, and that the transmit power varies by frequency: between 5 and 15 dBm from 10 MHz to 2,170 MHz, 13 to 15 dBm between 2,170 and 2,740 MHz, and between -10 and 0 dBm from 4 to 6 GHz, so that "Through most of the frequency range up to 4 GHz, the maximum TX power is between 0 and 10 dBm" ([HackRF documentation, HackRF One](https://hackrf.readthedocs.io/en/latest/hackrf_one.html)). In plainer units, 0 dBm is one milliwatt and 10 dBm is ten.

The documentation then says something every buyer should read twice: "Before you transmit, know your laws. HackRF One has not been tested for compliance with regulations governing transmission of radio signals. You are responsible for using your HackRF One legally." (same source). The section on the spectrum rules, below, shows what that sentence means in three jurisdictions.

The HackRF grew an ecosystem. The rad1o, the badge of the 2015 Chaos Communication Camp, carried a HackRF-compatible transceiver covering about 50 MHz to 4,000 MHz ([HackRF documentation, rad1o, read 16 November 2026](https://hackrf.readthedocs.io/en/latest/rad1o.html)). At DEF CON 23 in August 2015, Jared Boone of ShareBrained Technology demonstrated the PortaPack H1, an add-on with a screen, controls, audio and a microSD slot that "turns HackRF One into a portable SDR platform" ([Great Scott Gadgets, PortaPack H1 at DEF CON 23, 23 August 2015](https://greatscottgadgets.com/2015/08-23-portapack-h1-at-def-con-23/)). Its firmware was forked by the community: today's Mayhem firmware describes itself as a fork of the Havoc firmware, "which itself was a fork of the PortaPack" firmware ([PortaPack Mayhem, README, read 16 November 2026](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/portapack-mayhem/mayhem-firmware/next/README.md)). Open hardware also invites copies: in December 2021 Ossmann wrote that HackRF One is the company's most frequently cloned product, that some clones work and others arrive dead, and that clones raise the support burden without paying the staff who answer it ([Great Scott Gadgets, Testing a HackRF Clone, 7 December 2021](https://greatscottgadgets.com/2021/12-07-testing-a-hackrf-clone/)). The chip shortage hit the genuine article too: in 2022 and 2023 the company reported that distributors quoted lead times of up to a year for key HackRF One parts and that the manufacturer's price of the MAX2837 chip had nearly doubled ([Great Scott Gadgets, HackRF tag, posts of 19 December 2022 and 20 March 2023](https://greatscottgadgets.com/tags/hackrf/)).

### HackRF Pro

On 26 June 2025 Great Scott Gadgets announced the HackRF Pro, with production to begin in July 2025 and first shipments planned for September ([Great Scott Gadgets, Meet HackRF Pro, 26 June 2025](https://greatscottgadgets.com/2025/06-26-meet-hackrf-pro/)). The shipments slipped: on 16 January 2026 the company announced that all prepaid reseller preorders had shipped, and a later post dates the start of shipping to "the end of 2025" ([Great Scott Gadgets, HackRF Pro Shipping Update, 16 January 2026](https://greatscottgadgets.com/2026/01-16-hackrf-pro-shipping-update/); [Great Scott Gadgets, Self-Repair Guide for HackRF Pro USB-C Connector, 15 March 2026](https://greatscottgadgets.com/2026/03-15-self-repair-guide-for-hackrf-pro-usb-c-connector/)). The March 2026 post explains that the USB-C connector's manufacturer had changed tooling between prototypes and production, making the ground contact less reliable on units from the first production round, and gives a repair (same source).

The HackRF Pro's specifications, from its product page: operation from 100 kHz to 6 GHz, tunable from 0 Hz to 7.1 GHz; still half-duplex and still up to 20 million 8-bit I/Q samples per second over USB; a built-in TCXO; a field-programmable gate array (FPGA) in place of the HackRF One's complex programmable logic device (CPLD); no DC spike; an extended-precision mode with 16-bit samples at low sample rates (a typical effective number of bits of 9 to 11) and a half-precision mode with 4-bit samples at up to 40 million per second; shielding around the radio; USB-C; and a "Facility to hardware-disable transmit mode" ([Great Scott Gadgets, HackRF Pro, read 16 November 2026](https://greatscottgadgets.com/hackrf/pro/)). The company's question-and-answer post explains that this last feature was added "with classroom use in mind": cutting one trace on the board disables transmission ([Great Scott Gadgets, HackRF Pro Q+A, 27 June 2025](https://greatscottgadgets.com/2025/06-27-hackrf-pro-q-a/)). The same post says the name "Pro" was chosen because the design is a refinement rather than a revolution, and that the company considered "10", "360" and "Tau" (same source). Great Scott Gadgets publishes no price on either product page and sells through resellers; its January 2026 list of HackRF Pro resellers includes Hak5, the subject of [an earlier part of this series](/learn/hak5-from-a-video-show-to-a-hacking-gear-company), and Lab401 ([Great Scott Gadgets, HackRF Pro Shipping Update](https://greatscottgadgets.com/2026/01-16-hackrf-pro-shipping-update/)).

## The YARD Stick One: packet radio below 1 GHz

The HackRF records and replays waveforms; the YARD Stick One speaks packets. Great Scott Gadgets calls it "a sub-1 GHz wireless test tool controlled by your computer", built on the same radio circuit as the IM-Me, a Mattel texting toy whose firmware hackers had already reprogrammed, and descended from the ToorCon 14 badge ([Great Scott Gadgets, YARD Stick One, read 16 November 2026](https://greatscottgadgets.com/yardstickone/)). Its name expands to Yet Another Radio Dongle. The maker's specifications: half-duplex transmit and receive; official frequencies of 300 to 348 MHz, 391 to 464 MHz and 782 to 928 MHz, with wider unofficial ranges; amplitude-shift keying (ASK), on-off keying (OOK), Gaussian frequency-shift keying (GFSK), 2-FSK, 4-FSK and minimum-shift keying (MSK); data rates up to 500 kbit/s; Full-Speed USB 2.0; the rfcat firmware written by the developer known as atlas, which lets an interactive Python shell drive the transceiver; and a low-pass filter for the 800 and 900 MHz bands (same source). Where a HackRF sees the whole spectrum as samples, the YARD Stick One demodulates and modulates the simple digital schemes that remotes, sensors and alarms actually use.

## Fixed codes: the garage that trusted a number

The oldest remotes send the same code every time. Samy Kamkar's OpenSesame, released on 4 June 2015, showed how little that protects: a typical fixed-code garage remote set with 8 to 12 dual in-line package (DIP) switches has at most 2 to the power 12, or 4,096, combinations, which he compared to "A 2-character password on a website" ([Samy Kamkar, OpenSesame, 4 June 2015](https://samy.pl/opensesame/)). His published arithmetic is a worked example in why key space matters:

| Step in Kamkar's analysis | Time to try every 8- to 12-bit code |
|---|---|
| Send each code 5 times, with a wait after each code, as a normal remote does | 1,771.52 seconds, about 29 minutes |
| Send each code once | 354.304 seconds, about 6 minutes |
| Send codes back to back, with no wait between them | 177.152 seconds, about 3 minutes |
| Exploit the receiver's shift register with a De Bruijn sequence, so that overlapping bits test many codes at once | 8.214 seconds for every 8- to 12-bit code |

The first three rows are from the OpenSesame page; the last is from his DEF CON 23 slides ([Samy Kamkar, OpenSesame](https://samy.pl/opensesame/); [Samy Kamkar, Drive It Like You Hacked It, DEF CON 23, 2015](https://samy.pl/defcon2015/2015-defcon.pdf)). The decisive step is the fourth. Many receivers do not clear their input after a wrong code; they shift in one new bit and test again, so a stream of bits in which every possible code appears as a substring, a De Bruijn sequence, tests them all in a fraction of the time. Kamkar released the code deliberately broken ("bricked") and gave the remedy on the same page: owners of fixed-code gates and garages should upgrade to a system that states it uses "rolling codes, hopping codes, Security+ or Intellicode", which he said are "not foolproof" but do stop this attack ([Samy Kamkar, OpenSesame](https://samy.pl/opensesame/)).

Fixed codes are not only a garage problem. The United States National Vulnerability Database (NVD) records CVE-2022-27254: "The remote keyless system on Honda Civic 2018 vehicles sends the same RF signal for each door-open request, which allows for a replay attack", a related issue to CVE-2019-20626 ([NVD, CVE-2022-27254, published 23 March 2022](https://services.nvd.nist.gov/rest/json/cves/2.0?cveId=CVE-2022-27254)). That is the [replay attack](/glossary/replay-attack) in its simplest form: record once, transmit later.

## Rolling codes: how a hopping code works

The answer the industry adopted is the rolling, or hopping, code, and its most documented implementation is KeeLoq, now sold by Microchip Technology. The underlying patent, United States patent 5,517,187, names Frederick J. Bruwer, Willem Smit and Gideon J. Kuhn as inventors and Nanoteq Pty Ltd of South Africa as the original assignee, with a priority date of 29 May 1990 and publication on 14 May 1996; Microchip Technology is listed among the current assignees ([Google Patents, US5517187A, read 16 November 2026](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5517187A/en)).

Microchip's datasheet for the HCS301 encoder, a typical KeeLoq transmitter chip, is the clearest primary description of how a hopping code is built ([Microchip Technology, HCS301 KeeLoq Code Hopping Encoder, DS21143C, 2011](https://ww1.microchip.com/downloads/en/DeviceDoc/21143c.pdf)). Each button press produces a 66-bit code word:

| Field | Size | What it holds | Encrypted? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hopping code | 32 bits | the 16-bit synchronization counter, discrimination bits and the button status, encrypted with the device's 64-bit key | Yes |
| Serial number | 28 bits | a number unique to the transmitter | No |
| Button code | 4 bits | which of the four buttons was pressed | No |
| Status | 2 bits | battery-low and repeat information | No |

The datasheet calls the 34 unencrypted bits the fixed code and says the device combines "a 32-bit hopping code, generated by a nonlinear encryption algorithm, with a 28-bit serial number and 6 information bits to create a 66-bit code word" (same source). Before a remote works, the receiver must "learn" it: it derives the transmitter's key, typically from the serial number and a 64-bit manufacturer's code stored in every receiver of that brand, decrypts the hopping code, and stores the serial number, the counter and the key (same source). Each press increments the counter, so each transmission is different. Microchip's own description of the result is confident: the code word length "eliminates the threat of code scanning and the code hopping mechanism makes each transmission unique, thus rendering code capture and resend schemes useless" (same source).

The weak point is the synchronization window, which exists because people press buttons when they are out of range. The HCS301 datasheet describes three zones counted forward from the last counter value the receiver accepted (same source):

| Zone | Counter values ahead of the last accepted one | What the receiver does |
|---|---|---|
| Single Operation window | up to 16 | acts on one press and stores the new counter, which moves the whole window forward |
| Double Operation (resynchronization) window | from 16 up to 32K (32,768) | waits for a second, sequential transmission before acting, then resynchronizes |
| Blocked window | everything else, including every counter already used | ignores the transmission |

Microchip adds that the blocked window "excludes previously used, perhaps code-grabbed transmissions from accessing the system", and also that this is "only a typical implementation" which, being firmware, "can be altered to fit the needs of a particular system" (same source). The same design problem appears in one-time passwords for logins, where a validator must accept a small window of future codes without accepting old ones; this site's articles on [how TOTP and HOTP work](/learn/totp-and-hotp) and on [validating one-time passwords](/learn/validating-totp-codes) describe the window from the server's side, and the [TOTP and HOTP tool](/tools/totp-hotp) computes such codes. A counter-based remote is, in effect, an HOTP token with no screen and a radio. Every attack below exploits either the cipher, the key the receiver holds, or the window.

## Four ways rolling codes fell

### 1. Cryptanalysis of the cipher

KeeLoq was proprietary, which delayed but did not prevent its analysis; this site's article on [security through obscurity](/learn/security-through-obscurity) explains [Kerckhoffs's principle](/glossary/kerckhoffs-principle) and the long list of proprietary ciphers that failed once examined. In February 2007 Andrey Bogdanov described KeeLoq as a [block cipher](/glossary/block-cipher) with a 64-bit key and 32-bit blocks, based on a nonlinear feedback shift register (NLFSR), and published a key-recovery attack of about 2 to the power 52 steps, "the first paper to describe and cryptanalyze the KeeLoq block cipher" ([Andrey Bogdanov, Cryptanalysis of the KeeLoq block cipher, IACR ePrint 2007/055, received 20 February 2007](https://eprint.iacr.org/2007/055)).

At Eurocrypt 2008, held from 13 to 17 April, Sebastiaan Indesteege, Nathan Keller, Orr Dunkelman, Eli Biham and Bart Preneel presented a practical attack: 2 to the power 16 known plaintexts and 2 to the power 44.5 KeeLoq encryptions, with data collection taking 65 minutes and the computation 7.8 days on 64 processor cores; they estimated that "Using only 10 000 euro, an attacker can purchase a cluster of 50 dual core computers that will find the secret key in about two days" ([University of Haifa research portal, A practical attack on KeeLoq, EUROCRYPT 2008, pp. 1–18](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/a-practical-attack-on-keeloq-2/)). The worse finding concerned the system rather than the cipher: "one of the two suggested key derivation schemes for KeeLoq allows to recover the master secret from a single key" (same source). The extended journal version, published in the Journal of Cryptology in January 2012 with more authors from KU Leuven, says that in some scenarios an adversary may reveal "the master secret used in an entire class of devices from attacking a single device" ([University of Haifa research portal, A practical attack on KeeLoq, Journal of Cryptology 25(1), 2012](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/a-practical-attack-on-keeloq/)).

### 2. Power analysis of the hardware

The cipher's mathematics were not even the easiest door. At CRYPTO 2008, held from 17 to 21 August, Thomas Eisenbarth, Timo Kasper, Amir Moradi, Christof Paar, Mahmoud Salmasizadeh and Mohammad T. Manzuri Shalmani presented "the first successful differential power analysis attacks on numerous commercially available products employing KeeLoq code hopping" ([University of Lübeck research portal, On the Power of Power Analysis in the Real World: A Complete Break of the KeeLoq Code Hopping Scheme, CRYPTO 2008, pp. 203–220](https://research.uni-luebeck.de/en/publications/on-the-power-of-power-analysis-in-the-real-world-a-complete-break/)). Differential power analysis (DPA) reads a key from the tiny variations in a chip's power consumption while it computes. They reported that "a remote control can be cloned from only ten power traces", that the same techniques extract the manufacturer key from a receiver, and that once that key is known an attacker can recover a remote's key and replicate it "just by eavesdropping at most two messages", from a distance (same source). A Ruhr-Universität Bochum news list announced a seminar on the break by Moradi, Eisenbarth and Kasper on 20 May 2008 ([Ruhr-Universität Bochum, news list, 20 May 2008; read again 17 November 2026 in a browser](https://lists.ruhr-uni-bochum.de/pipermail/hgi-news-deutschland/2008-May/000479.html)). The manufacturer key is the lesson: when every receiver of a brand holds the same secret, extracting it once from one receiver turns every remote of that brand into a target.

### 3. Jam, record and replay: RollJam

The window itself was the next target. In August 2015, at [DEF CON](/glossary/defcon) 23, Samy Kamkar presented "Drive It Like You Hacked It: New Attacks and Tools to Wirelessly Steal Cars", promising "advanced 'code grabbers' using RF attacks on encrypted and rolling codes" built with low-cost tools "such as RTL-SDR, GNU Radio, HackRF, Arduino, and even a Mattel toy" ([DEF CON, DEF CON 23 speakers, 2015](https://defcon.org/html/defcon-23/dc-23-speakers.html)). WIRED described the device, RollJam, on 6 August 2015 as "The $32 radio device, smaller than a cell phone" ([Andy Greenberg, WIRED, This Hacker's Tiny Device Unlocks Cars And Opens Garages, 6 August 2015](https://www.wired.com/2015/08/hackers-tiny-device-unlocks-cars-opens-garages/)).

The principle, as WIRED and Kamkar's slides set it out, uses the window against itself. When the owner presses the button, the device jams the receiver while listening on a slightly offset frequency with a tight filter, so it captures a valid code the car never heard. The owner presses again; the device jams and captures the second code and transmits the first, which still lies inside the window, so the car unlocks and the owner notices nothing but a button that needed two presses. The device keeps the second code, which is newer than any the car has accepted and therefore still valid ([Andy Greenberg, WIRED, 6 August 2015](https://www.wired.com/2015/08/hackers-tiny-device-unlocks-cars-opens-garages/); [Samy Kamkar, Drive It Like You Hacked It, DEF CON 23 slides](https://samy.pl/defcon2015/2015-defcon.pdf)). Kamkar told WIRED he had tested it on Nissan, Cadillac, Ford, Toyota, Lotus, Volkswagen and Chrysler vehicles, Cobra and Viper alarms and Genie and Liftmaster garage openers, and placed the root of the problem in chips including Microchip's KeeLoq and Texas Instruments' Hisec ([WIRED, 6 August 2015](https://www.wired.com/2015/08/hackers-tiny-device-unlocks-cars-opens-garages/)). Cadillac replied that the method "is well-known to our cyber security experts" and that current models had "moved to a new system"; WIRED noted that the researcher Spencer Whyte had described a similar device the previous year (same source).

Kamkar's own slides end with a list of lessons, which is also a list of defences: "Encrypt/hash the button/action", use an [HMAC](/glossary/hmac) to prevent bit flipping, "Use time-based algorithm", or use "challenge/response via transceivers instead of one-way communication", noting that many car keys can both receive and transmit and yet still send a one-way unlock signal ([Samy Kamkar, DEF CON 23 slides](https://samy.pl/defcon2015/2015-defcon.pdf)). He told WIRED that Microchip's Dual KeeLoq used codes that expire after a short time and defeated RollJam ([WIRED, 6 August 2015](https://www.wired.com/2015/08/hackers-tiny-device-unlocks-cars-opens-garages/)).

### 4. Resynchronization: Rolling-PWN and RollBack

The double-operation window was built to forgive. In 2022 two research efforts showed that, in some receivers, it forgives too much. The NVD entry CVE-2021-46145 reads: "The keyfob subsystem in Honda Civic 2012 vehicles allows a replay attack for unlocking. This is related to a non-expiring rolling code and counter resynchronization." ([NVD, CVE-2021-46145, published 6 January 2022](https://services.nvd.nist.gov/rest/json/cves/2.0?cveId=CVE-2021-46145)). The researchers who named it Rolling-PWN, Kevin2600 and Wesley Li of Star-V Lab, wrote that sending commands "in a consecutive sequence" to the vehicles resynchronized the counter, after which "commands from the previous cycle of the counter worked again", and listed ten Honda models from 2012 to 2022 they said they had tested ([Rolling-PWN project page, 2022](https://rollingpwn.github.io/rolling-pwn/)). They quoted Honda as first saying its fobs used rolling-code technology "that would not allow the vulnerability as represented in the report", then acknowledging "it is possible to mimic Remote Keyless commands and gain access to certain vehicles", and finally describing the risk as low (same source); these are the researchers' quotations of Honda, not a statement on a Honda page.

In a paper submitted to arXiv on 14 September 2022, Levente Csikor, Hoon Wei Lim, Jun Wen Wong, Soundarya Ramesh, Rohini Poolat Parameswarath and Mun Choon Chan generalized the idea as RollBack: "replaying a few previously captured signals consecutively can trigger a rollback-like mechanism", after which already-used signals work again; unlike RollJam it needs no jamming and only one capture, and their "ongoing analysis (covering Asian vehicle manufacturers for the time being)" found "~70% of them are vulnerable" ([Csikor et al., RollBack: A New Time-Agnostic Replay Attack Against the Automotive Remote Keyless Entry Systems, arXiv 2210.11923, 14 September 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.11923)).

### A different door: relaying passive keyless entry

Passive keyless entry and start (PKES) systems, which unlock when the key is merely near the car, do not use one-way rolling codes at all, and they fall to a different attack. Aurélien Francillon, Boris Danev and Srdjan Capkun showed in 2010 and 2011 that an attacker can relay the conversation between car and key without understanding it: tested on 10 car models from 8 manufacturers, relaying in one direction only was enough while the key was up to 50 metres away, and the key could be excited from up to 8 metres; because the relay is "completely independent of the modulation, protocol, or presence of strong authentication and encryption", strong cryptography does not help ([Francillon, Danev and Capkun, Relay Attacks on Passive Keyless Entry and Start Systems in Modern Cars, IACR ePrint 2010/332, NDSS 2011](https://eprint.iacr.org/2010/332)).

## The Brazilian street version: the "chapolin"

In Brazil, the radio attack that reaches the police blotter is cruder than any of these and needs no code at all. On 10 November 2025 the Polícia Civil of Goiás (PCGO) arrested a man in Senador Canedo who, according to the police, used a device "popularmente conhecido como 'Chapolin', capaz de bloquear o sinal de travamento remoto de veículos" (popularly known as "Chapolin", able to block vehicles' remote locking signal): the victim parked, believed the car was locked, and the device kept the doors from locking so that he could take cash, phones and bank cards, which he then used for purchases ([Polícia Civil do Estado de Goiás, PCGO prende homem com tornozeleira que usava dispositivo eletrônico para furtar objetos de veículos, 10 November 2025](https://goias.gov.br/policiacivil/?p=170942)). The police seized the device (same source). SBT News reported on 30 January 2026, citing São Paulo police data, that such thefts in the city of São Paulo had grown almost 14% in the previous year, and on 19 February 2026 that the devices, nicknamed "Chapolin" and "Capetinha", were being sold in the Santa Ifigênia electronics district; the state's Secretaria da Segurança Pública (SSP) said that part of the robberies in the capital may be linked to them, and the report added that such blockers can stop vehicles locking, interfere with electronic gates and block trackers ([SBT News, Bloqueador de sinal facilita furtos em carros e acende alerta em SP, 30 January 2026](https://sbtnews.sbt.com.br/noticia/policia/bloqueador-de-sinal-facilita-furtos-em-carros-e-acende-alerta-em-sp-1); [SBT News, "Chapolin" e "Capetinha": reportagem exclusiva flagra venda ilegal de bloqueadores de sinal em São Paulo, 19 February 2026](https://sbtnews.sbt.com.br/noticia/policia/chapolin-e-capetinha-reportagem-exclusiva-flagra-venda-ilegal-de-bloqueadores-de-sinal-em-sao-paulo)).

The chapolin is half of RollJam without the recorder: it jams, and the owner's habit of walking away without checking does the rest. The manufacturing side has reached the courts too. On 15 September 2025 the Agência Nacional de Telecomunicações ([Anatel](/glossary/anatel)) reported that the civil police of Rio de Janeiro and São Paulo, with its technical support, had raided a workshop in Sorocaba that built jammers, anti-drone systems and Global Positioning System (GPS) blockers; Anatel noted that its fiscalization staff in Minas Gerais had earlier shut down a GPS jammer in Belo Horizonte that interfered with aircraft using Pampulha airport ([Agência Gov, via Anatel, Operação desmantela fábrica clandestina de bloqueadores de sinais em SP, 15 September 2025](https://agenciagov.ebc.com.br/noticias/202509/operacao-conjunta-desmantela-fabrica-clandestina-de-bloqueadores-de-sinais-em-sao-paulo)).

## What the spectrum rules say

The law treats receiving, transmitting and selling differently, and it differs by country. What follows is a summary of the rules as published on the dates shown, not legal advice; owning hacking gear legally, with Anatel homologation, imports and the criminal law in detail, is the subject of a later part of this series.

### United States

Unlicensed transmitters operate under Part 15 of the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) rules, on the condition "that no harmful interference is caused and that interference must be accepted" from authorized stations and other devices, and an operator must stop when the FCC says the device is causing harmful interference ([eCFR, 47 CFR 15.5, read 16 November 2026](https://www.ecfr.gov/api/renderer/v1/content/enhanced/current/title-47?part=15&section=15.5)). Remotes, door openers and alarms fall under section 15.231, "Periodic operation in the band 40.66-40.70 MHz and above 70 MHz": the transmitter must be restricted to "a control signal such as those used with alarm systems, door openers, remote switches", a manual transmitter must switch off within 5 seconds of release, and field strength is capped by a table measured at 3 metres ([eCFR, 47 CFR 15.231, read 16 November 2026](https://www.ecfr.gov/api/renderer/v1/content/enhanced/current/title-47?part=15&section=15.231)). The Communications Act says that "No person shall manufacture, import, sell, offer for sale, or ship devices ... or use devices, which fail to comply with regulations" made under section 302a ([47 U.S.C. 302a(b), Legal Information Institute, read 16 November 2026](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/302a)), and that "No person shall willfully or maliciously interfere with or cause interference to any radio communications" of authorized stations ([47 U.S.C. 333, Legal Information Institute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/333)). On jammers the FCC is categorical: "the operation, marketing, sale, importation, distribution, or shipment of RF jammers is a violation of federal law", with "no exceptions for personal, commercial, educational, or vehicular use", and unauthorized users "may face substantial civil penalties, seizure of illegal equipment, and criminal sanctions including imprisonment" ([FCC, Jammer Enforcement, updated September 2026](https://www.fcc.gov/general/jammer-enforcement)).

### Brazil

Brazil's equivalent of Part 15 is Anatel's regime for restricted-radiation equipment (equipamentos de radiação restrita), set by Resolution 680 of 27 June 2017 and detailed in the technical requirements of Act (Ato) 14,448 of 4 December 2017, as amended to October 2025 ([Anatel, Ato nº 14.448, de 4 de dezembro de 2017, updated 14 October 2025](https://informacoes.anatel.gov.br/legislacao/resolucoes/2017/1139-resolucao-680)). Its section 6.2 covers periodic-operation devices "operando nas faixas 40,66-40,70 MHz e acima de 70 MHz, cuja emissão está restrita à transmissão de um sinal de controle tais como aqueles usados com sistemas de alarme, dispositivos de abrir e fechar porta, chaves remotas" (operating in 40.66 to 40.70 MHz and above 70 MHz, whose emission is restricted to a control signal such as those of alarms, door openers and remote keys), with a field-strength table whose figures match the FCC's and a 5-second automatic shut-off (same source). Its section 4.1.5 allows restricted-radiation equipment in 433 to 435 MHz with a maximum radiated power of 10 mW equivalent isotropically radiated power (EIRP) (same source).

Selling and using a radio product in Brazil requires homologation: "A homologação é pré-requisito obrigatório para a utilização e a comercialização, no País, dos produtos abrangidos por este Regulamento" (homologation is a mandatory prerequisite for the use and sale in the country of the products this regulation covers), and any natural or legal person may apply "quando para uso próprio" (for their own use) ([Anatel, Resolução nº 715, de 23 de outubro de 2019, art. 20 and art. 55, updated 12 November 2026](https://informacoes.anatel.gov.br/legislacao/resolucoes/2019/1350-resolucao-715)). Transmitting without authorization can be a crime: article 183 of the General Telecommunications Law (Lei Geral de Telecomunicações, Lei 9.472 of 1997) punishes anyone who "Desenvolver clandestinamente atividades de telecomunicação" (clandestinely carries out telecommunications activities) with two to four years of detention and a fine, and its article 184 defines clandestine activity as one carried out without the required concession, permission or authorization of service or of the use of radio frequency, as quoted in the Pontifícia Universidade Católica de São Paulo's legal encyclopedia, whose entry classifies it as a crime of abstract danger ([Enciclopédia Jurídica da PUC-SP, O crime de telecomunicação clandestina, Tomo Direito Penal, edição 1](https://enciclopediajuridica.pucsp.br/verbetes/o-crime-de-telecomunicacao-clandestina/)).

Jammers have their own rule. Anatel's Resolution 760 of 6 February 2023 restricts radio signal blockers to certain public bodies, such as the armed forces, the Presidency and public security agencies, and Anatel states that "é vedada a utilização desses equipamentos por pessoa física ou jurídica" (their use by natural or legal persons is prohibited), with administrative sanctions in addition to civil and criminal penalties ([Agência Gov, via Anatel, 15 September 2025](https://agenciagov.ebc.com.br/noticias/202509/operacao-conjunta-desmantela-fabrica-clandestina-de-bloqueadores-de-sinais-em-sao-paulo)). Act 10,988 of 26 July 2024, published on 21 August 2024, set the technical and operational requirements for those authorized blockers, used in prisons, ports and airports and security areas ([TELETIME, Anatel aprova requisitos técnicos para bloqueadores de sinais, 21 August 2024](https://teletime.com.br/21/08/2024/anatel-aprova-requisitos-tecnicos-para-bloqueadores-de-sinais/)).

### Europe

In the countries of the European Conference of Postal and Telecommunications Administrations (CEPT), short range devices (SRD) follow European Radiocommunications Committee (ERC) Recommendation 70-03, last amended on 9 October 2026 ([CEPT ECO Documentation Database, ERC/REC 70-03, published 9 October 2026](https://docdb.cept.org/document/845)). Its October 2026 edition allows non-specific short range devices in 433.05 to 434.79 MHz at 10 mW effective radiated power (e.r.p.) with a duty cycle of at most 10%, and in 868.0 to 868.6 MHz at 25 mW e.r.p. with a duty cycle of at most 1% or listen before talk (LBT) with adaptive frequency agility (AFA) ([ERC Recommendation 70-03, Edition of October 2026, Annex 1](https://docdb.cept.org/download/5013)).

Receiving is a different matter in each country and depends on what is received; the later part on legal ownership treats it.

## What defenders and owners can do

The published research points to defences that work at each layer.

- **Retire fixed codes.** Kamkar's advice for gates and garages was to move to systems that state they use rolling or hopping codes, which stop key-space attacks even if they are "not foolproof" ([Samy Kamkar, OpenSesame](https://samy.pl/opensesame/)).
- **Make codes expire and authenticate the action.** His DEF CON 23 lessons call for encrypting or hashing the button and action, an HMAC against bit flipping, time-based codes, or two-way challenge and response ([Samy Kamkar, DEF CON 23 slides](https://samy.pl/defcon2015/2015-defcon.pdf)). Microchip lists among Ultimate KeeLoq's enhancements an "Industry-standard AES-128 block cipher" and a "Programmable 128-bit encryption key", and describes "a timer-driven message counter that continuously increments, independent of events" ([Microchip Technology, Ultimate KeeLoq Technology, read 17 November 2026 in a browser](https://www.microchip.com/en-us/products/wireless-connectivity/features/security/ultimate-keeloq-technology)); [AES](/glossary/aes), the Advanced Encryption Standard, replaces the proprietary KeeLoq cipher in that design.
- **Stop sharing one secret across a product line.** The KeeLoq cryptanalysis showed that one of the suggested key-derivation schemes leaks the master secret from a single key, and the power analysis extracted the manufacturer key from a receiver ([Indesteege et al., EUROCRYPT 2008](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/a-practical-attack-on-keeloq-2/); [Eisenbarth et al., CRYPTO 2008](https://research.uni-luebeck.de/en/publications/on-the-power-of-power-analysis-in-the-real-world-a-complete-break/)).
- **Tighten resynchronization.** The flaw behind Rolling-PWN is "a non-expiring rolling code and counter resynchronization" ([NVD, CVE-2021-46145](https://services.nvd.nist.gov/rest/json/cves/2.0?cveId=CVE-2021-46145)); the researchers recommend updating the body control module firmware over the air where possible, or by dealer recall ([Rolling-PWN project page](https://rollingpwn.github.io/rolling-pwn/)).
- **Against relays, measure distance, not signal strength.** Francillon, Danev and Capkun found that signal-strength proximity checks are "very weak", proposed RF distance bounding, which bounds distance by the round-trip time of a rapid challenge and response, and listed immediate owner measures: a shielding key case that acts as a Faraday cage, removing the key's battery and using the backup mode, and a button or setting that disables passive entry ([Francillon, Danev and Capkun, NDSS 2011, full paper](https://www.ndss-symposium.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/franc.pdf)).
- **Against jamming, check the result.** The PCGO case worked because the victim came to "acreditar que havia trancado o carro" (believe the car had been locked) ([PCGO, 10 November 2025](https://goias.gov.br/policiacivil/?p=170942)). Canaltech, summarizing the advice of police and security specialists on 19 March 2025, put the remedy in one line: "confira se as portas travaram após ter acionado o alarme" (check that the doors locked after setting the alarm) ([Canaltech, Chapolin: como funciona o controle que bandidos usam para roubar carros, 19 March 2025](https://canaltech.com.br/carros/chapolin-como-funciona-o-controle-que-bandidos-usam-para-roubar-carros/)). The same article reports that a Brazilian accessory maker sells a detector that, in its own words, sounds an alarm on detecting such signals, at a price given only on request; the article calls its effectiveness unknown (same source).

## What these tools cannot do

An RTL-SDR cannot transmit at all ([RTL-SDR Blog, About RTL-SDR](https://www.rtl-sdr.com/about-rtl-sdr/)). A HackRF One transmits at most about 10 milliwatts across most of its range and cannot transmit and receive at once ([HackRF documentation, HackRF One](https://hackrf.readthedocs.io/en/latest/hackrf_one.html)); RollJam itself, as WIRED described it, used a pair of cheap radios to jam and a third to listen ([WIRED, 6 August 2015](https://www.wired.com/2015/08/hackers-tiny-device-unlocks-cars-opens-garages/)). A plain replay of a rolling code fails once the receiver has accepted a later counter, which is the property Microchip designed for; every successful attack above needed something more: a broken cipher, a leaked manufacturer key, a jammer, a forgiving resynchronization rule, or a relay of a system that never used one-way codes. Kamkar's lessons and the relay researchers' distance bounding describe the opposite design: codes that expire, keys that differ per device, and a two-way exchange bounded in time, in which recording and replaying a burst of radio is not enough ([Samy Kamkar, DEF CON 23 slides](https://samy.pl/defcon2015/2015-defcon.pdf); [Francillon, Danev and Capkun, NDSS 2011](https://www.ndss-symposium.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/franc.pdf)).

## What to remember

- An SDR records the waveform itself as I/Q samples, so it can store and, with transmit hardware, replay a signal it does not understand; the security of a radio system lives entirely above the radio layer.
- The RTL-SDR began in 2012 when developers found raw I/Q data could be read from RTL2832U television dongles; it receives from about 500 kHz to 1.75 GHz and cannot transmit.
- The HackRF One (2013 Kickstarter, DARPA Cyber Fast Track funding) transmits and receives from 1 MHz to 6 GHz, half-duplex; the HackRF Pro, announced on 26 June 2025, extends that to 100 kHz and adds a hardware switch-off for transmission.
- Fixed-code remotes fall to arithmetic: Kamkar's OpenSesame tried every 8- to 12-bit code in about 8 seconds.
- KeeLoq rolling codes carry a 66-bit word with a 32-bit encrypted hopping code and a synchronization window of 16 and 32K codes; they have been broken by cryptanalysis (2007, 2008), power analysis (2008), jam-and-replay (RollJam, 2015) and resynchronization (Rolling-PWN and RollBack, 2022).
- In Brazil, the common attack is the "chapolin" jammer that stops cars locking; Anatel restricts jammers to listed public bodies, and the FCC prohibits them outright.

- The HackRF's documentation tells buyers that the device "has not been tested for compliance" and that they are responsible for using it legally. Should open research hardware carry a hardware switch-off for transmission by default, as the HackRF Pro now offers?
- Microchip wrote that its code word made "code capture and resend schemes useless". What would a manufacturer need to publish about its synchronization rules for buyers to judge such a claim?
- RollJam and the chapolin both depend on the owner not checking that the car locked. Is that a user's failure or a designer's?
- If a rolling-code flaw like Rolling-PWN can only be fixed by a dealer recall or an over-the-air update, who should pay for vehicles that receive neither?
- Anatel restricts jammers to public bodies, while the same shops sell legal remotes and illegal jammers side by side. Which control works better: on the device, on the sale, or on the use?
</ReaderQuestions>
