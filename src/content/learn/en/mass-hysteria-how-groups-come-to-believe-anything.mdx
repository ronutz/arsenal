In July 1518 the authorities of Strasbourg hired musicians and built stages so that people who could not stop dancing would dance the affliction out of their bodies ([Pennant-Rea, Public Domain Review, 10 July 2018](https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/the-dancing-plague-of-1518/)). In November 1998 a teacher in Tennessee noticed a "gasoline-like" smell, a school was evacuated, 80 students and 19 staff went to the hospital emergency department, and investigators from several agencies found no medical or environmental cause ([Jones et al., New England Journal of Medicine, 13 January 2000](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=10631279)). In March 1994 the press in São Paulo published reports about six people supposedly involved in the sexual abuse of pupils at the Escola Base; the police inquiry was closed "pela inocorrência de qualquer tipo penal", because no crime had occurred, and courts later ordered media outlets to pay damages ([Consultor Jurídico, 24 May 2006](https://www.conjur.com.br/2006-mai-24/sbt_condenado_pagar_900_mil_indenizacao//?print=1)). These are very different events. What they share is that many people came, together and quickly, to experience or believe something that the evidence did not support, and that the belief or the symptom travelled from person to person.

This article opens a series on how that happens. It sets out the words used for such episodes and the history each one carries, what the phenomena have in common and what separates them, the difference between a belief that someone steered and one that nobody steered, a dated timeline of landmark cases with one source for each, and the link to the series on [psychological operations](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign). After reading, you can tell the terms apart, place a case on the timeline with the kind of evidence behind it, and find the part that treats it in depth.

## The words and the history they carry

### Hysteria: a gendered word, and a contested origin story

The word comes from the Greek for the womb. A 2015 review of the classification of these disorders states that the term, "derived from the Greek word hystera (signifying the uterus), dates back to at least the time of Hippocrates, when it was thought that the uterus became physically displaced from its normal position in the pelvis, wandering throughout the body to create symptoms" ([North, Behavioral Sciences, 2015](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC4695775/fullTextXML)). A 2012 review of women and hysteria goes further: "Hippocrates (5th century BC) is the first to use the term hysteria" ([Tasca et al., Clinical Practice and Epidemiology in Mental Health, 2012](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC3480686/fullTextXML)).

<Claim status="disputed" by="by the historian Helen King">That the Hippocratic writers named a disease "hysteria"</Claim> is the part of the story historians contest. Helen King, examining the passage usually cited, found that it "does not use the term 'hysteria' at all", that the Hippocratic adjective *hysterikos* means "from the womb" and is used only in that sense, and that the idea that the corpus "not only described but also given its name" to hysteria traces back to the French edition of Émile Littré, whose section headings, written in the 1850s, "have no analogue in the Greek manuscripts" ([King, in Hysteria Beyond Freud, University of California Press, 1993](https://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/view?docId=ft0p3003d3&chunk.id=d0e94&toc.depth=1&toc.id=d0e94&brand=ucpress)). In her account, Littré "expected to find hysteria in the text, duly found it", and later readers accepted his categories (same).

What is not contested is that the label became a burden. In the nineteenth century the French physician Jean-Martin Charcot "concluded that only hysterics could be hypnotized" and classified "demonomania" as a form of hysteria ([North, 2015](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC4695775/fullTextXML)). When a psychiatric group at Washington University in St Louis drew up criteria for hysteria that were later published among the "Feighner criteria", it adopted the name "Briquet's syndrome" to replace "hysteria", which "had long since become heavily laden with pejorative connotations" (same). The third edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-III), in 1980, deleted the concept of hysterical neurosis ([Tasca et al., 2012](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC3480686/fullTextXML)) and listed conversion disorder "(or hysterical neurosis, conversion type)" among the somatoform disorders ([North, 2015](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC4695775/fullTextXML)).

The gendered assumptions are visible in the classic texts on group outbreaks. J. F. C. Hecker's history of the medieval epidemics, in its English edition of 1844, quotes Zimmermann's book *On Solitude*: "The imaginations of women are always more excitable than those of men, and they are therefore susceptible of every folly when they lead a life of strict seclusion" ([Hecker, The Epidemics of the Middle Ages, 1844](https://archive.org/details/epidemicsofmiddlin00heck)). Modern research measures what those authors assumed. In the Tennessee school, symptoms were "significantly associated with female sex, seeing another ill person, knowing that a classmate was ill, and reporting an unusual odor" ([Jones et al., 2000](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=10631279)). In a laboratory analogue in which students inhaled an inert substance described as a suspected toxin, watching a confederate display symptoms increased the specified symptoms among women but not among men, which the authors suggest may explain "the preponderance of women showing symptoms in outbreaks" ([Lorber, Mazzoni and Kirsch, Annals of Behavioral Medicine, February 2007](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=17291177)); a follow-up found that the effect of modelling "was independent of gender", while the mere presence of a person of the same gender increased expected symptoms ([Mazzoni et al., Health Psychology, March 2010](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=20230091)).

### Mass psychogenic illness: why researchers changed the name

Researchers now prefer terms that describe the mechanism without the old label. Robert Bartholomew and Simon Wessely define mass sociogenic illness as "the rapid spread of illness signs and symptoms affecting members of a cohesive group, originating from a nervous system disturbance involving excitation, loss or alteration of function, whereby physical complaints that are exhibited unconsciously have no corresponding organic aetiology" ([Bartholomew and Wessely, British Journal of Psychiatry, April 2002](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/protean-nature-of-mass-sociogenic-illness/2BDC2262E104B8A33F3DD49773DA0D8B)). The words "exhibited unconsciously" matter: the definition describes real symptoms, not pretence. The same paper calls the phenomenon "an under-appreciated social problem that is both underreported and often a significant financial burden" to emergency services and to the schools and workplaces that close for days or weeks (same). The investigators of the Tennessee episode used "mass psychogenic illness" and described "widespread subjective symptoms thought to be associated with environmental exposure to a toxic substance in the absence of objective evidence of an environmental cause" ([Jones et al., 2000](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=10631279)); a 1997 review by an epidemiologist of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) was still titled "Epidemic hysteria" ([Boss, Epidemiologic Reviews, 1997, record](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=9494785)).

Wessely proposed in 1987 that the literature describes two syndromes. "Mass anxiety hysteria" consists of acute anxiety, "occurring mainly in schoolchildren", without prior tension, spreading "by visual contact", treated by separating the participants, with a good prognosis; "mass motor hysteria" consists of abnormal movements, occurs at any age after a period of tension, spreads gradually and may last long, so treatment "should be directed towards the underlying stressors" ([Wessely, Psychological Medicine, February 1987](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=3575566)). Bartholomew and Wessely trace how the content changes with the era: before 1900 reports were dominated by motor symptoms in settings of long tension; twentieth-century reports feature anxiety triggered by "an innocuous odour or food poisoning rumours"; from the early 1980s chemical and biological terrorism themes grew, with "a sudden shift since the 11 September 2001 terrorist attacks" ([Bartholomew and Wessely, 2002](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/protean-nature-of-mass-sociogenic-illness/2BDC2262E104B8A33F3DD49773DA0D8B)).

The naming dispute continues with each new form. In 2021 a group of German clinicians described functional "Tourette-like" behaviours in teenagers that they traced to a "virtual" index case, a YouTube creator, and proposed the term "mass social media-induced illness" ([Müller-Vahl et al., Brain, April 2022](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=34424292)). A group of researchers and a community member answered in the same journal under the title "A call for caution: 'stop that' sentiments threaten tic research, healthcare and advocacy" ([Conelea et al., Brain, May 2022](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=35213691)). The words used for these outbreaks shape how the people affected are treated, which is why the parts on [mass psychogenic illness](/learn/mass-psychogenic-illness-when-symptoms-spread) and on [social media](/learn/social-media-and-the-contagion-of-symptoms-and-beliefs) return to them.

### Moral panic, folk devils and moral entrepreneurs

A moral panic is a different thing: no one need fall ill, and what spreads is alarm about a group. The concept was popularized by Stanley Cohen's study of youthful hooliganism in post-war Britain, and refers to episodes in which folk devils, "moral outlaws" and "'visible reminders of what we should not be'", in Cohen's phrase, "are blamed for societal malaise" ([Walsh, International Journal of Cultural Studies, March 2020](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7201200/fullTextXML)). Cohen's book examined the public reaction to clashes between two youth subcultures, "mods" and "rockers", on English beaches over public holiday weekends in 1964 ([Mannion and Small, International Journal of Health Policy and Management, 2019](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6885862/fullTextXML)). The concept has its critics: in the work of McRobbie and Thornton, folk devils defended by "niche and micro-media" are "no longer powerless victims" and can "fight back", which makes the success of moral crusades "much less certain" ([Walsh, 2020](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7201200/fullTextXML)).

The people who start and drive such crusades have their own name. In 1963 the sociologist Howard Becker described the moral entrepreneur in two forms: the crusading reformer or "rule maker", who "is interested in the content of rules. The existing rules do not satisfy him [sic] because there is some evil which profoundly disturbs him", and the "rule enforcer", whose job is to apply the rules; in his words, "the final outcome of a moral crusade is a police force" ([Greenhalgh et al., Journal of Evaluation in Clinical Practice, 2019](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6952841/fullTextXML)). James Walsh argues that on social media "distinctions between the media, the public and moral entrepreneurs are blurring" as lay users take part in exposing and sanctioning deviance ([Walsh, 2020](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7201200/fullTextXML)). The parts on [witch hunts and moral panics](/learn/witch-hunts-and-moral-panics) and on [the Satanic panic and the Escola Base case](/learn/satanic-panic-false-memories-and-the-escola-base-case) apply these ideas and their critiques.

### Mania, crowds and the panic that rarely happens

The oldest popular word is mania, and its best-known book is Charles Mackay's *Memoirs of Extraordinary Popular Delusions*, first published in London in 1841 ([Mackay, 1841 edition, scan at the Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/memoirsextraord12mackgoog)) and later reissued with the subtitle *and the Madness of Crowds*. Its preface to the 1852 edition puts the thesis in one sentence: "Men, it has been well said, think in herds; it will be seen that they go mad in herds, while they only recover their senses slowly, and one by one" ([Mackay, 1852 edition, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/24518)). Some of Mackay's best-known stories have not survived archival research. The historian Anne Goldgar, who studied the Dutch tulip trade of the 1630s in the archives, told *Smithsonian* magazine: <Claim status="reported" by="by Anne Goldgar, as quoted by Smithsonian magazine">"I couldn't find anybody that went bankrupt"</Claim>, and the stories of ruin, the magazine reports, "come from propaganda pamphlets published by Dutch Calvinists" ([Smithsonian magazine, There Never Was a Real Tulip Fever, 18 September 2017](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/there-never-was-real-tulip-fever-180964915/)). The part on [manias and bubbles](/learn/manias-and-bubbles-from-tulips-to-meme-stocks) sets Mackay's chapters against the records.

Gustave Le Bon's *The Crowd*, published in French as *Psychologie des foules*, gave the idea of contagion its classic form: "In a crowd every sentiment and act is contagious, and contagious to such a degree that an individual readily sacrifices his personal interest to the collective interest" ([Le Bon, The Crowd, English translation, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/445)). Research on disasters has weakened the picture of crowds that collapse into blind panic. E. L. Quarantelli, summarizing the sociology of panic, reports studies "showing for example that panic flight is very rare, and has few of the characteristics typically attributed to the behavior, even in situations where it might be expected" ([Quarantelli, The Sociology of Panic, University of Delaware Disaster Research Center, 2001](https://udspace.udel.edu/bitstream/handle/19716/308/PP%20283.pdf?sequence=1)). A survey of police officers, safety professionals, stewards and members of the public found that respondents still endorsed the "mass panic" and "civil disorder" myths while rejecting the myth of helplessness and accepting that emergency crowds show resilience ([Drury, Novelli and Stott, Journal of Applied Social Psychology, 2013](https://www.csap.cam.ac.uk/media/uploads/files/1/10.1111-jasp.12176-disaster-myths-as-published-early-view.pdf)). The part on [rumour, panic and lynching](/learn/rumour-panic-and-lynching) takes up what the research on panic found.

### Collective delusion and social contagion

Two broader terms cover belief without illness. The sociologists Robert Bartholomew and Erich Goode write that "collective delusions are typified as the spontaneous, rapid spread of false or exaggerated beliefs within a population at large, temporarily affecting a particular region, culture, or country", whereas episodes of mass hysteria "typically affect small, tightly knit groups in enclosed settings such as schools, factories, convents and orphanages" ([Bartholomew and Goode, Skeptical Inquirer, May/June 2000](https://cdn.centerforinquiry.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2000/05/22164854/p20.pdf)).

Social contagion is the widest of all. Marsden summarizes it as the thesis "that sociocultural phenomena can spread through, and leap between, populations more like outbreaks of measles or chicken pox than through a process of rational choice", traces its use for social phenomena to James Mark Baldwin, Gabriel Tarde and Le Bon in the late nineteenth century, and notes "a complete absence of agreement among researchers as to the particular mechanism that underlies social contagion" ([Marsden, Journal of Memetics, 1998](http://cfpm.org/jom-emit/1998/vol2/marsden_p.html)). The next part, [Conformity, cascades and contagion](/learn/conformity-cascades-and-contagion-the-mechanisms), examines the mechanisms proposed since, the experiments that test them and the replications that weakened some of them.

## What the phenomena share, and what separates them

The terms overlap but do not coincide. The table sets them side by side; each row cites the source used for it above.

| Term | What spreads | Typical setting | What the literature says ends it | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mass psychogenic (sociogenic) illness | symptoms without an organic cause | school, factory, convent | separating those affected (anxiety form); addressing the stressors (motor form) | [Wessely, 1987](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=3575566) |
| Moral panic | alarm and hostility towards a group cast as folk devils | a society and its media | contested; folk devils can now answer back | [Walsh, 2020](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7201200/fullTextXML) |
| Mania or bubble | buying, prices, expectations | markets | in Mackay's account, recovery "slowly, and one by one" | [Mackay, 1852](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/24518) |
| Collective delusion | false or exaggerated beliefs | a region, culture or country | temporary by definition | [Bartholomew and Goode, 2000](https://cdn.centerforinquiry.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2000/05/22164854/p20.pdf) |
| Social contagion | affect, attitudes, beliefs, behaviour | any network | no agreed mechanism | [Marsden, 1998](http://cfpm.org/jom-emit/1998/vol2/marsden_p.html) |

Several features recur across the cases on the timeline below. The first is ambiguity. In his experiments of the 1930s Muzafer Sherif chose a deliberately "unstable, unstructured situation", a point of light in a dark room, and found that people in groups converged on a common norm which each then carried into later judgements made alone ([Sherif, The Psychology of Social Norms, 1936](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.264611)). Bartholomew and Wessely make the same point about outbreaks: "The perceived threatening agent must be seen as credible to the affected group", so a fainting student on an ordinary day would not be expected to set one off ([Bartholomew and Wessely, 2002](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/protean-nature-of-mass-sociogenic-illness/2BDC2262E104B8A33F3DD49773DA0D8B)).

The second is visibility. Wessely's anxiety form spreads "by visual contact" ([Wessely, 1987](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=3575566)), and in Tennessee "seeing another ill person" was associated with reporting symptoms ([Jones et al., 2000](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=10631279)).

The third is amplification by media. In an experiment, people who watched a television report on the supposed health effects of Wi-Fi and then received a sham exposure were more worried about electromagnetic fields, and the anxious among them reported more symptoms; the authors conclude that such reports "can increase the likelihood of experiencing symptoms following sham exposure and developing an apparent sensitivity to it" ([Witthöft and Rubin, Journal of Psychosomatic Research, March 2013](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=23438710)). Colombia's national health institute, investigating the 2014 outbreak in El Carmen de Bolívar, suggested that media coverage may have amplified it ([Baltar-Moreno et al., Gerencia y Políticas de Salud, October 2022](https://revistas.javeriana.edu.co/index.php/gerepolsal/article/download/34614/28546)).

What separates the phenomena is just as important. Some involve the body (symptoms) and others only belief. Some have a target, a group blamed or accused, as in moral panics and witch hunts; others have none. And in some cases something real lay underneath, or may have, which is why the contested episodes in this series (Havana syndrome, the poisonings of schoolgirls in Afghanistan and Iran, El Carmen de Bolívar) are set out with each side's evidence rather than filed under hysteria.

## Led by someone, or no one at the wheel

### When someone steered

Le Bon described the crowd as something a leader works on. To imbue a crowd with ideas, he wrote, leaders use three means, "affirmation, repetition, and contagion", and of repetition he said: "The thing affirmed comes by repetition to fix itself in the mind in such a way that it is accepted in the end as a demonstrated truth" ([Le Bon, 1895](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/445)). Experimental psychology later measured a version of the second means as the illusory truth effect, set out in [The human attack surface: why repetition works](/learn/the-human-attack-surface-why-repetition-works). Sherif, too, ended his chapter on norm formation with a hungry crowd in which "a leader or a small party may standardize certain norms or slogans as guides to an outlook upon the situation and as guides to action" ([Sherif, 1936](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.264611)).

Several cases on the timeline had identifiable people who steered, accused or amplified. At Loudun, in France, the priest Urbain Grandier was said to have bewitched a convent into fits and was burned alive in 1634 ([Bartholomew and Wessely, 2002](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/protean-nature-of-mass-sociogenic-illness/2BDC2262E104B8A33F3DD49773DA0D8B)). At Salem, in Massachusetts, the special witchcraft court ended only when Governor William Phips ended it in October 1692, after which "accusations soon abated and eventually stopped" ([Salem Witch Trials Documentary Archive, University of Virginia, overview](https://salem.lib.virginia.edu/overview.html)). In the Escola Base case, the São Paulo court that condemned a television network in 2006 found that its reports had attributed personal responsibility "de forma prematura e subjetiva", prematurely and subjectively, before the official investigation ended ([Consultor Jurídico, 2006](https://www.conjur.com.br/2006-mai-24/sbt_condenado_pagar_900_mil_indenizacao//?print=1)). In India, the Supreme Court described lynchings by "frenzied mobs across the country instigated by intolerance and misinformed by circulation of fake news and false stories" ([Supreme Court of India, Tehseen S. Poonawalla v. Union of India, 17 July 2018](https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2016/27263/27263_2016_Judgement_17-Jul-2018.pdf)).

### When the panic was in the reporting

Sometimes the amplifier was the account of the event. Jefferson Pooley and Michael Socolow argue that <Claim status="disputed" by="by Pooley and Socolow, against Hadley Cantril's 1940 estimate">the panic attributed to Orson Welles's radio play *The War of the Worlds* in 1938 was largely a newspaper creation</Claim>: on the night of the broadcast the C. E. Hooper ratings service telephoned 5,000 households and "Only 2 percent answered a radio 'play' or 'the Orson Welles program,' or something similar indicating CBS"; they write that newspapers, which had lost advertising to radio, "sensationalized the panic to prove to advertisers, and regulators, that radio management was irresponsible", and that Hadley Cantril's estimate of about one million frightened listeners rested on a survey whose audience figure was, in Cantril's own words as they quote him, "over 100 per cent higher than any other known measure of this audience" ([Pooley and Socolow, Slate, October 2013](https://slate.com/culture/2013/10/orson-welles-war-of-the-worlds-panic-myth-the-infamous-radio-broadcast-did-not-cause-a-nationwide-hysteria.html)). The part on [rumour, panic and lynching](/learn/rumour-panic-and-lynching) sets out both sides.

### When nobody steered

In other cases the investigators found no one at the wheel. In Tennessee they found no evidence of carbon monoxide, volatile organic compounds, pesticides or other toxic compounds in blood, urine or the environment, and concluded that "alleviation of the anxiety surrounding an episode of mass psychogenic illness requires prompt recognition and a detailed investigation" ([Jones et al., 2000](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=10631279)). At Le Roy, in New York State, the state health department's interim report found "no infectious or environmental etiologies" for tics in 12 female students, eight of whom had been diagnosed with conversion disorder ([New York State Department of Health, interim report, 31 January 2012](https://www.health.ny.gov/press/releases/2012/leroy/docs/investigation_summary.pdf)). In the German cluster of 2021, the "virtual" index case was a video creator, not someone trying to cause illness ([Müller-Vahl et al., 2022](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=34424292)).

Between these lie cases where a rumour started the episode and an authority ended it. In Singapore in 1967, a rumour that eating pork from inoculated pigs caused the genitals to shrink set off an outbreak of koro, and the number of reported cases fell sharply after the Singapore Medical Association and the health ministry made announcements; over 460 cases had been officially reported ([Mothership, November 2016, drawing on the Singapore Medical Journal of 1969](https://mothership.sg/2016/11/sporean-men-panicked-in-1967-over-strange-case-of-shrinking-genitals-known-as-koro)). The government's first announcement that swine fever and its vaccine were harmless had, the same account says, "only caused the people to panic even more" (same). How reassurance helps and when it backfires is the subject of [the series' last part](/learn/what-ends-an-outbreak-and-what-helps).

### The meowing nuns: how a story without a source travels

One of the most repeated anecdotes in this literature shows how a thinly sourced story becomes a fact by repetition. Bartholomew and Wessely write that at one French convent "the nuns meowed together every day at a certain time for several hours together", citing Hecker's book of 1844 ([Bartholomew and Wessely, 2002](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/protean-nature-of-mass-sociogenic-illness/2BDC2262E104B8A33F3DD49773DA0D8B)). Hecker, in turn, quotes Zimmermann's book *On Solitude*, whose source is this: "I have read in a good medical work that a nun, in a very large convent in France, began to mew like a cat; shortly afterwards other nuns also mewed" ([Hecker, 1844](https://archive.org/details/epidemicsofmiddlin00heck)). The convent, the date and the "good medical work" are not named. The chain runs from an unnamed book to Zimmermann, from Zimmermann to Hecker and from Hecker to a psychiatric journal in 2002; the part on [dancing plagues and possessed convents](/learn/dancing-plagues-and-possessed-convents) follows it further.

## The link to the series on psychological operations

The series on [psychological operations](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign) studies deliberate attempts to change belief through perception: the repeated small lie, the scam sent to millions, the covert campaign. This series studies what groups do with beliefs and symptoms, whether or not anyone is steering. The two meet in at least three places.

The first is repetition. Le Bon's "affirmation, repetition, and contagion" ([Le Bon, 1895](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/445)) anticipates the experimental findings set out in [The human attack surface](/learn/the-human-attack-surface-why-repetition-works), where a statement heard before is rated truer than a new one.

The second is persistence. The reasons a corrected falsehood keeps its influence, examined in [Why misbeliefs persist](/learn/why-misbeliefs-persist), explain why outbreaks and panics often outlast the investigation that dispels them, as in El Carmen de Bolívar, where the families rejected the official findings and vaccination coverage fell ([Baltar-Moreno et al., 2022](https://revistas.javeriana.edu.co/index.php/gerepolsal/article/download/34614/28546)).

The third is reach. A rumour once passed along by word of mouth now travels by forwarded messages and pages, as in Guarujá in 2014 ([Canaltech, 2020](https://canaltech.com.br/redes-sociais/facebook-nao-pagara-indenizacao-a-familia-de-vitima-fatal-de-fake-news-160851/)) and in the Indian lynchings that the Supreme Court addressed in 2018 ([Supreme Court of India, 2018](https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2016/27263/27263_2016_Judgement_17-Jul-2018.pdf)); [From the lodge to the network](/learn/from-the-lodge-to-the-network-how-reach-changed) examines what that change in reach did and did not alter.

Some of the founding research on social influence was paid for by a military research office, and its authors wrote with propaganda in mind. Solomon Asch opened his 1955 article on group pressure with the observation that "The same epoch that has witnessed the unprecedented technical extension of communication has also brought into existence the deliberate manipulation of opinion and the 'engineering of consent.'" ([Asch, Opinions and Social Pressure, Scientific American, November 1955](https://archive.org/details/solomon-asch_confirmaty)), and his monograph acknowledges "financial assistance from the Office of Naval Research" ([Asch, Psychological Monographs, 1956](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0093718)). The Stanford prison experiment's report also states that "This research was funded by an ONR grant" ([Haney, Banks and Zimbardo, Interpersonal Dynamics in a Simulated Prison, report deposited with the National Criminal Justice Reference Service](https://www.ncjrs.gov/pdffiles1/Photocopy/10301NCJRS.pdf)). Where a case in this series had a manipulator, the part says so and links the psyops part that covers the technique; where nobody steered it, the part says that too. The penultimate part, [How manipulators harness collective panic](/learn/how-manipulators-harness-collective-panic), is the bridge between the two series.

## What to remember

- "Hysteria" comes from the Greek word for the womb; the story that Hippocrates named the disease is contested, and the label was dropped from American psychiatry's diagnostic manual in 1980.
- Researchers prefer "mass psychogenic" or "sociogenic illness": real symptoms, exhibited unconsciously, without an organic cause, spreading in a cohesive group; Wessely distinguished an anxiety form and a motor form.
- A moral panic spreads alarm about a group (folk devils), often driven by moral entrepreneurs; a collective delusion spreads a false belief across a population; a mania spreads buying and expectations; social contagion is the umbrella term, with no agreed mechanism.
- Ambiguity, visibility of others and media amplification recur across cases; what separates them is whether symptoms arise, whether a group is targeted and whether something real lay underneath.
- Some episodes were steered (Loudun, Salem, Escola Base, the Indian lynchings), some were inflated in the telling (the 1938 radio panic, disputed), and some had no one at the wheel (Tennessee, Le Roy).
- Famous stories need their sources checked: the tulip ruin, the meowing nuns and the date of the Strasbourg plague all rest on chains of retelling.
