Most of the episodes in this series were steered by no one. A smell in a Tennessee school, a twitch that spread through a New York high school and a rumour of brigands in revolutionary France had no author that investigators could find, as the parts on [mass psychogenic illness](/learn/mass-psychogenic-illness-when-symptoms-spread) and on [rumour, panic and lynching](/learn/rumour-panic-and-lynching) set out. This part is about the others: the episodes in which the record shows someone who started a panic, steered one that had started on its own, or made money from it. It is the bridge back to the series on [psychological operations](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign), which studies deliberate attempts to change belief through perception, and it links the part of that series that treats each technique. After reading, you can tell the three roles apart, say what the research on fear does and does not show about persuasion, follow five documented cases from the seventeenth century to 2025, and judge how strong the evidence of intent is in each.

## Starting, steering, profiting

The cases below fall into three roles, which the sources keep apart and which are often confused in retelling. Each row cites the source for its example; the sections that follow give the evidence in full.

| Role | What the record shows | Example in this part | Source for the example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Starting | someone fabricates the threat | a text message about a toxic leak at a Louisiana chemical plant that had no leak | [Chen, The New York Times Magazine, 2 June 2015](https://www.nytimes.com/2015/06/07/magazine/the-agency.html) |
| Steering | someone gives an existing fear a culprit and a direction | the Soviet claim that the AIDS virus came from a United States laboratory | [State Department, 1987](https://sspconline.org/sites/default/files/2026-01/soviet-influence-activities-1987_0.pdf) |

The roles overlap. The East Anglian witch-finders steered a fear that existed before them and were paid for it; the Soviet campaign steered a real epidemic's fear and also invented details; a pump-and-dump promoter starts the excitement and profits from it in the same act.

## What fear does to persuasion, and what the research cannot say

### The theories

In 1953 Irving Janis and Seymour Feshbach published a study titled "Effect of fear-arousing communications" ([Janis and Feshbach, Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology 48(1), January 1953, PubMed record](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=13022199)); only its bibliographic record could be read for this part. In 1975 Ronald Rogers proposed protection motivation theory, which "postulates the three crucial components of a fear appeal to be (a) the magnitude of noxiousness of a depicted event; (b) the probability of that event's occurrence; and (c) the efficacy of a protective response" ([Rogers, Journal of Psychology 91(1), September 1975, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=28136248)). The idea that the response to fear depends on whether people believe they can act runs through everything that followed.

### The meta-analyses

Kim Witte and Mike Allen pooled the literature in 2000 and found that "strong fear appeals produce high levels of perceived severity and susceptibility, and are more persuasive than low or weak fear appeals", but also that fear appeals motivate both "adaptive danger control actions such as message acceptance and maladaptive fear control actions such as defensive avoidance or reactance"; in their reading, "strong fear appeals and high-efficacy messages produce the greatest behavior change, whereas strong fear appeals with low-efficacy messages produce the greatest levels of defensive responses" ([Witte and Allen, Health Education & Behavior 27(5), October 2000, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=11009129)).

The largest synthesis, by Melanie Tannenbaum, Dolores Albarracín and colleagues, included 127 articles yielding 248 independent samples and 27,372 participants, and found "a positive effect of fear appeals on attitudes, intentions, and behaviors, with the average effect on a composite index being random-effects d = 0.29"; effectiveness rose when messages "included efficacy statements, depicted high susceptibility and severity, recommended one-time only (vs. repeated) behaviors", and the authors concluded that "there are no identified circumstances under which they backfire and lead to undesirable outcomes" ([Tannenbaum et al., Psychological Bulletin 141(6), November 2015, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=26501228)). Only the abstract could be read for this part.

A group at Maastricht University reached a narrower conclusion by applying stricter criteria. Gjalt-Jorn Peters, Robert Ruiter and Gerjo Kok kept only studies with "full factorial orthogonal manipulations of threat and efficacy, and measurement of behaviour as an outcome"; six qualified, and they "clearly showed a significant interaction between threat and efficacy, such that threat only had an effect under high efficacy (d = 0.31), and efficacy only had an effect under high threat (d = 0.71)" ([Peters, Ruiter and Kok, Health Psychology Review 7(Suppl 1), May 2013, full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC3678850/fullTextXML)). A 2014 review by the same group concluded that coping information "is more important in promoting protective action than presenting threatening health information" and that "Alternative behavior change methods than fear appeals should be considered" ([Ruiter, Kessels, Peters and Kok, International Journal of Psychology 49(2), April 2014, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=24811876)).

### The dispute

In 2018 Kok and colleagues went further: the assumption that confronting people with the negative effects of their behaviour changes it is "simple and intuitive, but only true under specific, rare circumstances", and the evidence "does not support positive effects of fear appeals" ([Kok et al., Health Psychology Review 12(2), June 2018, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=29233060)). The journal printed eight commentaries. Two titles state the opposing case: Benjamin X. White and Dolores Albarracín's "Fear appeals do change behaviour in experimental laboratory studies" and Noel Brewer, Marissa Hall and Seth Noar's "Pictorial cigarette pack warnings increase quitting" ([White and Albarracín, 2018, PubMed record](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=29504457); [Brewer, Hall and Noar, 2018, PubMed record](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=29478371)); the Maastricht authors answered with "Towards consensus on fear appeals" ([Peters et al., 2018, PubMed record](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=29558243)). Only the records of these three papers could be read, so their arguments are not summarized here. <Claim status="disputed" by="by the 2015 meta-analysis against the Maastricht re-analyses">Whether fear appeals work in general</Claim> is contested; both sides agree that fear without a sense that something can be done is the weakest combination.

### From fear to belief

A separate line of work asks whether emotion makes false claims easier to believe. Cameron Martel, Gordon Pennycook and David Rand found that "heightened emotionality at the outset of the study was predictive of greater belief in fake (but not real) news posts" (Study 1, N = 409), and, across four experiments with 3,884 participants, that "inducing reliance on emotion resulted in greater belief in fake (but not real) news stories compared to a control or to inducing reliance on reason" ([Martel, Pennycook and Rand, Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications 5, October 2020, full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7539247/fullTextXML)). The emotions in that study were general, not panic in a crowd.

These studies measured health campaigns, laboratory ratings and online headlines, not panics steered by a manipulator; none of them tested a witch-finder or a troll factory. They show that fear changes behaviour most when it comes with a way out, and that emotional states make false headlines more credible; how far that explains the cases below is for the reader to judge. The persuasion principles that fraudsters use are in [the human attack surface](/learn/the-human-attack-surface-why-repetition-works), the fear and urgency of pretext calls in [social engineering](/learn/social-engineering-pretexting-vishing-and-baiting), and the "fearmongering" technique that prebunking videos teach people to spot in [prebunking and inoculation](/learn/prebunking-and-inoculation).

## Witch-finders: panic as a trade, East Anglia, 1645 to 1647

### Two men and a war

The Malleus Maleficarum and the European trials are in [the part on witch hunts and moral panics](/learn/witch-hunts-and-moral-panics); this section follows two men who made a business of an existing fear. In 1911 the historian Wallace Notestein described Matthew Hopkins as "a lawyer of but little note" at Ipswich who moved to Manningtree, in Essex, and who "is said to have taken the title of witchfinder-general" ([Notestein, A History of Witchcraft in England from 1558 to 1718, 1911, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/31511.txt.utf-8)). Hopkins dated the start to March 1644, when, he wrote, he overheard a witch at a meeting near his house speak to her imps ([Hopkins, The Discovery of Witches, 1647, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/14015.txt.utf-8)); Notestein, following the calendar of the time, writes the date as 1644/5 ([Notestein, 1911](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/31511.txt.utf-8)). His partner was John Stearne.

The modern scale comes from Malcolm Gaskill's study of the hunt. The publisher's description of his book, on the University of East Anglia's research portal, says that by the autumn of 1647 at least 250 people, mostly women, had been interrogated and brought before the courts, that "More than a hundred were hanged", and that the campaign "was never legally sanctioned" ([University of East Anglia research portal, Gaskill, Witchfinders, Harvard University Press, October 2007 edition](https://research-portal.uea.ac.uk/en/publications/witchfinders-a-seventeenth-century-english-tragedy/)). By the spring of 1645, the same description notes, England had endured two years of civil war (same). Notestein explained the hunt by the war more than by belief: assizes were almost suspended in 1645 and 1646, local justices were left to themselves, "England was in a state of judicial anarchy", and the arrival of Hopkins and Stearne "gave them their chance, and there was no one to say stop" ([Notestein, 1911](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/31511.txt.utf-8)).

### The method

The witch-finders' method was observation under deprivation. John Gaule, the vicar of Great Staughton in Huntingdonshire, described it in 1646 from a conversation with one of the witch-finders, from a suspect's confession and from village talk: "Having taken the suspected Witch, she is placed in the middle of a room upon a stool, or Table, cross legged, or in some other uneasy posture, to which if she submits not, she is then bound with cords, there is she watched & kept without meat or sleep for the space of 24. hours" ([Gaule, Select Cases of Conscience Touching Witches and Witchcrafts, 1646, EarlyPrint transcription of EEBO-TCP A85867](https://distantreader.org/stacks/earlyprint/txt/A85867.txt); [scan of a 1646 copy, Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/JohnGauleSermonOnWitches)). Stearne quoted the objection in his own book, "you watched them, and kept them from meat, drink, or rest, and so made them say what you would", and answered that "For the watching, it is not to use violence, or extremity to force them to confess", though he did "not deny but at first, some were kept two, three, or four days" ([Stearne, A Confirmation and Discovery of Witchcraft, 1648, EarlyPrint transcription of EEBO-TCP A61373](https://distantreader.org/stacks/earlyprint/txt/A61373.txt)).

### The money

The fear was organized as a service that towns bought. On 15 August 1645 the corporation of Great Yarmouth voted to send for Hopkins, who should have his fee and allowance for his pains "as he hath in other places"; at Aldeburgh he was paid six pounds and the accounts record "Paid ... eleven shillings for hanging seven witches"; Stowmarket paid him twenty-three pounds, from which Notestein estimated that the two men cleared "from three hundred to a thousand pounds, not an untidy sum in that day, when a day's work brought six pence" ([Notestein, 1911](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/31511.txt.utf-8)).

The demand was also cultivated. Invited to search Great Staughton, and told that its minister was against him, Hopkins wrote to one of the parishioners that unless the town offered "good welcome and entertainment, as other where I have been", he would "betake me to such places where I do and may persist without control, but with thanks and recompense"; Gaule printed the letter in his book ([Gaule, 1646](https://distantreader.org/stacks/earlyprint/txt/A85867.txt)). Among the objections put to Hopkins through the judges of assize in Norfolk was the charge that "All that the witch-finder doth is to fleece the country of their money", that he "possesseth many men that they have so many wizzards and so many witches in their towne, and so hartens them on to entertaine him"; Hopkins replied that he never went anywhere unless "they rode, writ, or sent often for him", and that "he demands but 20.s. a town" to maintain "his Companie with 3. horses" ([Hopkins, 1647](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/14015.txt.utf-8)). Notestein called the reply untrue in view of the Stowmarket accounts ([Notestein, 1911](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/31511.txt.utf-8)). Stearne, for his part, complained a year later that "in many places I never received penny as yet" ([Stearne, 1648](https://distantreader.org/stacks/earlyprint/txt/A61373.txt)).

### The critics, at the time

The hunt had contemporary critics, and their words survive. A parliamentary newspaper, the Moderate Intelligencer, wrote during the Bury St Edmunds trials of 1645 that "Life is precious and there is need of great inquisition before it is taken away" ([Notestein, 1911, quoting the newspaper](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/31511.txt.utf-8)). Gaule asked his readers to consider "Whether Witch-seeking or witch-finding be an Art, Vocation, Profession, Office, Occupation, or Trade of Life, allowable in a Christian Church, or State?", warned that "the Country People talk already, and that more frequently, more affectedly, of the infallible and wonderful power of the Witchfinders; then they do of God, or Christ, or the gospel preached", and described a "sect or sort" for whom "every old woman with a wrinkled face, a furred brow, a hairy lip, a gobber tooth, a squint eye, a squeaking voice, or a scolding tongue" was "not only suspected, but pronounced for a witch" ([Gaule, 1646](https://distantreader.org/stacks/earlyprint/txt/A85867.txt)).

### How it ended

Notestein traced the end to opposition. At home in Essex, the friends of a reprieved woman indicted Stearne and some forty others for conspiracy; at Bury the opposition appealed to Parliament, which sent a commission of oyer and terminer; in Norfolk the queries were put to Hopkins through the judges; and the two men, he wrote, "went into retirement and set to work to exonerate themselves" ([Notestein, 1911](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/31511.txt.utf-8)). Hopkins died in 1647; Stearne wrote that he "died peaceably at Manningtree, after a long sickness of a Consumption" ([Stearne, 1648](https://distantreader.org/stacks/earlyprint/txt/A61373.txt)). Notestein's verdict on the cause: "two aggressive men had taken advantage of a time of popular excitement and alarm" ([Notestein, 1911](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/31511.txt.utf-8)).

### Newcastle, 1649 and 1650: paid by the head

A deposition printed in 1655 describes a sharper incentive. John Wheeler swore that the magistrates of Newcastle upon Tyne had sent two sergeants into Scotland to hire "a Scotchman, who pretended knowledge to find out Witches by pricking them with pins", who was "to have twenty shillings a piece for all he could condemn as Witches"; the magistrates then "sent their Bellman through the Town, ringing his Bell, and crying, All people that would bring in any complaint against any woman for a Witch, they should be sent for and tried by the person appointed"; thirty women were brought into the town hall and pricked in public, and a lieutenant colonel exposed the method by asking for one woman to be tested again ([Gardiner, England's Grievance Discovered, 1655, EarlyPrint transcription of EEBO-TCP A42371](https://distantreader.org/stacks/earlyprint/txt/A42371.txt)). The same chapter reports that the man was later condemned in Scotland and "upon the Gallows he confessed he had been the death of above two hundred and twenty women in England and Scotland, for the gain of twenty shillings a piece" (same). <Claim status="reported" by="by depositions printed in a pamphlet against the Newcastle magistrates">The account of the pricker's fee, the town crier and the gallows confession</Claim> comes from a book written to attack the town's rulers over the coal trade, so it is evidence of what witnesses swore, not a neutral record.

A service sold to towns, with a fee per visit or per conviction, is the oldest form of what the psyops series calls [influence for hire](/learn/influence-for-hire-the-firms-that-sell-influence-operations); the deprivation that produced confessions is the coercion the [witch hunts part](/learn/witch-hunts-and-moral-panics) follows into the courts.

## A state feeds the fear of an epidemic: the AIDS campaign, 1983 to 1987

### What is already on the record

The campaign that spread the claim that the virus behind acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS) was made in a United States military laboratory is told in the psyops series: its origin in an Indian newspaper in 1983, the Soviet denial and later retreat, the Russian acknowledgement of 1992 and the dispute over the East German share and the code name, INFEKTION or Denver, are in [state campaigns on the record](/learn/state-influence-campaigns-on-the-record) and [a short history of propaganda](/learn/a-short-history-of-propaganda-and-psychological-warfare), and its use as a model "kill chain" is in [anatomy of an influence operation](/learn/anatomy-of-an-influence-operation-abc-disarm-and-kill-chains). This section reads the same documents for something else: how the campaign used the fear of a new and fatal disease.

### The fear it found

The timing was chosen. The United States Department of State, the target government, wrote in its 1987 report that the original article "appeared just as AIDS was beginning to attract the attention of the international scientific and medical communities" and seemed intended to link the United States "with what was then an unexplored public health problem"; the article that launched the full campaign in the Soviet weekly Literaturnaya Gazeta on 30 October 1985 was titled "Panic in the West or What is Hidden Behind the Sensation About AIDS" ([US Department of State, Soviet Influence Activities: A Report on Active Measures and Propaganda, 1986-87, August 1987](https://sspconline.org/sites/default/files/2026-01/soviet-influence-activities-1987_0.pdf)). The historians Douglas Selvage and Christopher Nehring, in a study published by the agency that holds the East German secret police files, explain the gap between 1983 and 1985 by the change in public attention: by 1985 "man bereits von einer Epidemie sprach", people were already speaking of an epidemic ([Selvage and Nehring, Die AIDS-Verschwörung, Stasi Records Agency, BF informiert 33, 2014, second edition 2020](https://www.bundesarchiv.de/assets/bundesarchiv/de/Publikationen/BFi_33_Selvage_Nehring_AIDS_Auflage-02.pdf)).

### The theses the KGB sent out

The telegram that the Soviet Committee for State Security (KGB) sent to Bulgarian State Security on 7 September 1985, held in the Bulgarian archives and translated by Selvage, asked its allies to promote theses in the press of Western and developing countries. Besides the claim of a laboratory origin, already quoted in the psyops series, it told them to say that the American authorities had blamed Haitian immigrants and called AIDS "not dangerous for the 'normal' part of the population", but that "during the following two years, it was revealed that AIDS can be transmitted through saliva and tears, and it turned out that even children are susceptible to this disease" ([KGB, Information Nr. 2955, 7 September 1985, translation, Digital Archive, University of Maryland and the National Cold War Center](https://digitalarchive.umd.edu/node/102957)). Selvage and Nehring summarize the intent: "Darüber hinaus sollte Angst vor der Verbreitung des Virus durch die amerikanische Regierung und vor Amerikanern im Allgemeinen geweckt werden", fear was also to be aroused of the virus being spread by the American government and of Americans in general, including through American donor blood ([Selvage and Nehring, 2014](https://www.bundesarchiv.de/assets/bundesarchiv/de/Publikationen/BFi_33_Selvage_Nehring_AIDS_Auflage-02.pdf)).

### Fear aimed at the bases

By 1987 the campaign pointed the fear at a target. The State Department recorded that the Soviet press warned that United States military personnel "pose a public health danger to local populations", that the news agency TASS reported on 12 February 1987 "panic" in Japan and that local businesses were "closing their doors" to United States military personnel, that a Radio Moscow broadcast in English warned in April 1986 of infected personnel from ships visiting Mombasa, Kenya, and that in May 1987 a bogus leaflet offering free AIDS tests at the United States Army hospital in Berlin was followed by a forged press release on the stationery of the Berlin Senate, purporting to say that the hospital "was unable to treat civilian AIDS cases because it was overflowing with its own AIDS victims" ([State Department, 1987](https://sspconline.org/sites/default/files/2026-01/soviet-influence-activities-1987_0.pdf)). A KGB telegram of 1987, also from the Bulgarian archives, stated the aim in its own words: "efforts are being made to intensify anti-base sentiments in countries where American forces are deployed by using slogans suggesting that U.S. soldiers are the most dangerous carriers of the virus" ([KGB, Information Nr. 2742, 1987, translation, Digital Archive](https://digitalarchive.umd.edu/node/102959)). According to Selvage and Nehring, citing a Bulgarian record of talks with the KGB, the Soviet service counted as a success a large demonstration in Seoul in 1988 for the closure of American bases because of the alleged danger of AIDS ([Selvage and Nehring, 2014](https://www.bundesarchiv.de/assets/bundesarchiv/de/Publikationen/BFi_33_Selvage_Nehring_AIDS_Auflage-02.pdf)).

The same 1987 telegram shows the campaign using a grievance that was real. The laboratory version, it reported, "gained considerable resonance in African countries, which have persistently rejected as racist the theory propagated by the Americans that the AIDS virus originated in African green monkeys", and "By demonstrating the defeat of the 'African version' [of AIDS' origins], we can whip up anti-American sentiments throughout the states of the continent" ([KGB, Information Nr. 2742, 1987](https://digitalarchive.umd.edu/node/102959)). The plan for joint measures that the East German foreign intelligence service's active-measures division drew up with the Bulgarians on 3 September 1986, under the heading Operation "DENVER", had promised a study proving "that AIDS originated in the USA, not in Africa" ([HVA/X, Plan for Common and Coordinated Active Measures of the Intelligence Organs of the MOI of the PR Bulgaria and the MfS of the GDR for 1987 and 1988, 3 September 1986, translation, Digital Archive](https://digitalarchive.umd.edu/node/102958)).

### Fear redirected at home, and laundered abroad

The State Department suggested a domestic motive as well: faced with its own cases, Moscow "may be attempting to redirect domestic fears about a public health problem that has so far been primarily associated with the U.S.", encouraging "xenophobic sentiment by underscoring the possible harmful results of contact with foreigners", and later shifting blame onto African students in the Soviet Union ([State Department, 1987](https://sspconline.org/sites/default/files/2026-01/soviet-influence-activities-1987_0.pdf)). It also traced how the story was laundered: an interview with the biologist Jakob Segal in the London Sunday Express in October 1986 was picked up by a Spanish magazine, then by an Argentine daily, which retracted it, and the news agency TASS then cited the Argentine paper as the source, after which Nigerian and Sudanese outlets reprinted the TASS item; in the report's words, the story could "travel quickly across national boundaries, lose the link to its original source, and continue to mislead new audiences even though its arguments have been effectively rebutted elsewhere" (same). The Soviet Union's own leading AIDS specialist, Viktor Zhdanov, rejected the theory in public; asked by a reporter in June 1986 whether the United States had made the virus, he replied, according to the agencies the report cites, "That is a ridiculous question. Perhaps it was the Martians" (same).

### What ended it, and what it left

According to Selvage and Nehring, in the second half of 1987 the KGB had to end its AIDS disinformation, or at least confine it to measures against American bases, because the United States had made its end a precondition for the summits between Mikhail Gorbachev and Ronald Reagan, while the East German service carried on ([Selvage and Nehring, 2014](https://www.bundesarchiv.de/assets/bundesarchiv/de/Publikationen/BFi_33_Selvage_Nehring_AIDS_Auflage-02.pdf)). The same authors cite the economist Nicoli Nattrass's finding that conspiracy beliefs about AIDS in the United States and South Africa are associated with unsafe sex, with not taking antiretroviral treatment and with not being tested, and the South African judge and activist Edwin Cameron's words to the Berlin newspaper taz in 2010: "Thesen wie die von Segal haben viele Jahre Prävention in Afrika verhindert", theses like Segal's prevented prevention in Africa for many years (same). <Claim status="established" by="by Bulgarian and East German archival documents">The KGB's direction of the campaign and its deliberate use of the fear of AIDS</Claim> rest on the services' own papers; how much of the later belief the campaign caused, as against older distrust, is <Claim status="disputed">not measured</Claim> in these sources, and the East German share remains contested, as the [history part](/learn/a-short-history-of-propaganda-and-psychological-warfare) sets out. Why such stories outlast their rebuttal is the subject of [why misbeliefs persist](/learn/why-misbeliefs-persist); the vaccine and epidemic scares that followed are in the part on [health scares](/learn/health-scares-from-the-revolta-da-vacina-to-the-autism-myth).

## Staged disasters: the Internet Research Agency's hoaxes of 2014

### A chemical leak that never happened

Around 8:30 a.m. on 11 September 2014, the emergency director of St. Mary Parish, Louisiana, Duval Arthur, received a call from a resident about a text message: "Toxic fume hazard warning in this area until 1:30 PM," it read. "Take Shelter. Check Local Media and columbiachemical.com." ([Chen, The Agency, The New York Times Magazine, 2 June 2015](https://www.nytimes.com/2015/06/07/magazine/the-agency.html)). On Twitter, the reporter Adrian Chen found, hundreds of accounts were documenting an explosion at the Columbian Chemicals plant in Centerville, with a doctored screenshot of CNN's home page, a video in which ISIS appeared to claim the attack, "fully functional clones of the websites of Louisiana TV stations and newspapers" and even a Wikipedia page; text messages went to real residents at the same time (same). An analysis by the threat intelligence company Recorded Future counted "nearly five thousand reported events related to #ColumbianChemicals, from nearly a thousand distinct authors" on 11 and 12 September, directed at 267 different profiles, in a pattern that, it argued, "looks overly produced" ([Recorded Future, #ColumbianChemicals Hoax: Trolling the Gulf Coast for Deceptive Patterns, 2015](https://www.recordedfuture.com/blog/columbianchemicals-hoax-analysis)).

The panic did not take hold locally. Arthur called his staff and the plant, which reported no problem, and "Roughly two hours after the first text message was sent, the company put out a news release, explaining that reports of an explosion were false" ([Chen, 2015](https://www.nytimes.com/2015/06/07/magazine/the-agency.html)). Interviewed in 2018 by the local paper, Arthur said he needed "only about an hour" to establish that there was no release, that "It appeared to be a hoax to us right off the bat", and that the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) did not share who owned the number used ([St. Mary Now, 17 February 2018](https://www.stmarynow.com/node/199476)).

### The same accounts, other fears

Chen found the same accounts in other fabricated emergencies. On 13 December 2014, "two months after a handful of Ebola cases in the United States touched off a minor media panic", many of them posted about an outbreak of Ebola in Atlanta under #EbolaInAtlanta, which briefly trended there; the same day another group spread a rumour that an unarmed black woman had been shot dead by police, a hoax that "seemed designed to piggyback on real public anxiety" after the protests in Ferguson, Missouri ([Chen, 2015](https://www.nytimes.com/2015/06/07/magazine/the-agency.html)). The United States Senate Select Committee on Intelligence (SSCI), drawing on a classification by researchers at Clemson University, recorded a category of the Internet Research Agency's (IRA) Twitter accounts called "Fearmonger": "the IRA's 122 Fearmonger Twitter accounts were specifically dedicated to furthering the spread of a hoax concerning poisoned turkeys during the Thanksgiving holiday of 2014. The Fearmonger Twitter accounts tweeted over 10,000 times" ([SSCI, Report on Russian Active Measures Campaigns and Interference in the 2016 U.S. Election, Volume 2, October 2019](https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/sites/default/files/documents/Report_Volume2.pdf)).

### Who did it, and their answer

The 2015 attribution was journalistic. Chen traced the tweets about #ColumbianChemicals to a posting tool, Masss Post, linked to the domain Add1.ru, which online records showed had been registered by a Mikhail Burchik, the name listed as executive director of the IRA in documents leaked by a hacker group; when Chen called him, Burchik "denied working at the Internet Research Agency", telling him: "I have heard of it, but I don't work in this organization" ([Chen, 2015](https://www.nytimes.com/2015/06/07/magazine/the-agency.html)). The grand jury indictment of February 2018 alleges that <Claim status="alleged" by="by the grand jury indictment">"By approximately March 2014, BURCHIK was the executive director, the ORGANIZATION's second-highest ranking position"</Claim> and that two of the defendants travelled through nine states, Louisiana among them, in June 2014 "to gather intelligence" ([United States v. Internet Research Agency LLC et al., indictment, 16 February 2018](https://www.justice.gov/opa/press-release/file/1035562/download)); it does not mention the St. Mary hoax, as the local paper noted, in an article that gives the reporter's name wrongly as "Amy Chen" ([St. Mary Now, 2018](https://www.stmarynow.com/node/199476)). The indictment, the replies of Yevgeny Prigozhin and the Russian government, the dismissal of the case against his companies and his later admission that he had created the agency are set out in [state campaigns on the record](/learn/state-influence-campaigns-on-the-record).

### Did it work?

No study read for this part measured what the hoaxes changed. The Senate committee warned against overrating them in its own report: "even the fear of active measures being unleashed on a society risks societal damage, whether the foreign capability exists or not" ([SSCI, Volume 2, 2019](https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/sites/default/files/documents/Report_Volume2.pdf)). The same report describes a case in which the fear did take hold: in Germany in 2016 a report that falsely accused Arab migrants of abducting and raping a 13-year-old Russian-German girl, who later admitted that she had made the story up, was promoted by Russian state-controlled media; protests followed, and the Russian foreign minister, Sergei Lavrov, said that Germany was "covering up reality in a politically correct manner for the sake of domestic politics", as the committee quotes him from The New York Times Magazine (same). The machinery of fake accounts and bought reach is in [bots, sockpuppets and astroturfing](/learn/bots-sockpuppets-and-astroturfing); what reach does and does not change is in [from the lodge to the network](/learn/from-the-lodge-to-the-network-how-reach-changed).

## Excitement and fear for sale: market manipulation

### The scheme, in the regulator's words

The United States Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) defines the scheme in its investor alerts: in a "pump-and-dump" scheme, "promoters 'pump' up, or increase, the stock price of a company by spreading positive, but often false, rumors"; investors buy, and the promoters "quickly 'dump' their own shares before the hype ends", after which "the stock price drops and the remaining investors lose most of their money" ([SEC Office of Investor Education and Advocacy, Look Out for Coronavirus-Related Investment Scams, 4 February 2020, updated 10 February 2021](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-alerts/look-out-coronavirus-related-investment-scams-investor-alert)). The mechanism is the reverse of a bank run: excitement instead of fear, but the same crowd following the crowd that [the part on manias and bubbles](/learn/manias-and-bubbles-from-tulips-to-meme-stocks) describes.

### London, 21 February 1814: a false messenger

An early case on the record is a fabricated war bulletin. The indictment tried in the Court of King's Bench in June 1814 charged that a man in uniform calling himself Colonel Du Bourg landed near Dover with the news that Napoleon Bonaparte had been killed and the allies were in Paris; prosecuting counsel told the jury that the price of omnium, a government stock, opened "extremely flat at 27-1/2", rose to 30 as the news reached the Stock Exchange, and, after a carriage carrying men dressed as French officers drove through the City scattering notes, rose to "30, 31, 32 and 32-1/2", before "it was discovered that this had been a gross and wicked deception; and the Funds returned to very nearly their former level" ([The Trial of Charles Random de Berenger, Sir Thomas Cochrane and others, for a conspiracy, 1814, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/21027.txt.utf-8)). "There is nothing so contagious as popular feeling, especially on a subject of great public interest," counsel said (same). The jury convicted; the court fined Lord Cochrane and Richard Butt one thousand pounds each, sentenced six defendants to twelve months' imprisonment and ordered three of them, including Cochrane, into the pillory opposite the Royal Exchange (same). <Claim status="denied" by="by Lord Cochrane at sentencing">Cochrane told the court he would "bow with submission, feeling conscious of my innocence of the charge upon which I have been found guilty"</Claim> (same).

### 2020: shares sold on a pandemic's fear

The COVID-19 pandemic produced the largest recent wave of promotions on fear. On 4 February 2020 the SEC warned of "Internet promotions, including on social media, claiming that the products or services of publicly-traded companies can prevent, detect, or cure coronavirus, and that the stock of these companies will dramatically increase in value as a result", noted that "These claims may be made as part of fraudulent 'pump-and-dump' schemes", and reminded investors that "investment scam artists often exploit the latest crisis to line their own pockets" ([SEC investor alert, 2020](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-alerts/look-out-coronavirus-related-investment-scams-investor-alert)). The alert, as updated on 10 February 2021, lists 39 companies whose trading the SEC suspended in connection with coronavirus claims between 7 February 2020 and 9 February 2021; a suspension, it explains, can last up to ten days when the SEC believes information about a company "is inaccurate or unreliable", which is not a finding of fraud (same).

Some of these cases reached a court. According to the Department of Justice, Mark Schena, president of Arrayit Corporation, one of the suspended companies, was convicted by a federal jury on 6 September 2022 and sentenced in October 2023 to eight years in prison and 24 million dollars in restitution, in a case in which he had falsely announced that the company "had a test for COVID-19" and told investors it "could be valued at $4.5 billion" ([US Department of Justice, Silicon Valley Executive Sentenced for Defrauding Investors and Participating in COVID-19 and Allergy Testing Scheme, October 2023](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/silicon-valley-executive-sentenced-defrauding-investors-and-participating-covid-19-and)).

### Influencers and a reinstated indictment

The crowd now gathers on social media, and so do the promoters. In December 2022 the SEC charged eight men with a securities fraud of about 100 million dollars, alleging that seven of them presented themselves on Twitter and Discord as successful traders, urged followers to buy shares they held and sold without disclosing it ([SEC, Litigation Release No. 25591, 14 December 2022](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/litreleases/lr-25591)). In the parallel criminal case, <Claim status="alleged" by="by the superseding indictment">the defendants would buy a security and then "'pump' the price of that security by posting false and misleading information about the security on [social media]"</Claim>, and the indictment puts their profit at 114 million dollars; a district court dismissed it, and on 2 October 2025 the Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit reversed ([United States v. Constantinescu et al., No. 24-20143, US Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, 2 October 2025](https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/opinions/pub/24/24-20143-CV0.pdf)). One defendant pleaded guilty on 27 March 2023; the Department of Justice records that "All of the defendants have pleaded not guilty and denied these charges", that an indictment "is merely an allegation", and that the trial of the remaining seven is set for 3 May 2027 ([US Department of Justice, Criminal Division, United States v. Edward Constantinescu et al., case page, updated 5 August 2026](https://www.justice.gov/criminal/criminal-vns/case/united-states-v-constantinescu-et-al)). The GameStop episode of 2021, in which a crowd coordinated openly and the SEC staff found no manipulation, is in [the part on manias and bubbles](/learn/manias-and-bubbles-from-tulips-to-meme-stocks).

### Brazil: the regulator and the controlling shareholder's tweets

Brazil's securities regulator, the Comissão de Valores Mobiliários (CVM), has sanctioned manipulation by social media. On 11 June 2019 it unanimously barred the controlling shareholder of RJCP Equity S.A. from acting as a director of a listed company for 180 months for manipulating its share price between 2011 and 2013, barred its investor relations director for 60 months and fined two board members and another participant ([CVM, Processo Administrativo Sancionador RJ2013/8880, judgment of 11 June 2019](https://conteudo.cvm.gov.br/export/sites/cvm/sancionadores/sancionador/anexos/2018/RJ20138880__COMPLETO.pdf)). As a law firm's summary of the decision describes the pattern, the controlling shareholder announced deals that mostly failed or happened differently, the accused posted optimistic messages on Twitter and personal blogs around each announcement, and they then traded, "mas sempre com um saldo de vendas líquidas", always ending as net sellers; the defence argued that the comments expressed what the accused believed ([Capital Aberto, Decisão da CVM reforça preocupação com uso indevido de redes sociais, 28 June 2019](https://capitalaberto.com.br/regulacao/2019/06/decisao-da-cvm-reforca-preocupacao-com-uso-indevido-de-redes-sociais)). The rapporteur, Henrique Machado, answered that unofficial information in chats or blogs "pode ser reproduzida por diversas outras pessoas e, com isso, afetar a cotação de um valor mobiliário, pois, ao ser reproduzida repetidamente as pessoas acabam confiando e negociando com base nela", can be reproduced by many others and move a price, because, repeated, people come to trust it and trade on it ([CVM, RJ2013/8880](https://conteudo.cvm.gov.br/export/sites/cvm/sancionadores/sancionador/anexos/2018/RJ20138880__COMPLETO.pdf)). The decision could be appealed to the Conselho de Recursos do Sistema Financeiro Nacional (CRSFN), the appeals council (same); the outcome of any appeal was not found.

The same vote recalls a decision of 27 May 2019 in which, in Machado's words, the CVM recognized "manipulação de preços por meio de publicações enviesadas em redes sociais", price manipulation through biased posts on social media, by the businessman Eike Batista about the oil company OGX ([CVM, RJ2013/8880](https://conteudo.cvm.gov.br/export/sites/cvm/sancionadores/sancionador/anexos/2018/RJ20138880__COMPLETO.pdf)). Here the posts worked against fear rather than for excitement: as InfoMoney reported, the CVM found that he had manipulated the market through Twitter posts "incentivando os investidores a manterem as ações que tinham", encouraging investors to hold their shares, while he sold, and imposed two fines totalling about 536 million reais for insider trading and manipulation ([InfoMoney, 27 May 2019](https://www.infomoney.com.br/mercados/eike-batista-e-condenado-a-pagar-r-536-mi-por-uso-de-informacao-privilegiada-na-ogx/)). <Claim status="denied" by="by Eike Batista's defence">He denied selling to profit from inside information, and his lawyer attributed the decision to "clamor público, porque o Eike é uma figura que desperta muitas emoções", public clamour over a man who stirs strong emotions</Claim>, and said the defence would appeal (same). The report calls the infractions "crimes"; the CVM's sanctions are administrative.

The economics of mass fraud, in which a small response from a large audience pays, are in [scams at scale](/learn/scams-at-scale-why-a-tiny-success-rate-pays).

## Fear turned into law

A panic can also be harnessed to obtain powers. The best-known Brazilian case, the forged Plano Cohen of 1937, is examined as a conspiracy narrative in [conspiracy theories at scale](/learn/conspiracy-theories-at-scale); what concerns this part is how quickly the fright became law. According to the legal scholar Orides Mezzaroba, the plan, presented as a communist plot seized by the army, was published widely on 30 September 1937, including on the official radio programme Hora do Brasil, and "apenas 24 horas depois de receber uma simples nota do Ministério da Justiça, a Câmara dos Deputados, por ampla maioria (2/3), concedeu o 'estado de guerra'", only 24 hours after receiving a simple note from the Ministry of Justice, the Chamber granted the state of war by a two-thirds majority ([Mezzaroba, Plano Cohen: a consolidação do anticomunismo no Brasil, Revista Seqüência 24, September 1992](https://periodicos.ufsc.br/index.php/sequencia/article/download/16143/14696/49698)). The text was written to frighten: as a later reportage in the magazine O Cruzeiro quotes it from the newspapers of the day, it said that in the neighbourhoods "as massas deverão ser conduzidas aos saques e depredações, nada poupando para aumentar cada vez mais a sua excitação", the masses were to be led to looting and destruction, sparing nothing to raise their excitement ever higher ([Glauco Carneiro, Três documentos forjados, O Cruzeiro, reproduced on the Arquivo Nacional's Que República é essa? site](https://querepublicaeessa.an.gov.br/images/surpresa/br_rjanrio_d7_0_dco_txt_0001_0004_d0001de0001.pdf)). The decrees of October 1937 rest on legislative decree 117 and decree 2,005 of 2 October 1937, and one of them created a commission of the justice minister, a general and an admiral to oversee the exceptional measures "em todo o território nacional" ([Decreto n. 2.020, de 7 de outubro de 1937, Senado Federal](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/395109/publicacao/15781294)). When Getúlio Vargas imposed a new constitution on 10 November 1937, its preamble cited the "estado de apreensão criado no País pela infiltração comunista, que se torna dia a dia mais extensa e mais profunda, exigindo remédios, de caráter radical e permanente", the state of apprehension created by communist infiltration, demanding radical and permanent remedies ([Constituição dos Estados Unidos do Brasil, 10 de novembro de 1937, Senado Federal](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/579491/publicacao/15772673)). Who wrote the document, and who knew it was false, is set out in that part; Mezzaroba adds that General Eurico Gaspar Dutra later said it "não passou de um boletim anônimo, de origem duvidosa, e que por isso mesmo nenhuma influência teve no desenrolar dos acontecimentos", was no more than an anonymous bulletin of doubtful origin with no influence on events ([Mezzaroba, 1992](https://periodicos.ufsc.br/index.php/sequencia/article/download/16143/14696/49698)).

Recent cases of the same kind are told elsewhere in this series: the confessions that Afghanistan's intelligence service presented in 2012 after schoolgirl illnesses, which a human rights researcher called the government's own psychological operations, and the court cases brought in Iran in 2023 against people accused of spreading rumours about the school poisonings, are in [mass psychogenic illness](/learn/mass-psychogenic-illness-when-symptoms-spread).

## What to remember

- Three roles recur: starting a panic, steering an existing fear and profiting from it; most cases combine two.
- Fear appeals change behaviour most when people believe they can act; whether they work in general is disputed between the 2015 meta-analysis and its critics, and none of that research tested a manipulated panic.
- Hopkins and Stearne turned a wartime witch scare into a paid service: towns sent for them and paid by the visit, a Newcastle pricker was paid by the head, and critics at the time named it a trade.
- The Soviet AIDS campaign fed on the fear of a new disease: its telegrams told allies to say the virus spread through tears and saliva, aimed the fear at United States bases and used a real grievance about racism; it was curtailed in 1987 under diplomatic pressure.
- The IRA's 2014 hoaxes staged a chemical disaster and an Ebola outbreak; a local official dispelled the first within hours, and no study read here measured their effect.
- Pump-and-dump schemes sell excitement, and in 2020 they sold pandemic fear; the SEC suspended 39 stocks over coronavirus claims, and the CVM has sanctioned price manipulation through tweets.
- A fright can become law within a day, as the Plano Cohen and the state of war of 1937 show.
