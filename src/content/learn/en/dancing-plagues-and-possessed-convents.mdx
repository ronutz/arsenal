In midsummer 1374, a chronicler at Limburg on the Lahn wrote that along the Rhine and the Moselle people "anhuben zu tantzen und zu rasen" (began to dance and to rage), two facing one, in one place for half a day, falling down and having others tread on their bodies ([Tilemann Elhen von Wolfhagen, Die Limburger Chronik, Marburg edition of 1828, pages 71 to 73](https://gdz.sub.uni-goettingen.de/download/pdf/PPN518206289/PPN518206289.pdf)). In Strasbourg, a council record headed "Instruction der armen dantzenden Personen so zu Sant Vit geschickt" (instruction for the poor dancing persons sent to Saint Vitus) told officials to split the dancers into three groups and take them to the saint's chapel near Saverne, where priests would sing masses for each group ([Johann Schilter's edition of Königshofen's chronicle, Strasbourg, 1698, page 1088](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_OMg-AAAAcAAJ)). And on Friday 18 August 1634 Urbain Grandier, parish priest of Loudun, was burned alive after judges appointed by the king found him guilty of bewitching a convent of Ursuline nuns ([Arrest de mort donné contre Urbain Grandier, 1634](https://archive.org/details/per_witchcraft-in-europe-and-america_grandier-urbain_1634_404)). This part of the series opened by [Mass hysteria: how groups come to believe anything](/learn/mass-hysteria-how-groups-come-to-believe-anything) goes back to those documents. It sets out what the chronicles, council orders, judgments, exorcists' reports and their critics actually say, with the numbers and where they come from; what people at the time believed was happening, and what their own physicians and sceptics said; what historians conclude now; why the ergot hypothesis is set aside; where the story of the meowing nuns comes from; and who used these outbreaks for their own ends. The modern cases with real symptoms and a social cause are in [Mass psychogenic illness: when symptoms spread](/learn/mass-psychogenic-illness-when-symptoms-spread), and the mechanisms of imitation in [Conformity, cascades and contagion](/learn/conformity-cascades-and-contagion-the-mechanisms). Quotations in German, French, Italian and Latin are given with an English gloss; the originals are at each link.

## The documents behind the stories

Most retellings of these episodes rest on a handful of nineteenth- and twentieth-century books. The documents underneath are of very different kinds, and each kind has its own blind spot: a chronicler writes years later and repeats what he heard, a council writes orders and not outcomes, a judgment records what a court decided and not what happened, an exorcist writes to prove a possession and a critic writes to expose one.

## The dancing plagues

### 1374: the Rhine, the Meuse and the Moselle

The Limburg chronicle, written by Tilemann Elhen von Wolfhagen, records the outbreak in a single dense paragraph. In midsummer 1374, "sonderlich in Teutschen Landen, auff dem Rhein und auff der Mosel" (especially in German lands, on the Rhine and on the Moselle), people began to dance and rage, stood two against one, danced in one place for half a day, often fell, and "liessen sich mit Füssen tretten auff ihren Leib" (had themselves trodden on the body with feet), claiming that this cured them; they went from town to town and church to church collecting money, and at Cologne "mehr dann fünff hundert Täntzer" (more than five hundred dancers) were found ([Limburger Chronik, 1828, pages 71 to 72](https://gdz.sub.uni-goettingen.de/download/pdf/PPN518206289/PPN518206289.pdf)). The chronicler then gives three readings side by side. Some held that <Claim status="alleged" by="by the Limburg chronicler">it was a heresy, done for money and so that men and women could come together in unchastity</Claim>, and he reports that more than a hundred unmarried women and servant girls at Cologne became pregnant during the dancing. "ein Theils Meister, sonderlich der guten Ärtzt" (some masters, especially the good physicians) said that those who danced were "von heisser Natur" (of a hot nature) or had other natural infirmities, since few were affected. "Die Meister von der heiligen Schrift" (the masters of Holy Scripture) exorcized some of the dancers, believing them possessed by the evil spirit. The outbreak lasted "wohl sechszehen Wochen" (some sixteen weeks), the dancers said they could not bear to see red, and the chronicler's own verdict was that it "war ein eitel Teuscherey" (was pure deception) (same, pages 72 to 73).

Johann Schilter, editing the Strasbourg chronicle of Jacob von Königshofen in 1698, added a note summarizing the clerical accounts of the same year. Dancers were seen in July and reached Maastricht, Liège and Tongres in the autumn; the plague struck men and women, "vornehmlich aber die armen, und Leuthe von schlechtem Ruff" (but mainly the poor and people of bad reputation), and few clergy or rich people; at Liège the dancers' horror of pointed shoes led to those shoes being forbidden; and a demon speaking through one dancer said that had they waited four or five weeks more, the demons would have entered the bodies of the rich and of princes and through them overthrown the clergy ([Schilter, 1698, pages 1086 to 1087](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_OMg-AAAAcAAJ)). Schilter calls the movement "Secta chorizantium, die Tantz Secte" (the sect of dancers) and cites Peter of Herental's life of Pope Gregory XI and Radulphus de Rivo (same). A translation of Peter of Herental quoted by Amelia Soth in JSTOR Daily gives the anticlerical edge in his own terms: the people of Liège said they had been attacked "because they were not truly baptized, inasmuch as most of the priests kept concubines", and proposed to "rise against the priests, kill them and take their property" ([Amelia Soth, JSTOR Daily, 10 January 2019](https://daily.jstor.org/when-dancing-plagues-struck-medieval-europe/)).

Justus Hecker, professor of medicine at Berlin, whose books of the 1830s made the subject famous, assembled the same sources into the narrative most later writers follow: the dancers appeared at Aix-la-Chapelle (Aachen) in July, then at Liège, Utrecht and Tongres with garlands in their hair and cloths tied round their waists; processions were held and masses said; the clergy hastened their exorcisms "in order that the evil might not spread amongst the higher classes"; at Cologne more than five hundred were affected, and the streets of Metz "are said to have been filled with eleven hundred dancers"; "gangs of idle vagabonds" imitated the dancers for alms; and within ten or eleven months the dancers had disappeared from the cities of Belgium ([Hecker, The Black Death and the Dancing Mania, translated by Benjamin Guy Babington](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1739/1739.txt)).

The year had begun with a disaster. The Limburg chronicle records, just before the dancing, a flood dated "Anno 1373 Donnerstags vor Faßnacht" (in the year 1373, on the Thursday before Shrovetide), when the Rhine and the Lahn rose "mehr dann sechs und zwantzig Fuß hoch" (more than twenty-six feet high) after "der grösseste Schnee der je gefallen war in hundert Jaren" (the greatest snow that had fallen in a hundred years) ([Limburger Chronik, 1828, pages 70 to 71](https://gdz.sub.uni-goettingen.de/download/pdf/PPN518206289/PPN518206289.pdf)). Hecker places the flood in February 1374, with the Rhine and the Main over their banks and part of Cologne's walls collapsed ([Hecker](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1739/1739.txt)), and the historian John Waller, citing E. Louis Backman, writes of "the waters of the Rhine rising 34 feet" ([Waller, The Psychologist 22, July 2009](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/dancing-plagues-and-mass-hysteria)). The sources agree on a great late-winter flood; they differ on its year and its height.

### Strasbourg, 1518: what the council wrote

The best-documented outbreak began in Strasbourg in July 1518. John Waller, author of a book-length study of it, summarizes it from the chronicles: a woman began dancing in the street and kept on for four to six days, "within a week another 34 had joined the dance", and "by the end of August, one chronicler asserts, 400 people had experienced the madness"; the authorities, having consulted physicians, prescribed "more dancing", cleared two guildhalls and the outdoor grain market and had a wooden stage built opposite the horse fair ([Waller, In a spin: the mysterious dancing epidemic of 1518, Endeavour 32(3), 2008, abstract published by Medievalists.net](https://www.medievalists.net/?p=29200)). Ned Pennant-Rea's essay for The Public Domain Review, drawing on Waller and on the historian Erik Midelfort, adds what each chronicle contributes: Paracelsus, who visited Strasbourg eight years later, names the first dancer as Frau Troffea and dates her dance to 14 July; the physicians declared it "a natural disease, which comes from overheated blood"; the architect Daniel Specklin's chronicle has the council order guild halls turned into dance floors and platforms set up "in the horse market and in the grain market"; the Imlin family chronicle says that within a month four hundred citizens were seized; and Sebastian Brant, the city's chancellor and author of The Ship of Fools, recorded the ban that followed, which allowed "honourable persons" to dance at weddings and first masses with stringed instruments but not "tambourines and drums" ([Ned Pennant-Rea, The Public Domain Review, 10 July 2018](https://publicdomainreview.org/2018/07/10/the-dancing-plague-of-1518/)). The worst affected were taken to the shrine of Saint Vitus near Saverne, and within a week or so "the stream of suffering pilgrims had diminished to a trickle" (same).

Some of the council's own records survive in print. In 1698 Schilter published, "wie aus folgendem bey dem Archiv befindlichen Registraturen zu ersehen" (as may be seen from the following records held in the archive), the council's instruction and the minutes of one sitting ([Schilter, 1698, pages 1087 to 1089](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_OMg-AAAAcAAJ)). The instruction, dated only "Veneris post Magdalene &c. XVIII" (Friday after Saint Mary Magdalene's day, in a year written as eighteen), orders that the poor people be kept in their three groups, that the servants assigned to them stay with them, that someone ride ahead to Saverne to arrange three or four priests with the dean's advice, that each group be led round the altar while a mass is sung, and that each sick person give a penny, paid from the alms collected for them (same, page 1088). The minutes of the sitting record the three councillors present deliberating "der armen menschen halb so izt dantzen" (about the poor people who are now dancing), summoning the citizens whose children were among the dancers to keep them at home or share the costs, and hearing the families: a schoolmaster said his son had watched and then begun to dance, and in the afternoon wanted to go to the dance, so that he had had to take him there; a cartwright said that when the poor people came by with a bagpipe his wife jumped up and ran after them. The hospital's steward was told to have three horses and a wagon ready, and the steward of "unser Frawen hus" (Our Lady's house) two wagons with three horses each (same, pages 1088 to 1089). These lines are the closest the record comes to showing how the dance passed from person to person: by watching, and by the sound of the pipes.

<Claim status="disputed">How many died in 1518</Claim> is not settled by any document read for this part. Waller writes that "the chronicles agree, dozens were dying from exhaustion" ([Waller, 2008, abstract](https://www.medievalists.net/?p=29200)) and, elsewhere, of "dozens of deaths" ([Waller, 2009](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/dancing-plagues-and-mass-hysteria)); Pennant-Rea writes that "at its height, as many as fifteen people were dying each day" and that the toll, if that rate were true, "could have been into the hundreds" ([Pennant-Rea, 2018](https://publicdomainreview.org/2018/07/10/the-dancing-plague-of-1518/)). The deaths he quotes come from an astrological chronicle published in 1636, more than a century later (same, note 2). The council records Schilter printed deal with transport, masses and costs, and say nothing of deaths ([Schilter, 1698](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_OMg-AAAAcAAJ)).

### Why some books say 1418

Hecker wrote that "Strasburg was visited by the 'Dancing Plague' in the year 1418" ([Hecker](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1739/1739.txt)), and that date still appears beside 1518 in modern retellings, including the series opener. Hecker's footnote points to Schilter's edition of Königshofen, "Observat. 21, of St. Vitus's Dance, p. 1085" ([Hecker, The Epidemics of the Middle Ages, translated by Babington, 1844](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/63232/63232-h/63232-h.htm)). On that page Schilter writes: "Allein im Jahr 1418. nach des von Königshofen Tode ist der so genannte Veitz-Tantz in Elsaß entstanden" (but in the year 1418, after Königshofen's death, the so-called Vitus dance arose in Alsace), and then prints the council records; he heads the rhymed verse that follows, from a Strasbourg manuscript chronicle, "St. Veits Tantz An. 1418" ([Schilter, 1698, pages 1087 and 1089](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_OMg-AAAAcAAJ)). The records themselves carry no century: only "XVIII". The date 1418 is therefore Schilter's reading of a two-digit year, which Hecker followed; Waller, Midelfort and Pennant-Rea, working from the chronicles of the sixteenth century, place the outbreak in 1518 ([Waller, 2009](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/dancing-plagues-and-mass-hysteria); [Pennant-Rea, 2018](https://publicdomainreview.org/2018/07/10/the-dancing-plague-of-1518/)). The verse Schilter printed is the one Hecker translated: "Many hundreds of men and women began to dance and jump in the public market-place, the lanes, and the streets of Strasburg ... The plague was called St. Vitus's Dance" ([Hecker, 1844](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/63232/63232-h/63232-h.htm)).

### Saint Vitus, Saint John and a name that moved

The saint invoked in Strasbourg gave his name to a disease that has nothing to do with dancing crowds. Hecker records the legend that Vitus, a young martyr of the persecution under Diocletian, prayed before his execution that all who kept his feast should be protected from the dancing mania, and that his altars multiplied after his relics were taken to Corvey in 836 ([Hecker](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1739/1739.txt)). Today "Sydenham chorea, or St. Vitus dance" is the name of a movement disorder that appears in up to 40% of patients with rheumatic fever, thought to be an autoimmune reaction after a streptococcal infection ([Beier, Lui and Pratt, Sydenham Chorea, StatPearls, 2 February 2024, record](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=28613588)). Hecker already noticed that a milder form described by Paracelsus "evidently approximates to the modern chorea" ([Hecker](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1739/1739.txt)).

Pictures have travelled as well. The neurologist Jean-Martin Charcot presented a nineteenth-century engraving after Pieter Bruegel as an image of Saint Vitus' dance; a study of the art-historical record found that the composition shows "a pilgrimage of patients suffering from St John's disease or falling sickness, one of the many names applied to epilepsy" ([Aubert, Archives of Neurology 62(1), January 2005, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=15642865)). And a ritual survives: at Echternach in Luxembourg, pilgrims still dance in procession to the grave of Saint Willibrord on Whit Tuesday; the procession "is first mentioned in the Echternach city archives in 1497", and the neurologist Henri Meige, who went to watch it in 1900, "was disappointed with the lack of hysteria" ([Krack, Neurology 53(9), December 1999, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=10599799)).

### Tarantism: the spider, the music and who was believed

In southern Italy the dance had a cause everyone could name. Tarantism, in the words of Jean Fogo Russell, "is a disorder characterized by dancing which classically follows the bite of a spider and is cured by music"; it was described from the eleventh century, observed in many outbreaks from the fifteenth to the eighteenth centuries, notably in Apulia, and treated with the tarantella, "a quick, lively, uninterrupted tune with short repetitive phrases played with increasing tempo" ([Russell, Tarantism, Medical History 23, 1979, full text](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/E48236CE504359115D4450E9DF454F20/S0025727300052054a.pdf/div-class-title-tarantism-div.pdf)).

The physician Giorgio Baglivi, whose dissertation on the tarantula became the standard account, began by complaining that earlier authors had "either gone upon Hearsay, or coin'd several Things at Pleasure", wrote that the bite was venomous only in Apulia, in summer and above all in the dog days, when reapers were bitten in the fields, and reported the belief that "Those who are once bit by it, are never quite cur'd of the Venom; for it revives every Year" ([Baglivi, The Practice of Physick, English translation, London, 1723, pages 313 to 335](https://archive.org/details/practiceofphysic00bagl)). He also wrote that women "very frequently counterfeit it under the Mask of its usual Symptoms", that the musical entertainment was so welcome to them "that 'tis a Proverb with us, Il Carnevaletto delle Donne" (the little carnival of the women), and that musicians could spot "the Cheat" when a dancer ignored the tempo, while insisting that not everyone counterfeited (same). Russell adds a caution about this famous witness: Baglivi "did not see a typical attack but relied on the description given by his father, a physician in Lecce" ([Russell, 1979](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/E48236CE504359115D4450E9DF454F20/S0025727300052054a.pdf/div-class-title-tarantism-div.pdf)).

Later observers kept finding the ritual alive. Russell cites a 1753 letter in The Gentleman's Magazine by a Storace who watched the dancers and wrote down the music, an outbreak at Wardo in Apulia in 1957 studied by Hans Schadewaldt, and the 1959 field study of Ernesto de Martino and his team at Galatina, where the afflicted sought Saint Paul's help; he concludes that "the bite of the spider is comparatively harmless" and that "there seems to be little doubt that modern outbreaks of tarantism should be called dancing mania" (same). Two authors at a Madrid hospital, reading the reports of a Spanish epidemic of 1782, found that the bodily symptoms matched the bite of the black widow (Latrodectus tredecimguttatus), "which was at that time identified as a tarantula", and attributed the cure by dance to suggestion; the behavioural disturbances, yearly recurrences and collective involvement reported in Italy, absent in Spain, suggest to them "an hysterical phenomenon, probably a continuation of the dancing mania of the Middle Age" ([Corral-Corral and Corral-Corral, Revista de Neurología 63(8), October 2016, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=27699754)). In 1698 Schilter already noted that some physicians counted the dancing sickness "als eine species morbi convulsivi" (as a kind of convulsive disease) with natural causes, "Inmassen auch der so genannte Tarantismus dahin gezogen wird" (just as so-called tarantism is counted among them), while the council records showed that contemporaries had treated it as supernatural, with spiritual remedies ([Schilter, 1698, page 1090](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_OMg-AAAAcAAJ)).

## Possessed convents

The possessions of nuns belong to the same centuries and the same Rhineland and France, and they share the dancers' pattern of a few cases growing into many. The sociologist Robert Bartholomew and the psychiatrist Simon Wessely list major convent outbreaks at Lyon in 1526, Wertet in 1550, Kintorp in 1552, Cologne and Flanders in 1560, Oderheim in 1577, Mons in 1585, Milan in 1590, Aix in 1609, Lille in 1613, Madrid in 1628, Chinon in 1640, Louviers in 1642, Auxonne in 1662 and Toulouse in 1681, and one at a convent in Ohio as late as 1880 ([Bartholomew and Wessely, British Journal of Psychiatry 180(4), April 2002](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/protean-nature-of-mass-sociogenic-illness/2BDC2262E104B8A33F3DD49773DA0D8B)). Waller observes that "nearly all possession epidemics occurred within a single 300-year period, from around 1400 to the early 1700s", because "only during this period did religious writers insist that such events were possible" ([Waller, 2009](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/dancing-plagues-and-mass-hysteria)). Four cases are documented here from their own records.

### Aix-en-Provence, 1609 to 1611

The judgment of the Parlement of Provence lists the file on which it rested: the procès-verbal of the Dominican prior Sébastien Michaëlis on the possession of Madeleine de Demandols de la Palud, "l'vne des sœurs de la compagnie Sainte Vrsule" (one of the sisters of the company of Saint Ursula), observed at Sainte-Baume from 1 January to 5 February; a report by the physicians Jacques Fontaine, Louis Grassy and Antoine Merindol and the surgeon Pierre Bontemps on her fits, on "marques insensibles" (insensible marks) on her body and on her virginity; the priest Louis Gaufridy's confessions of 14 and 15 April, his retraction "du mesme iour quinziesme Auril apres midy" (the same day, 15 April, in the afternoon) and his repeated confessions of 22 and 23 April; and a medical attestation that her marks had been abolished on Easter Day during Mass ([Arrest de la Cour de Parlement de Provence contre Louys Gaufridy, 1611](https://archive.org/details/arrestdelacourde00fran_64)). The court sentenced Gaufridy to make public penance before the cathedral, to be tortured "pour auoir de la bouche la vérité des complices" (to have from his mouth the truth about his accomplices), to be degraded from the priesthood, and to be burned alive in the place des Prêcheurs; the endorsement records that he was executed at about five in the afternoon on the last day of April 1611 (same).

Jacques Fontaine, royal physician and professor at Aix, published a treatise the same year to show "la réelle possession que le diable prend sur le corps des hommes" (the real possession the devil takes of human bodies). He held that devil's marks are dead flesh, without feeling or fluid when pricked with a needle; that the Parlement had ordered physicians and surgeons to examine Madeleine, who showed them where her marks were; and that on Easter Day they found the same places soft and bleeding, as if the marks had been erased ([Fontaine, Des marques des sorciers, Lyon, 1611, reprinted 1865](https://archive.org/details/b24884236)). To writers who warned that such marks were hard to tell from natural blemishes, he answered that "ils ne sont pas bons Médecins" (they are not good physicians) (same). Here the physicians were on the side of the demonologists.

Michaëlis's own account was translated into English in 1613, and the translator's preface shows how such books crossed confessional lines. It was put into English "that our people may see with what draffe their neighbours are fedde", and it calls the possession "the iugling and crafty conueyance of a few masse-mongring Priests, and some of their women-disciples, who with a great deale of paines are wonne to their instructors lure, and taught what they should say" ([Michaëlis, The Admirable History of the Possession and Conversion of a Penitent Woman, London, 1613](https://archive.org/details/per_witchcraft-in-europe-and-america_michaelis-sebastie_1613_663)). Michaëlis, in his dedication to the queen regent, hoped that the boy king would prove "another Iosias" who "put to death all Magicians", and conceded that some would say "it is not expedient to beleeue all that is written in this History" (same). Marianne Closson, co-editor of a new edition of Bavent's book, notes that Gaufridy's confession was first made before two Capuchins sent to "assist" him in prison, that is, to persuade him to confess, and that he then "vainement tenté de se rétracter" (tried in vain to retract) ([Closson, Fabula / Les colloques, 2025](https://www.fabula.org/pdf/colloques-14972.pdf)).

### Loudun, from 1632

The historian Michel de Certeau summarized the affair for the Encyclopædia Universalis. Loudun was a small Protestant stronghold near Poitiers; from September 1632, ten young Ursulines aged eighteen to thirty were seized by a frightening mental disorder; crowds followed, "comme dans un théâtre populaire" (as at a popular theatre), exorcists struggling for hours against "devils" who answered with insults, foretold the future, guessed the onlookers' secrets, rolled on the ground and denounced the magician; Cardinal Richelieu followed the case closely, "Grandier s'étant malencontreusement mêlé aux adversaires de l'autorité centrale" (Grandier having unwisely sided with the opponents of central authority); bishops presided over sessions and the curious came from all of France and beyond ([de Certeau, Loudun (procès de), Encyclopædia Universalis](https://www.universalis.fr/encyclopedie/proces-de-loudun/)). His judgment of the trial: "le jugement a-t-il été politique, organisé par Laubardemont, commissaire de Richelieu", after "un procès dont la forme était régulière, mais qui condamnait un homme bien étranger à la sorcellerie" (the judgment was political, organized by Laubardemont, Richelieu's commissioner, after a trial regular in form that condemned a man quite foreign to sorcery) (same).

The judgment itself names its authority: councillors appointed by the king as sovereign judges, acting under letters patent of 8 July 1634. It declares Grandier guilty of magic, maleficium and the possession of some Ursuline nuns and some lay women of Loudun; orders him to make public penance, to be tortured for the names of his accomplices, and to be burned alive on the place Sainte-Croix together with the pacts and magic characters held by the clerk and the manuscript book he had written against the celibacy of priests; and sets aside 150 livres for a copper plate engraved with the judgment, to be fixed in the Ursulines' church in perpetuity. It records that it was pronounced and carried out on Friday 18 August 1634. Printed with it is a "cédule", a pact with Lucifer in Grandier's name, said to have been returned by a demon on 2 August 1634 ([Arrest de mort, 1634](https://archive.org/details/per_witchcraft-in-europe-and-america_grandier-urbain_1634_404)).

The case for the possession was made in print the same year. The Capuchin Father Tranquille de Saint-Rémi called it "la plus memorable, & la plus celebre en ce genre" (the most memorable and celebrated of its kind) in several centuries, and the king's own work: Laubardemont, sent to demolish Loudun's citadel, saw the nuns' plight and advised the king, judging "que les demons ne pourroient estre chassés qu'à coups de Sceptre" (that the demons could be driven out only with blows of the sceptre) ([Tranquille de Saint-Rémi, Véritable relation des justes procédures, Paris, 1634](https://archive.org/details/per_witchcraft-in-europe-and-america_tranquille-de-saint_1634_983)). His defence answers the explanations already current: the nuns were held by the world "pour fourbes & pour folles" (for frauds and for madwomen); but girls of good family gained nothing from fraud and lost their boarders and their income; they had never seen Grandier; and <Claim status="attributed" by="by Father Tranquille">"plus de trente Medecins les ont iugées par dessus la nature" (more than thirty physicians judged their contortions beyond nature)</Claim>. He adds that their affliction did not cease after Grandier's death (same). It did not: de Certeau notes that the possession continued, that the Jesuit Jean-Joseph Surin arrived in December 1634 and tried to reduce the public exorcisms, and that the prioress, Jeanne des Anges, turned from possessed woman into visionary as Loudun became a centre of devotion ([de Certeau](https://www.universalis.fr/encyclopedie/proces-de-loudun/)).

One of those later sessions shows the theatre at work. A procès-verbal dated Thursday 29 November 1635, printed at Orléans, records that the Archbishop of Tours had asked Surin to see that an English lord, "le sieur de Montagu", "receut edification en la veuë de ce qui se passe aux Exorcismes" (received edification from seeing what happens at the exorcisms); during the exorcism the demon Balam left the prioress, and the name JOSEPH formed in blood on the back of her left hand ([Relation de la sortie du demon Balam du corps de la Mere Prieure des Urselines de Loudun, Orléans, 1635](https://archive.org/details/per_witchcraft-in-europe-and-america_1635_811)). An English Protestant reprint of the same procès-verbal added "Observations upon the Relation" that read the scene as staged: "these french Comedians playing their parts so cunningly at Loudon"; a "holy potion" of sack, salad oil, galbanum and asafoetida given to the nun; "the devils are no where else to be seene or heard, but in the actions of the maid, and the tongue of the Priest"; and, for the name on the hand, the suggestion that readers write with lemon juice on paper and hold it to the fire ([A Relation of the Devill Balams Departure, with Observations upon the Relation, 1635](https://archive.org/details/per_witchcraft-in-europe-and-america_1635_812)).

Other readings followed. A Protestant history printed at Amsterdam in 1693 described the affair as the long and fatal intrigues of a convent and of a great many churchmen, supported by some of the magistrates and favoured at court, which had sent a priest to the fire ([Aubin, Histoire des diables de Loudun, Amsterdam, 1693](https://archive.org/details/histoiredesdiab00niaugoog)). In 1886 the physicians Gabriel Legué and Gilles de la Tourette, who dedicated the book to Charcot as their "illustre Maître" (illustrious master), published Jeanne des Anges's autobiography with a preface in which Charcot wrote that hysteria "peut, à travers les âges, non seulement conserver son individualité, mais encore se montrer immuable" (can, across the ages, not only keep its identity but even show itself unchanging); the dedication thanks him for showing "que les malheureuses possédées n'étaient que des malades" (that the unhappy possessed women were only sick) ([Sœur Jeanne des Anges, autobiographie d'une hystérique possédée, Paris, 1886](https://archive.org/details/soeurjeannedesan00jean)). The same editors also judged Jeanne "suggestionnée par Laubardemont, Mignon, tous les ennemis du curé" (under the suggestion of Laubardemont, Mignon and all the parish priest's enemies), wrote that the nuns had been "stylées" (coached) until "elles surent bien leur leçon d'après le rituel" (they knew their lesson from the ritual well), and explained the name on her hand as a skin mark produced by suggestion (same). Their account of an episode in December 1632 matters most for what ended outbreaks: Grandier asked the bailiff to "séquestrer et séparer les religieuses"; the Archbishop of Bordeaux, Henri d'Escoubleau de Sourdis, to whom Grandier appealed, sent his physician, who declared the nuns not possessed, forbade the exorcisms and ordered the separation, and "ces sages mesures mirent fin comme par enchantement aux convulsions des religieuses" (these wise measures ended the nuns' convulsions as if by enchantment) for several months (same, citing an ordinance of 27 December 1632).

### Louviers, 1642 to 1647

Louviers produced the clearest contemporary medical critique. After the queen regent had sent commissioners, a "ieune Médecin du Commun de sa Maison" (young physician of her household), Dr Yvelin, was sent to check, and his circle published the Examen de la possession des religieuses de Louviers in Paris in 1643 ([Histoire de Madeleine Bavent, reprint of the 1652 edition with a notice and supplementary documents, Rouen, 1878, bibliography and pages 135 to 146](https://archive.org/details/histoiredemadel00lemogoog)). The Examen, written as a letter by a companion who spent fifteen days at Louviers with the physician, reports that of about fifty nuns, six were held to be possessed and seventeen "obsedées ou charmées" (obsessed or bewitched); that all were healthy in their natural functions; and that their movements had nothing extraordinary about them (same). It then applies the tests of the Roman Ritual one by one. No nun spoke a language she had not learned: one, exorcized "ex parte Mariæ Virginis" (on behalf of the Virgin Mary), asked why the exorcist spoke of the Virgin's "départ" (departure). None revealed hidden things, and a prophecy that the companion would be ill that night failed. Their pulse and breathing rose with their agitation. A girl of about fifteen exorcized from nine in the morning to five in the afternoon nearly died of a faint and was revived with wine on the physician's advice. And the devils spoke with a Norman accent: "ce sont des Diables normands" (they are Norman devils) (same).

The Examen does not call it all fraud. "Ie ne veux pas attribuer tout ce deffaut à vne fourberie: ie crois qu'il y a de l'erreur dans l'imagination de quelques-vnes de ces Filles" ("I do not wish to put all this down to trickery: I believe there is error in the imagination of some of these girls"), it says, and it names the mechanism: the nuns' conversation with one another and "le récit qu'elles se font l'vne à l'autre de leur imagination" (what they tell one another of their imaginings). Hence the physician's advice "de les separer" (to separate them), without which "toutes les autres Religieuses courreront la mesme fortune que celles-cy" (all the other nuns will go the same way as these). The theologians refused a proposal to give the nuns an unconsecrated host, to see whether they would convulse as they did at communion (same). The reply came from two physicians of Rouen, Lamperière and Magnart, who defended the possession, and from a run of pamphlets for and against that the 1878 editor lists (same, bibliography).

The accused nun's story survives in her own words, shaped by others. Madeleine Bavent was thrown in 1643 into "la basse-fosse" (the dungeon) of the bishop's palace at Évreux, interrogated from 21 June 1644, and confessed to the sabbath, confirming the accusations of the possessed nuns ([Closson, 2025](https://www.fabula.org/pdf/colloques-14972.pdf)). Her confessor, the Penitentiary of Évreux, pressed her for two hours at a time; she later wrote that she gave him the name he wanted, "Toute ennuyée & lasse que j'étois, je luy dis à la fin, pour demeurer en repos" ("worn out and weary as I was, I told him at last, so as to be left in peace") (same). The Parlement of Rouen's judgment of 21 August 1647 ordered the body of the late director Mathurin Picard and his vicar Thomas Boullé to be dragged on hurdles through the city, Boullé to make public penance before the cathedral, to be tortured for accomplices and burned alive in the Vieux-Marché, and Picard's corpse to be burned with him, "iusques à ce que lesdits corps soient réduits en cendres, lesquelles seront jettées au vent" (until the bodies are reduced to ashes, which shall be thrown to the wind); it deferred Bavent's judgment, ordered the former superior to be arrested, ordered the nuns to be moved to other convents or to their families, and urged the bishops to send outside confessors to convents of women three or four times a year ([Arrest du Parlement de Rouen, 21 August 1647, in Histoire de Madeleine Bavent, 1878, pages 119 to 124](https://archive.org/details/histoiredemadel00lemogoog)). The judgment thus ended with the separation the physician had asked for in 1643, four years later, after one man had been burned alive and another's corpse burned.

### Würzburg, 1749

The last case here comes from the convent of Unterzell near Würzburg, where, in Bartholomew and Wessely's summary, abnormal movements and trance states affected the nuns and "suspicion of witchcraft fell on a Sister Maria von Mossau who was beheaded" ([Bartholomew and Wessely, 2002](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/protean-nature-of-mass-sociogenic-illness/2BDC2262E104B8A33F3DD49773DA0D8B)). The Jesuit Georg Gaar's sermon at the pyre, printed in Italian at Verona the same year, gives the charge as the court accepted it: Maria Renata, the sub-prioress, had by her own confession made four nuns dangerously ill with poisonous breath and enchanted herbs and bewitched five more and a lay sister not yet professed; her sentence was "mitigata" (mitigated) by the clemency of "Sua Altezza Reverendissima" (His Most Reverend Highness), the ruler, so that she was "solamente decapitata" (only beheaded), and her body was burned "acciò non rimanga alcun vestigio di costei" (so that no trace of her remains) ([Gaar, Ragionamento fatto avanti al rogo di Maria Renata, Verona, 1749](https://archive.org/details/per_witchcraft-in-europe-and-america_gaar-georg_1749_359)). The title page dates the burning to 21 June 1749 (same). The Verona printer added critical annotations "come per correttivo" (as a corrective) against the harm the example might do to weak minds and "Tribunali meno illuminati" (less enlightened courts); one recalls the Jesuit theologian Adam Tanner's report that, at a public reading of witches' confessions in a German city, the condemned confessed to having killed people who were standing alive and well among the spectators (same).

## The meowing nuns: following a story to its source

One image recurs in writing on mass hysteria: a convent where the nuns meowed like cats. The chain behind it can be followed link by link.

Zimmermann introduces the story with a claim about women: "Weibliche Imagination ist immer reitzbarer als Männerimagination" (female imagination is always more excitable than male imagination), so that in orphanages, hospitals and convents "die Nervenkrankheit von einem Mädchen so leicht und schnell die Krankheit von allen" (the nervous illness of one girl so easily and quickly becomes the illness of all) ([Zimmermann, Ueber die Einsamkeit, volume 2, Carlsruhe printing, 1784, pages 62 to 63](https://archive.org/details/10042298bsb)). Babington's note translates it faithfully ([Hecker, 1844, note 102](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/63232/63232-h/63232-h.htm)). Bartholomew and Wessely cite it as Hecker's ([Bartholomew and Wessely, 2002](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/protean-nature-of-mass-sociogenic-illness/2BDC2262E104B8A33F3DD49773DA0D8B)). In the same paragraph they cite a different case from a different author: "At Cambrai, France, in 1491 a group of nuns exhibited fits, yelped like dogs and foretold the future" (same, citing Robbins, 1966). Waller's version joins a date like Cambrai's to cats like Zimmermann's ([Waller, 2009](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/dancing-plagues-and-mass-hysteria)), and the fact-checking site repeats it under the wrong name ([Bethania Palma, Snopes, 14 October 2022](https://www.snopes.com/articles/460234/dancing-plague-medieval-europe/)). <Claim status="reported" by="by Zimmermann, from an unnamed book">A French convent where all the nuns meowed together</Claim> may have existed; no document read for this part names it, dates it or says who saw it.

## Ergot: a hypothesis and why historians set it aside

Ergot is a fungus, Claviceps purpurea, that grows on rye; bread made from infected grain causes ergotism, which has "two main clinical forms of toxicity, gangrenous and convulsive, where coma and death often supervene", with a death rate "between 10 and 20 per cent in major outbreaks" ([Lee, Journal of the Royal College of Physicians of Edinburgh 39(2), June 2009, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=19847980)). In 1976 Linnda Caporael proposed ergot as the cause of the afflicted girls' fits at Salem, and Nicholas Spanos and Jack Gottlieb answered in the same journal later that year ([Caporael, Science 192(4234), 2 April 1976, record](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=769159); [Spanos and Gottlieb, Science 194(4272), 24 December 1976, record](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=795029)); that dispute is followed in [Witch hunts and moral panics](/learn/witch-hunts-and-moral-panics). For the dancing plagues, Waller notes "a vogue for simple biological explanations" that attributed them to ergot, citing E. Louis Backman (1952) and Matossian (1989) ([Waller, 2009](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/dancing-plagues-and-mass-hysteria)).

| Question | What ergotism does | What the dancing records show | Sources |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Can the poisoned dance for days? | the gangrenous form restricts blood flow to the limbs; the convulsive form brings fits, coma, death in 10 to 20% of major outbreaks | dancing for half a day at a time (1374), for four to six days (1518), dancers led to a shrine and back | Lee, 2009; Pennant-Rea, 2018; Limburg chronicle; Waller, 2008 |
| Does the behaviour follow a belief? | toxicity does not choose a saint or a colour | dancers of 1374 could not bear red and pointed shoes and called on Saint John; those of 1518 were sent to the saint believed to have cursed them | Limburg chronicle; Schilter; Hecker; Waller, 2009 |
| Does it spread by sight and sound? | poisoning follows the bread, not the onlookers | a boy watched and began to dance; a woman ran after the bagpipes; vagabonds imitated for alms | Schilter, 1698; Hecker |
| Does it stop when believers are separated or the rite changes? | no | Loudun's convulsions stopped for months when exorcisms were forbidden and the nuns separated in 1632, according to the 1886 editors; Strasbourg's dwindled after the shrine ritual | Jeanne des Anges, 1886; Pennant-Rea, 2018 |

Waller's own conclusion is that "it is highly unlikely that ergotism would cause remorseless bouts of dancing", citing a 1931 monograph on ergot and ergotism ([Waller, 2009](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/dancing-plagues-and-mass-hysteria)); Pennant-Rea puts the physiology plainly: ergot "restricts blood flow to the extremities. Someone poisoned by it simply could not dance for several days in a row" ([Pennant-Rea, 2018](https://publicdomainreview.org/2018/07/10/the-dancing-plague-of-1518/)). A public-health review of 1997 found claims for demonic possession, epilepsy, the tarantula bite, ergot poisoning and social adversity, judged it "unlikely that Dancing Mania resulted from a single cause", and called it "one of the unresolved mysteries of public health" ([Donaldson, Cavanagh and Rankin, Public Health 111(4), July 1997, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=9242030)).

The people who lived through these outbreaks did not share one explanation, and neither do the historians who study them. The table lists the explanations with who offered them and on what evidence.

Three of these deserve more space. Waller argues that the dancers and the nuns were in dissociative trance, since "it is hard to imagine people dancing for several days, with bruised and bloodied feet, except in an altered state of consciousness"; that trance comes more easily to those who already believe in possession, which the anthropologist Erika Bourguignon called an "environment of belief"; and that the decade before 1518 brought famine, high bread prices, starving migrants, leprosy, plague and the new disease syphilis to Strasbourg ([Waller, 2009](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/dancing-plagues-and-mass-hysteria)). He also blames the authorities: they "ensured that the outbreak got out of control by having the dancers gathered together and left to dance in some of the most public spaces in the city" (same). Bartholomew, by contrast, questions the whole category: diagnoses of mass psychogenic illness rest on "subjective, ambiguous categories that reflect stereotypes of female normality", and labelling dancing manias, tarantism and demonopathy as its variants ignores that "episodes may involve normal, rational people who possess unfamiliar conduct codes, world-views and political agendas" ([Bartholomew, Psychological Medicine 24(2), May 1994, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=8084927)). And de Certeau sets aside the old three-way choice ("Miracle ? Duperie ? Maladie ?", miracle, deception or illness) for "quelque chose de plus fondamental: c'est le procès de l'autorité religieuse, à qui se substitue la 'raison d'État'" (something more fundamental: the trial of religious authority, replaced by reason of state) ([de Certeau](https://www.universalis.fr/encyclopedie/proces-de-loudun/)).

Two discrepancies in the modern literature should be kept in view. Waller writes that Grandier was burned "after an investigation by the Inquisition" ([Waller, 2009](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/dancing-plagues-and-mass-hysteria)); the judgment shows that he was tried by royal commissioners acting under letters patent ([Arrest de mort, 1634](https://archive.org/details/per_witchcraft-in-europe-and-america_grandier-urbain_1634_404)). And Russell dates the author of the 1753 letter on tarantism to 1763 to 1796, a span that begins ten years after the letter was printed ([Russell, 1979](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/E48236CE504359115D4450E9DF454F20/S0025727300052054a.pdf/div-class-title-tarantism-div.pdf)).

## Brazil: the "choreomania" of Itapagipe, 1882

Brazil had its own dancing epidemic, and its own racial explanation of it. The historian Filipe Pinto Monteiro reconstructs it from the medical press. In October 1882 the Gazeta Médica da Bahia printed a short note, "Uma moléstia singular" (a singular illness), describing people in Itapagipe, a suburb of Salvador, who "depois de caminharem naturalmente em apparencia por algum tempo, dobram de repente uma ou ambas as pernas" (after walking apparently normally for some time, suddenly bend one or both legs), as if lame or staggering, and then walk on ([Monteiro, Revista Brasileira de História da Ciência 8, July to December 2015, pages 65 to 82](https://rbhciencia.emnuvens.com.br/revista/article/download/188/148)). In April 1883 the journal published the report of a medical board set up by the municipal council and led by José Luiz de Almeida Couto, under the title "Choreomania": a nervous illness of the chorea class, at first confined to the workers of the Penha textile factory, that then spread through the suburb and "transmite-se facilmente pelo que se chama contágio por imitação" (is easily transmitted by what is called contagion by imitation) (same).

The physician Raimundo Nina Rodrigues took up the case in 1890 under the name "abasia coreiforme epidêmica" (epidemic choreiform abasia). He accepted contagion by imitation and suggestion, but looked for "alguma coisa de anormal" (something abnormal) in the Brazilian environment and, following the French writer on crowds Le Bon, gave race a separate place; in a later book he used the epidemic to argue for a "fundo nevropathico ou hysterico do negro" (a neuropathic or hysterical basis in Black people), and wrote that he knew of outbreaks "em diversos candomblés e sambas" (in various candomblés and sambas) (same). Monteiro reads these texts as the beginnings of crowd psychology in Brazil, applied to collective and religious epidemics "vinculadas à população negra e mestiça" (linked to the Black and mixed-race population) ([Monteiro, record at the Arca repository of the Oswaldo Cruz Foundation](https://www.arca.fiocruz.br/handle/icict/54050)). The board's contagion explanation and Nina Rodrigues's racial one were offered for the same facts; the board's rested on what its physicians observed in Itapagipe, while the racial reading followed Le Bon's general theory, as Monteiro shows ([Monteiro, 2015](https://rbhciencia.emnuvens.com.br/revista/article/download/188/148)).

## Who steered, and who did not

The dancing plagues had no single author. The chronicles describe a contagion that ran ahead of everyone, and the authorities mostly reacted. Some reactions made things worse: in Waller's reading, Strasbourg's council turned a private affliction into a public spectacle by building stages and hiring musicians ([Waller, 2009](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/dancing-plagues-and-mass-hysteria)), and the clergy of 1374 hurried their exorcisms, in Hecker's account, so that the dance would not reach the higher classes ([Hecker](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1739/1739.txt)). Others used it: the vagabonds who imitated the dancers for alms (same), and the dancers of Liège who, according to Peter of Herental, turned their affliction against the priests ([Soth, 2019](https://daily.jstor.org/when-dancing-plagues-struck-medieval-europe/)).

The convent possessions were steered, and the record names the hands. Exorcisms were held in public before crowds and visiting nobles, a form de Certeau calls "un théâtre populaire" ([de Certeau](https://www.universalis.fr/encyclopedie/proces-de-loudun/)); the 1635 session at Loudun was staged so that an English visitor would receive "edification" ([Relation de la sortie du demon Balam, 1635](https://archive.org/details/per_witchcraft-in-europe-and-america_1635_811)). Confessions were obtained in prison by confessors and Capuchins, as with Gaufridy and Bavent, and the judgments against Gaufridy, Grandier and Boullé each ordered the "question ordinaire et extraordinaire", judicial torture to extract the names of accomplices, before execution ([Arrest contre Gaufridy, 1611](https://archive.org/details/arrestdelacourde00fran_64); [Arrest de mort, 1634](https://archive.org/details/per_witchcraft-in-europe-and-america_grandier-urbain_1634_404); [Arrest du Parlement de Rouen, 1647](https://archive.org/details/histoiredemadel00lemogoog)). At Loudun a royal commissioner ran the trial, which de Certeau calls political ([de Certeau](https://www.universalis.fr/encyclopedie/proces-de-loudun/)). And the possessions were propaganda on both sides of the confessional divide: Tranquille presented Loudun as a victory of the Catholic truth over heresy, while English translators printed the same kind of documents to show their readers "with what draffe their neighbours are fedde" ([Tranquille, 1634](https://archive.org/details/per_witchcraft-in-europe-and-america_tranquille-de-saint_1634_983); [Michaëlis in English, 1613](https://archive.org/details/per_witchcraft-in-europe-and-america_michaelis-sebastie_1613_663)). How a staged fear is turned into consent is the subject of [How manipulators harness collective panic](/learn/how-manipulators-harness-collective-panic) and, for the older techniques, [A short history of propaganda and psychological warfare](/learn/a-short-history-of-propaganda-and-psychological-warfare); the confessions extracted from the accused are the theme of [Witch hunts and moral panics](/learn/witch-hunts-and-moral-panics).

What ended outbreaks is also on the record, though rarely in one place. Separating the nuns and stopping the exorcisms ended Loudun's convulsions for months in 1632, according to the 1886 editors ([Jeanne des Anges, 1886](https://archive.org/details/soeurjeannedesan00jean)); the physician at Louviers asked for the same in 1643 and the court ordered it in 1647 ([Histoire de Madeleine Bavent, 1878](https://archive.org/details/histoiredemadel00lemogoog)); Strasbourg banned public dancing and drums and sent the dancers to a ritual they believed in ([Pennant-Rea, 2018](https://publicdomainreview.org/2018/07/10/the-dancing-plague-of-1518/)). The evidence on what helps, then and now, is weighed in [What ends an outbreak and what helps](/learn/what-ends-an-outbreak-and-what-helps).

## Echoes

Some forms outlived their centuries. Tarantism was still being observed in Apulia in 1957 and 1959 ([Russell, 1979](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/E48236CE504359115D4450E9DF454F20/S0025727300052054a.pdf/div-class-title-tarantism-div.pdf)), and the dancing procession at Echternach continues ([Krack, 1999](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=10599799)). Simon Wessely's 1987 description of "mass motor hysteria" (abnormal motor behaviour, following prolonged tension, spreading gradually and sometimes lasting long) is the category Bartholomew and Wessely later applied to the convents ([Wessely, Psychological Medicine 17(1), February 1987, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=3575566)). Modern outbreaks of motor symptoms, such as the laughing epidemic of Tanganyika in 1962, which Waller places among outbreaks of mass motor hysteria in African schools, and the tic-like behaviours that spread with online videos after 2019, are covered in [Mass psychogenic illness: when symptoms spread](/learn/mass-psychogenic-illness-when-symptoms-spread) and, for the platforms, in [Social media and the contagion of symptoms and beliefs](/learn/social-media-and-the-contagion-of-symptoms-and-beliefs). An illness called "Dinga dinga" in Bundibugyo district, Uganda, with uncontrollable tremors, fever and fatigue mainly among women and girls, prompted two authors to ask whether it was "a new emerging disease or a modern-day dancing plague"; they describe the investigations as ongoing ([Patel and Surti, Diagnostic Microbiology and Infectious Disease 113(2), October 2025, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=40516158)).

## What to remember

- The dancing plague of 1374 is recorded by a chronicler at Limburg: dancing on the Rhine and Moselle, more than 500 dancers at Cologne, about sixteen weeks; he gave physicians', theologians' and his own explanation (heresy and deception) side by side.
- Strasbourg's council records survive in Schilter's 1698 edition: groups of dancers sent to Saint Vitus near Saverne, families summoned, a boy who began to dance after watching, a woman who ran after the bagpipes.
- The date 1418 for Strasbourg comes from Schilter's reading of a record dated only "XVIII", which Hecker followed; historians working from the sixteenth-century chronicles place it in 1518.
- The 400 dancers of 1518 rest on one chronicle; the deaths rest on later chronicles, one of 1636; the council records do not mention them.
- Tarantism was blamed on a spider whose bite is comparatively harmless; contemporaries already suspected feigning, and a modern reading separates real bites (probably the black widow) from the cure by suggestion.
- At Aix, Loudun and Louviers, possessed nuns accused priests: Gaufridy, Grandier and Boullé were sentenced to torture and burned alive, and Picard's corpse was burned; physicians argued on both sides, a royal commissioner ran the Loudun trial, and contemporaries already proposed fraud, imagination and contagion by talk.
- Separation ended Loudun's convulsions for a while in 1632, according to the 1886 editors, and was urged at Louviers in 1643; the court there ordered it only in 1647.
- The meowing nuns trace back to one unnamed "good medical book" cited by Zimmermann in 1784, with no convent or date.
- Historians judge that ergot does not explain dancing for days, behaviour shaped by saints and colours, or spread by sight and sound; historians now read these outbreaks as trance or motor hysteria in a world that believed in curses and possession, or, with Bartholomew, as roles that outsiders mislabelled.
