<MethodNote />

A witch hunt is the clearest case of a group coming to believe something false and acting on it with full legal force. For about three centuries European courts tried people for making pacts with the Devil, flying to nocturnal gatherings and harming neighbours by sorcery, and executed tens of thousands of them. The hunts were not spontaneous outbursts of village fear alone: they needed a theory of the witch, books that spread it, judges who accepted it and procedures that turned suspicion into confession and confession into new names. This part of the series opened by [the map of mass hysteria](/learn/mass-hysteria-how-groups-come-to-believe-anything) follows that machinery from the papal bull of 1484 and the Malleus Maleficarum to the trials and the disputes over how many died, through Salem in 1692 and the three centuries it took Massachusetts to clear every name, to the Portuguese Inquisition's first visit to Brazil, which hunted other targets. It ends with the sociologist Stanley Cohen's concept of moral panic, the name now given to the same reaction when the gallows are replaced by headlines and new laws, and with the critics who think the concept has been stretched too far. After reading, you can say what each number rests on, tell a witch hunt from a moral panic, and recognize who was steering each case.

English quotations from early modern documents keep their original spelling. Portuguese-language sources are quoted verbatim with an English gloss.

## Two words, one pattern

*Witch hunt* began as a literal description and became a metaphor for any campaign that presumes guilt and treats denial as proof. *Moral panic* was coined for modern societies. In Cohen's definition, quoted by Erich Goode and Nachman Ben-Yehuda in the opening chapter of their book on the subject, a moral panic occurs when <Claim status="attributed" by="by Stanley Cohen, 1972">"A condition, episode, person or group of persons emerges to become defined as a threat to societal values and interests; its nature is presented in a stylized and stereotypical fashion by the mass media; the moral barricades are manned by editors, bishops, politicians and other right-thinking people"</Claim>, after which "socially accredited experts pronounce their diagnoses and solutions" ([Goode and Ben-Yehuda, Moral Panics, 2nd edition, Wiley-Blackwell, 2009, chapter 1, publisher's excerpt](https://media.wiley.com/product_data/excerpt/47/14051893/1405189347.pdf)). The person or group cast as the threat is the *folk devil*; the people who lead the crusade are what Howard Becker called moral entrepreneurs, whom Goode and Ben-Yehuda describe as leaders "who believe that existing remedies are insufficient" ([Goode and Ben-Yehuda, 2009](https://media.wiley.com/product_data/excerpt/47/14051893/1405189347.pdf)). The [conformity and cascades part](/learn/conformity-cascades-and-contagion-the-mechanisms) sets out the mechanisms; this part follows the cases.

Chas Critcher, one of the authors of the British study of the 1970s "mugging" panic, draws the line between the two phenomena in one sentence: "modern moral panics are unthinkable without the media, though medieval witch trials managed without them" ([Lashmar, Journalist, Folk Devil?, in Moral Panics in the Contemporary World, Bloomsbury, 2013, accepted version](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/19050/3/MoralPanicBloomsburyLashmar%20%2528PL%20Revise%2529.pdf)). The early modern hunts did have a medium, though: the printing press, and one book above all.

## From doubt to doctrine: the witch becomes a crime

For most of the Middle Ages the Church doubted that witches existed in the sense later courts assumed. Peter Leeson and Jacob Russ, economists who assembled the largest dataset of European witch trials, summarize the record: "Between 900 and 1400, Christian authorities were unwilling to so much as admit that witches existed, let alone try someone for the crime of being one", and in 1258 Pope Alexander IV issued a canon to prevent prosecutions for witchcraft; the shift came when theologians at the University of Paris declared witchcraft the result of contracts with Satan in 1398 ([Leeson and Russ, Witch Trials, The Economic Journal 128, August 2018, author's copy](https://www.peterleeson.com/Witch_Trials.pdf)). By 1550, they write, authorities had "reversed their position entirely" ([Leeson and Russ, 2018](https://www.peterleeson.com/Witch_Trials.pdf)).

## The handbook: Summis desiderantes and the Malleus Maleficarum

### An inquisitor at Ravensburg and Innsbruck

The Dominican inquisitor Heinrich Institoris (Kramer, in German) is the key figure. The historian Hans Peter Broedel, author of the first book-length study of the Malleus in English, found that although Institoris claimed in a 1490 report to the Nuremberg council to have been responsible for the discovery and execution of more than two hundred witches, <Claim status="established" by="by the surviving records, according to Broedel">"there is an almost complete lack of corroborating evidence"</Claim>: the only witch trials in which his participation can be proven took place at Ravensburg in 1484, where eight women were convicted and burned after he preached against witchcraft and invited denunciations, and at Innsbruck in 1485 ([Broedel, The Malleus Maleficarum and the construction of witchcraft, Manchester University Press, 2003, open access](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/handle/20.500.12657/35002/341393.pdf)). Broedel concludes that "Institoris' own account of the extent of his personal experience in witchcraft prosecutions is greatly exaggerated".

Opposition from local clergy and officials sent Institoris to Rome. The result was the bull *Summis desiderantes affectibus* of Pope Innocent VIII, which in Montague Summers's 1928 translation complains that "many persons of both sexes, unmindful of their own salvation and straying from the Catholic Faith, have abandoned themselves to devils, incubi and succubi", names Institoris and his fellow Dominican Jacob Sprenger as inquisitors, and orders that they be allowed to proceed "without let or hindrance"; Summers's text dates it 9 December 1484 ([Malleus Maleficarum, translated by Montague Summers, 1928, Standard Ebooks edition](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/heinrich-kramer/malleus-maleficarum/montague-summers/text/single-page)). Broedel notes that the bishop of Strasbourg was charged with enforcing it, by excommunication or the secular arm if needed ([Broedel, 2003](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/handle/20.500.12657/35002/341393.pdf)). Christopher Mackay, whose 2009 translation is the standard scholarly edition, notes that the bull's account of the situation was taken largely from the petition that requested it, so the bull largely repeated the inquisitor's own account ([Mackay, The Hammer of Witches, Cambridge University Press, 2009, publisher's excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805217/47875/excerpt/9780521747875_excerpt.pdf)).

Innsbruck went badly for him. Broedel recounts that a commission halted the proceedings, rejecting the link between female sexuality and witchcraft that the inquisitor pressed; Institoris stayed on, "harassing witnesses, even briefly seizing a suspected witch or two on his own initiative", until in February 1486 Bishop Georg Golser ordered him to leave the diocese and warned that the husbands and friends of the women he had persecuted might harm him ([Broedel, 2003](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/handle/20.500.12657/35002/341393.pdf)). Golser had already written to a friend that Institoris seemed "completely childish on account of his age" (Broedel's translation).

### The book and its credentials

Institoris then wrote the Malleus Maleficarum, the *Hammer of Witches*. Its date is itself disputed in the sources read: Mackay's introduction says it was "Published in 1486" by a press at Speyer and was "already being dispatched for sale in February 1487" ([Mackay, 2009](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805217/47875/excerpt/9780521747875_excerpt.pdf)); Broedel used what he calls the "first edition of the Malleus (1487)" ([Broedel, 2003](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/handle/20.500.12657/35002/341393.pdf)); and Summers quotes an older French bibliographer who placed the first edition at Cologne in 1489 while admitting he did not know "on what authority" ([Summers, 1928](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/heinrich-kramer/malleus-maleficarum/montague-summers/text/single-page)).

Authorship is disputed too. The book names Sprenger as co-author. Broedel thinks that, beyond lending the work his prestige, "Sprenger's contribution was minimal", because the work is of one piece and Institoris wrote the third part alone; Mackay says the claim that Institoris "in fact wrote the whole thing himself" is often made with little solid evidence, and that any narrower role for Sprenger is "mere speculation" ([Broedel, 2003](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/handle/20.500.12657/35002/341393.pdf); [Mackay, 2009](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805217/47875/excerpt/9780521747875_excerpt.pdf)).

The book was printed with the bull, letters of the future emperor Maximilian dated 1486, and an endorsement by the theology faculty of Cologne. That endorsement is the third dispute. Broedel describes two approbations: one signed by just four members of the faculty, which endorsed the book's third part as a model for prosecutions, and a more general one with twice as many signatures that did not mention the Malleus at all; the historian Joseph Hansen suggested the second was "in effect, a forgery committed by Institoris with the help of a compliant notary", while Schnyder argued that busy academics simply signed the general text ([Broedel, 2003](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/handle/20.500.12657/35002/341393.pdf)). Mackay says Hansen is sometimes misunderstood, since "what he actually claimed was that the proceedings were flawed", and calls the case for fabrication "fundamentally nugatory" ([Mackay, 2009](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805217/47875/excerpt/9780521747875_excerpt.pdf)). Summers, writing in 1928, had stated flatly that the book "was officially approved by all the Doctors of the Theological Faculty on 9 May, 1487", while the notarial document his own translation reproduces is dated Saturday 19 May 1487 ([Summers, 1928](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/heinrich-kramer/malleus-maleficarum/montague-summers/text/single-page)). <Claim status="disputed" by="by Hansen, Schnyder, Broedel and Mackay">How the Cologne approbation was obtained</Claim> remains open; that it was printed as a seal of authority is not.

### What the book taught

Two features of the Malleus matter for this series: whom it blamed and how it told judges to proceed. On the first, its conclusion to the question of why more women than men are witches is blunt: "All witchcraft comes from carnal lust, which is in women insatiable" ([Summers, 1928](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/heinrich-kramer/malleus-maleficarum/montague-summers/text/single-page)). In the Scottish survey described below, 84% of the accused whose sex is known were women ([Survey of Scottish Witchcraft, Introduction](https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/faq/)).

On procedure, the third part is a manual for producing confessions. It states that "common justice demands that a witch should not be condemned to death unless she is convicted by her own confession", so the confession becomes the goal of the process. To obtain it, the book discusses whether a judge may promise the accused her life; one opinion it reports is that she may be promised her life on condition of life imprisonment, provided she supplies "evidence which will lead to the conviction of other witches", and "she is not to be told, when she is promised her life, that she is to be imprisoned in this way"; another, that the judge may promise her life and then hand the sentence to another judge. It adds that "it has certainly been very often found by experience that many would confess the truth if they were not held back by the fear of death". If torture fails, it "must be continued on the second or third day, but not repeated at that present time unless there should be some fresh indication of its probable success", a distinction that let a single authorized session run on for days ([Summers, 1928](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/heinrich-kramer/malleus-maleficarum/montague-summers/text/single-page)). Deception, the promise of mercy and the demand for accomplices are the techniques; the [psyops part on the human attack surface](/learn/the-human-attack-surface-why-repetition-works) and the [part on how manipulators harness collective panic](/learn/how-manipulators-harness-collective-panic) follow the same levers in modern settings.

### How far the book reached

Broedel counts eight editions by 1500 and five more by 1520, and a second surge of sixteen editions between 1576 and 1670, when George Mora estimated that "between thirty and fifty thousand copies" were distributed ([Broedel, 2003](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/handle/20.500.12657/35002/341393.pdf)). Its influence is easier to assert than to measure. Mackay credits it with a "major role in the savage efforts undertaken to stamp out witchcraft" ([Mackay, 2009](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805217/47875/excerpt/9780521747875_excerpt.pdf)); Broedel, more cautiously, argues that the book was "not generally representative of late medieval learned thinking" but became the authority that later writers, sceptics included, argued against, so that by the late sixteenth century "authors no longer bothered to argue about what witchcraft was; instead, they argued over whether it existed" ([Broedel, 2003](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/handle/20.500.12657/35002/341393.pdf)).

## How many: the estimates and what they rest on

### The dataset and the consensus

No complete count exists, because records were lost or never kept. Leeson and Russ assembled data covering "more than 43,000 people tried for witchcraft across 21 European countries" over five and a half centuries. They report that "The consensus among historians is that 100,000–110,000 Europeans were tried for witchcraft between c. 1400 and c. 1750, about half of them executed", that 60% of the trials in their data fell between 1560 and 1630, that more than half took place within about 300 miles (some 480 kilometres) of Strasbourg, and that "Spain, Italy and Portugal, on other hand, largely avoided witch trials" ([Leeson and Russ, 2018](https://www.peterleeson.com/Witch_Trials.pdf)). ### Three warnings about these numbers

**Deaths recorded are not deaths.** The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft at the University of Edinburgh identified 3,837 people accused in Scotland between 1563 and 1736, 3,212 of them named; of the named, the sentence is known for only 305, of whom 205 were sentenced to death, 52 acquitted and the rest banished, outlawed or otherwise punished. That "seems to suggest that 67%, two-thirds, were executed", but the authors warn the figure "is probably not very accurate" because the 305 cases are not typical ([Survey of Scottish Witchcraft, Introduction](https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/faq/)). The 190 Scottish deaths in the Leeson and Russ table and the Survey's two-thirds are both partial; neither is the true total.

**Local research raises local counts.** For Catalonia, the historian Pau Castell told the newspaper Ara that he had "podido poner nombres y apellidos a cerca de un millar de mujeres y también algunos hombres" (put names to about a thousand women and some men) accused of the crime ([Ara, 23 January 2022](https://es.ara.cat/politica/parlament-disculpa-brujas-catalanas_1_4248823.html)), far above the single death the Leeson and Russ table records for all of Spain. The public broadcaster 3Cat wrote of "més de mil dones assassinades" (more than a thousand women killed) between the fifteenth and seventeenth centuries ([3Cat, 24 January 2022](https://www.3cat.cat/3catinfo/el-parlament-indultara-les-bruixes-assassinades-en-un-feminicidi-institucionalitzat/noticia/3141448/)), while the resolution the Catalan Parliament adopted spoke of the names of "mais de 700 mulheres" (more than 700 women) processed, tortured and executed, according to the Portuguese newspaper Diário de Notícias ([Diário de Notícias, 27 January 2022](https://www.dn.pt/internacional/catalunha-perdoa-centenas-de-bruxas-torturadas-e-executadas-durante-tres-seculos-14532804.html)). The three figures count different things: accused, killed and named.

**Inflated figures persist.** The Edinburgh survey notes that older accounts gave Scotland 4,500 or 30,000 witches, "based on speculation rather than detailed research", and that "a figure of 9 million witches executed in Europe is sometimes given, when most scholars agree that it was about 60,000"; its authors add, "We think that 3,837 people accused of witchcraft is a lot" ([Survey of Scottish Witchcraft](https://witches.hca.ed.ac.uk/faq/)). The 60,000 of the Edinburgh team and the "about half" of 100,000 to 110,000 cited by Leeson and Russ are compatible orders of magnitude, not identical estimates. When Scotland's First Minister apologized in 2022 she spoke of "more than 4,000 people in Scotland" accused ([Scottish Government, First Minister's statement, 8 March 2022](https://www.gov.scot/publications/international-womens-day-2022-first-ministers-statement-8-march-2022/)), a rounder number than the survey's.

## Inside a trial: Bamberg, 1628

Numbers hide how the process worked. The minutes of the trial of Johannes Junius, burgomaster of the episcopal city of Bamberg, and a letter he smuggled to his daughter, both translated by the historian George Lincoln Burr from manuscripts in the Bamberg library, show it from both sides ([Burr, The Witch Persecutions, University of Pennsylvania, 1898 to 1912, vol. 3, no. 4, Hanover Historical Texts Project](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/bamberg.html)).

On 28 June 1628 Junius, aged 55, said he was "wholly innocent". He was confronted with witnesses who swore they had seen him at witches' gatherings and was told "that his accomplices had confessed against him". On 30 June he was tortured with thumbscrews, leg screws and the strappado, and maintained that he had never renounced God. On 5 July, "without torture, but with urgent persuasions", he began to confess a pact with the Devil, a demonic baptism and the names of people he had seen at the sabbath. On 6 August his confession was read back and ratified, and Burr notes that he was burned at the stake ([Burr, Bamberg](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/bamberg.html)).

His letter of 24 July 1628 tells what the minutes leave out: "Innocent have I come into prison, innocent have I been tortured, innocent must I die. For whoever comes into the witch prison must become a witch or be tortured until he invents something out of his head". He writes that the executioner urged him to "confess something, whether it be true or not", and describes how the names were obtained: told to say whom he had seen, he said he had recognized no one, until the interrogator said, "Take one street after another; begin at the market, go out on one street and back on the next", and he "had to name eight persons" on one street alone. In the margin he added: "six have confessed against me at once", all of them "false, through compulsion, as they have all told me" ([Burr, Bamberg](https://history.hanover.edu/texts/bamberg.html)).

The document shows the cascade that the Malleus procedure made possible: every confession had to contain accomplices, and every accomplice named became a new defendant whose confession would name others. The chain was produced by the court; it needed no belief from the person who supplied the names.

## Why the hunts rose and fell: competing explanations

Historians and economists offer several explanations, and the strength of each depends on the data used.

**Religious competition.** Leeson and Russ argue that the trials "reflected non-price competition between the Catholic and Protestant churches for religious market share in confessionally contested parts of Christendom"; in their data, "More intense religious-market contestation led to more intense witch-trial activity", while weather, income and state capacity were not important by comparison ([Leeson and Russ, 2018](https://www.peterleeson.com/Witch_Trials.pdf)). Germany, the Reformation's birthplace, alone accounts for 38% of the persons in their dataset.

**Bad weather and scapegoats.** The hypothesis they test against, associated with the historian Wolfgang Behringer, links hunts to the cold and harvest failures of the Little Ice Age; Leeson and Russ describe Emily Oster's earlier test on 11 European regions between 1520 and 1770, which "finds support for it", and report that when they re-estimate it with Oster's own weather data and their larger count of trials, the weather variable is not statistically significant while their measure of Catholic-Protestant conflict is ([Leeson and Russ, 2018](https://www.peterleeson.com/Witch_Trials.pdf)). <Claim status="disputed" by="by Leeson and Russ against the weather hypothesis">Which factor mattered most</Claim> is contested, and the datasets differ in coverage.

**Who held the courts.** In Catalonia, the historian Agustí Alcoberro told Ara that convictions were frequent because they were pronounced by local courts, far from larger institutions and from the Inquisition, which "tuvo una posición escéptica respecto a la existencia de la brujería" (took a sceptical position on whether witchcraft existed), especially from 1614; trials declined after 1622, when local cases had to go to the Audiència, the high court ([Ara, 2022](https://es.ara.cat/politica/parlament-disculpa-brujas-catalanas_1_4248823.html)). He also described a 1424 law in the Pyrenean valley of Àneu as the oldest in Europe to punish witchcraft as a sect led by the Devil, whereas the magazine Sàpiens, cited by Diário de Notícias, dates the region's first action against witchcraft to 1471 ([Diário de Notícias, 2022](https://www.dn.pt/internacional/catalunha-perdoa-centenas-de-bruxas-torturadas-e-executadas-durante-tres-seculos-14532804.html)).

**The book as authority.** Broedel's explanation for the long life of the witch theory is textual: before the Malleus, writers on witches cited almost no contemporary authority, and its ready availability "created a certain uniformity of discourse in subsequent witchcraft debate" ([Broedel, 2003](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/handle/20.500.12657/35002/341393.pdf)).

These accounts are not exclusive. What they share is that the hunts were organized by institutions, and that they declined when institutions changed their rules of evidence, as the next case shows.

## Salem, 1692 to 2022

### The crisis

The Salem Witch Trials Documentary Archive at the University of Virginia, built on the 1977 transcription *The Salem Witchcraft Papers* and on digitized manuscripts from the Massachusetts State Archives, the Peabody Essex Museum and other collections, is the primary source here ([Benjamin Ray, The Salem Witch Trials Digital Archive: How and Why, 2022](https://salem.lib.virginia.edu/intro.html)). Its director summarizes: the trials began in late February 1692 and lasted until the end of May 1693; accusations spread across twenty-four communities; "At least 155 people were arrested and jailed, thirty were convicted, nineteen were executed by hanging, one man was crushed to death with stones, and five more died in prison due to harsh conditions" ([Ray, 2022](https://salem.lib.virginia.edu/intro.html)). The archive's own overview gives "Over 160 people" accused and an end in April 1693 ([Salem Witch Trials Documentary Archive, Overview](https://salem.lib.virginia.edu/overview.html)), and Ray's interactive map shows "the locations of the 151 people accused" ([Ray, 2022](https://salem.lib.virginia.edu/intro.html)): the counts differ with the definition of accused and the documents included.

The records begin with arrests at Salem Village. On 1 March 1692, by the calendar then used in England (1691/92 in the records), Tituba, described as "an Indian Woman", was brought before the magistrates ([Salem Witchcraft Papers, no. 125: Tituba](https://salem.lib.virginia.edu/n125.html)). The archive's index of case files records the executions: one on 10 June 1692, five on 19 July, five on 19 August and eight on 22 September, nineteen in all, and lists Giles Corey as "Pressed to Death, September 16, 1692" ([Salem Witchcraft Papers, index](https://salem.lib.virginia.edu/category/swp.html)), a date that differs from the 19 September often given elsewhere; deaths in prison listed in the same index include Sarah Osborne on 10 May 1692 and Roger Toothaker on 16 June 1692.

Two features made Salem what it was. First, the special Court of Oyer and Terminer accepted *spectral evidence*: testimony by the afflicted that the shape of the accused had tormented them. Governor William Phips, writing to London in February 1693, described the method: when the suspects "looked upon them" the afflicted "instantly fell to the ground in strange agonies and grievous torments", and on oath declared "that they saw their shape or spectre come from their bodies" ([Salem Witchcraft Papers, no. 163: two letters of Governor Phips](https://salem.lib.virginia.edu/n163.html)). Second, confession saved lives. Ray found that confessors "gradually increased the number of spectral witches they 'saw'" at demonic rituals opposite the minister's house "in order to reinforce their confessions, which would save them from trial, one of the oddities of the Salem court" ([Ray, 2022](https://salem.lib.virginia.edu/intro.html)). As in Bamberg, every confession supplied new names.

Rebecca Nurse, a 71-year-old church member, was the fifth person accused; 39 neighbours signed a petition for her, and the jury first returned a verdict of not guilty, was told to continue deliberating, and returned a guilty verdict; she was hanged on 19 July ([Ray, 2022](https://salem.lib.virginia.edu/intro.html)).

### How it stopped

The end came from changes in the rules of evidence, not from a change in belief in witchcraft. In October 1692 Phips wrote that he had found "that the Devill had taken upon him the name and shape of severall persons who were doubtless inocent and to my certain knowledge of good reputation", and that he had forbidden further commitments and stopped the printing of discourses on either side, "because I saw a likelyhood of kindling an inextinguishable flame" ([Phips to the Privy Council, 12 October 1692](https://salem.lib.virginia.edu/n163.html)). In February 1693 he reported that once Increase Mather and other ministers had judged that "the Devill might afflict in the shape of an innocent person and that the look and touch of the suspected persons was not sufficient proofe", a new Superior Court sat with an altered method, and "all that were brought to tryall to the number of fifety two, were cleared saving three"; he reprieved those three and five others ([Phips to the Earl of Nottingham, 21 February 1692/3](https://salem.lib.virginia.edu/n163.html)).

Increase Mather's *Cases of Conscience*, written in October 1692, contains the line usually quoted as Salem's turning point, "It were better that ten suspected Witches should escape, than that one innocent Person should be Condemned"; the same paragraph also says that where credible human witnesses, not spectres, testify to dealings with the Devil, "it is proof enough" that the accused "ought to be exterminated from amongst men" ([Increase Mather, Cases of Conscience, London edition of 1693, reprinted 1862, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/28513/pg28513.txt)). Mather objected to the evidence, not to the crime.

### Repentance and reversal

On 14 January 1697, a province-wide day of prayer and fasting, the minister Samuel Willard read in his Boston church a statement by Samuel Sewall, one of the judges ([Salem Witch Trials Documentary Archive, Samuel Sewall](https://salem.lib.virginia.edu/people/sewall.html)), in which Sewall "Desires to take the Blame and Shame of it, Asking pardon of Men" ([Sewall, Diary, in the Salem archive](https://salem.lib.virginia.edu/diaries/sewall_diary.html)). In 1706 Ann Putnam, who had been one of the principal child accusers, was received into full communion at Salem Village after a confession read before the congregation, in which she said she had been made "an instrument for yt accuseing of severall persons of a grievous crime" and that "it was a great delusion of Satan yt deceived me in that sad time" ([Salem Village church record kept by Joseph Green, 25 August 1706](https://salem.lib.virginia.edu/green.html)). She too blamed Satan, not the accusers or the court.

### A medical explanation that did not settle it

In April 1976 the journal Science published Linnda Caporael's paper "Ergotism: the Satan loosed in Salem?", proposing poisoning by a fungus on rye as a cause of the afflicted girls' fits, and in December 1976 it published a second paper on the hypothesis, "Ergotism and the Salem Village witch trials", by Nicholas Spanos and Jack Gottlieb ([PubMed record, Caporael, 1976](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=769159); [PubMed record, Spanos and Gottlieb, 1976](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=795029)). Only the bibliographic records of both papers could be read for this part, so their arguments are not summarized here; the ergot hypothesis and the reasons historians give against it are examined in the [part on dancing plagues and possessed convents](/learn/dancing-plagues-and-possessed-convents). Whatever moved the afflicted, the documents above show that the deaths were produced by a court and its rules of evidence.

## The Inquisition in Brazil, 1591 to 1595: a hunt for other targets

### The visit

Brazil never had a permanent tribunal of the Portuguese Inquisition; the Holy Office acted through visits and local agents. According to the historian Alécio Nunes Fernandes, four visits are known: Bahia, Pernambuco, Itamaracá and Paraíba from 1591 to 1595; Bahia again from 1618 to 1620; the southern captaincies in 1627 and 1628, whose records are believed lost in a shipwreck; and Grão-Pará from 1763 to 1769 ([Revista Pesquisa FAPESP, August 2025](https://revistapesquisa.fapesp.br/segunda-visita-da-inquisicao-a-bahia-teve-cinco-vezes-mais-denuncias-do-que-se-conhecia)).

The first visit is documented in the published books of confessions and denunciations. In his 1922 preface to the confessions of Bahia, the historian Capistrano de Abreu sets out the chronology from the documents: the licentiate Heitor Furtado de Mendonça was appointed visitor on 2 March 1591, arrived in Salvador with the governor Francisco de Sousa on 9 June, published his commission on 28 July granting thirty days of grace for voluntary confession in the city, received the first confession on 29 July, and left for Pernambuco on 2 September 1593; the visitation's books were nine, three of confessions, four of denunciations and two of ratifications, some of them missing when he wrote ([Primeira Visitação do Santo Officio ás partes do Brasil: Confissões da Bahia 1591-92, São Paulo, 1922, scan by the University of Toronto](https://archive.org/download/primeiravisita00sociuoft/primeiravisita00sociuoft_djvu.txt)). The denunciations of Pernambuco for 1593 to 1595 were published separately ([Primeira Visitação: Denunciações de Pernambuco, scan](https://archive.org/download/primeira-visitacao-do-santo-oficio-as-partes-do-brasil-denunciacoes-de-pernambuco_202212/Primeira%20Visita%C3%A7%C3%A3o%20do%20Santo%20Of%C3%ADcio%20as%20Partes%20do%20Brasil%20-%20Denuncia%C3%A7%C3%B5es%20de%20Pernambuco_djvu.txt)).

### What it prosecuted

The visit was a hunt, but not a witch hunt. Fernandes studied the 240 processes the visitor's tribunal opened; he counts 67 of the accused as acquitted, not condemned or given light penalties, "27,91% dos processados" (27.91% of the accused) ([Fernandes, A defesa dos réus, doctoral thesis, Universidade de Brasília, 2020](https://repositorio.unb.br/bitstream/10482/40938/3/2020_Al%c3%a9cioNunesFernandes.pdf)). As Revista Pesquisa FAPESP summarizes his book, about 900 denunciations were recorded; among the processes the largest groups were heretical propositions (68), blasphemy (29), sodomy (24) and "práticas gentílicas" (gentile practices, meaning Indigenous and African rituals) (18), while only 17 concerned Jewish practices, although Judaism led the denunciations. "Se a intenção era perseguir cristãos-novos, na prática os cristãos-velhos foram muito mais processados" (if the intention was to persecute New Christians, in practice Old Christians were prosecuted far more), Fernandes told the magazine ([Revista Pesquisa FAPESP, 2025](https://revistapesquisa.fapesp.br/segunda-visita-da-inquisicao-a-bahia-teve-cinco-vezes-mais-denuncias-do-que-se-conhecia)).

### Sorcery before the visitor

Accusations of sorcery did reach the visitor. Capistrano wrote that none of the women reputed to be sorceresses came forward to confess, and that three were named most insistently in the denunciations, known by nicknames: Isabel Rodrigues, "Boca Torta"; Antonia Fernandes, "Nóbrega"; and Maria Gonçalves, "Arde-lhe-o-rabo" ([Confissões da Bahia, 1922](https://archive.org/download/primeiravisita00sociuoft/primeiravisita00sociuoft_djvu.txt)). Fernandes reconstructed Maria Gonçalves Cajada's process from the Torre do Tombo file: she had been exiled to Brazil for setting fire to houses and throwing a jug at a judge, was denounced by at least seven witnesses, was left free while ill, and was sentenced to appear at a public auto with the *carocha*, the mitre "pena usual para feiticeiras" (usual penalty for sorceresses), to spiritual penances and to be shipped back to Portugal to live with her husband; the judges took into account that her spells had not been shown to work and that she said she had lied "a fim de adquirir dinheiro" (to get money) ([Fernandes, 2020](https://repositorio.unb.br/bitstream/10482/40938/3/2020_Al%c3%a9cioNunesFernandes.pdf)). The Inquisition's General Council in Lisbon went further, writing on the file that "parece que tudo são embustes e enganos as culpas desta Ré" (it seems that the defendant's offences are all tricks and deceptions) and that the case belonged to the bishop's court rather than to the Inquisition.

This matches the wider Portuguese pattern. The historians Juliana Torres Rodrigues Pereira and Marcus Vinícius Reis write that, by consensus among historians of the Portuguese Holy Office, it concentrated on New Christians suspected of secret Judaism and that "A feitiçaria, ao contrário, não foi um delito privilegiado pela vigilância inquisitorial" (sorcery, on the contrary, was not a crime favoured by inquisitorial vigilance); José Pedro Paiva's study of Portugal between 1600 and 1774 bears the title *Bruxaria e superstição num país sem caça às bruxas* (witchcraft and superstition in a country without a witch hunt) and attributes the difference partly to the scepticism of Portuguese elites about demonic power ([Pereira and Reis, Revista Cantareira 24, 2016](https://periodicos.uff.br/cantareira/article/download/27842/16249/96266)). Reis, interviewed by FAPESP, adds that clients who had bought spells sometimes denounced the sorceress first, hoping to obtain light penalties or pardon ([Revista Pesquisa FAPESP, 2025](https://revistapesquisa.fapesp.br/segunda-visita-da-inquisicao-a-bahia-teve-cinco-vezes-mais-denuncias-do-que-se-conhecia)). The Brazilian visits were persecution by denunciation, with time-of-grace confessions and neighbours informing on neighbours, but the folk devil of the Iberian tribunals was the New Christian, the sodomite and the heretic more than the witch.

## Moral panic: Cohen and the seaside, 1964

### The events and the coverage

Cohen's case was small. Goode and Ben-Yehuda reconstruct it from his book: on Easter Sunday 1964 at Clacton, an English seaside resort, bored young people scuffled, windows of a dance hall were smashed and beach huts damaged; "The damage, perhaps £500 in value"; the police arrested nearly one hundred people. The next day every national newspaper except The Times led with the story, with headlines such as "Day of Terror by Scooter Groups" in the Daily Telegraph and "Wild Ones Invade Seaside" in the Daily Mirror ([Goode and Ben-Yehuda, 2009](https://media.wiley.com/product_data/excerpt/47/14051893/1405189347.pdf)). Cohen documented the distortions: reports that "boats" were overturned when one was; the windows of "all" the dance halls smashed in a town that had one; a youth's boast that he would pay his fine by cheque, repeated for four years as proof of affluent hooligans, from a young man who had no bank account; offenders described as an invasion from London on motorbikes when most were local and on foot; and violent crime when only a tenth were charged with violence ([Goode and Ben-Yehuda, 2009](https://media.wiley.com/product_data/excerpt/47/14051893/1405189347.pdf)).

### The political response, in the record

The House of Commons record shows the escalation Cohen described. On 16 April 1964, asked for emergency legislation, the Home Secretary, Henry Brooke, replied: "I know of no evidence which suggests that existing powers are inadequate to enable such behaviour to be dealt with" ([Hansard, House of Commons, 16 April 1964, Hooliganism](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1964/apr/16/hooliganism)). After further disturbances at Whitsun, on 23 June he moved the second reading of a Malicious Damage Bill, "the Bill which I said that the Government intended to introduce forthwith" after the disturbances "which broke out at Whitsun at three seaside towns in the South of England" ([Hansard, House of Commons, 23 June 1964, Malicious Damage Bill](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1964/jun/23/malicious-damage-bill)). In the same debate Sir William Teeling, member for Brighton Pavilion, who supported the bill, told the House, "There was practically no damage done at Brighton". The Malicious Damage Act received Royal Assent on 31 July 1964 ([Hansard, Malicious Damage Act 1964](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/acts/malicious-damage-act-1964)).

### Five criteria

Goode and Ben-Yehuda later turned the concept into a checklist. As Critcher summarizes their five "elements or criteria":

| Criterion | Goode and Ben-Yehuda's wording (1994), as quoted by Critcher |
| --- | --- |
| Concern | a "heightened level of concern over the behaviour of a certain group or category" |
| Hostility | "an increased level of hostility" towards the deviants, "collectively designated as the enemy, or an enemy, of respectable society" |
| Consensus | "at least a certain minimal measure of consensus" that "the threat is real, serious and caused by the wrongdoing group members and their behaviour" |
| Disproportionality | "public concern is in excess of what is appropriate if concern were directly proportional to objective harm" |
| Volatility | panics "are by their nature fleeting, subsiding as quickly as they erupt" (Critcher's paraphrase) |

Source: [Critcher, Moral Panic Analysis: Past, Present and Future, Sociology Compass 2(4), 2008, copy hosted by Erasmus University Rotterdam](https://eur.nl/media/123420). Critcher calls this "an attributional model of moral panic because attributes are the defining characteristics", distinct from Cohen's processual model of stages.

### The critics

The concept has been criticized from several sides, and its defenders accept part of the criticism.

**The media changed.** Angela McRobbie and Sarah Thornton argued in 1995 that a plural, fragmented media system made it "impossible to rely on the old models with their stages and cycles, universal media, monolithic societal or hegemonic reactions" ([Critcher, 2008](https://eur.nl/media/123420)). As James Walsh summarizes them, folk devils able to "produce their own media" can now "fight back", rendering moral crusades "much less certain" ([Walsh, International Journal of Cultural Studies, 2020, full text through Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7201200/fullTextXML)). Walsh, writing in 2020, argues that social media cut both ways, also facilitating "division and hostility".

**Disproportionate to what?** Cohen himself, in his 2002 introduction, wrote that the term "implies that societal reaction is disproportionate to the actual seriousness (risk, damage, threat) of the event", and acknowledged the critics' question: "on what grounds is the sociologist's view always correct, rational and justified?" ([Lashmar, 2013](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/19050/3/MoralPanicBloomsburyLashmar%20%2528PL%20Revise%2529.pdf)). Calling a reaction a panic presupposes a judgement about the real size of the threat, which is often what is in dispute.

**Too loose to analyse.** Critcher reports that Yvonne Jewkes found "fundamental flaws" in moral panic analysis while declining to reject it, and that Benjamin Cornwell and Annulla Linders, studying the American reaction to lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD), concluded the concept "is so laden with ontological and methodological difficulties as to render it virtually useless as an analytical guiding light" ([Critcher, 2008](https://eur.nl/media/123420)).

**Folk devils answer back.** Mary de Young, studying the American day-care abuse scare of the 1980s, argued that "each of Cohen's stages is contestable", since accused day-care workers organized and used publicity to counter their persecution ([Lashmar, 2013](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/19050/3/MoralPanicBloomsburyLashmar%20%2528PL%20Revise%2529.pdf)). That scare is the subject of the [next part](/learn/satanic-panic-false-memories-and-the-escola-base-case), along with the opposite critique: that the label "panic" has also been used to dismiss real abuse.

Paul Lashmar, a journalist turned academic, adds a practitioner's objection: the seaside clashes of 1964 to 1966 remain the iconic images of British youth culture, which sits uneasily with a story in which the conflict was minimal and wholly blown out of proportion ([Lashmar, 2013](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/19050/3/MoralPanicBloomsburyLashmar%20%2528PL%20Revise%2529.pdf)).

## A Brazilian case with official findings: Queermuseu, 2017

The criteria can be tested on a recent Brazilian episode in which public prosecutors issued findings. On 10 September 2017 the sponsors closed the exhibition "Queermuseu: Cartografias da Diferença na Arte Brasileira" at Santander Cultural in Porto Alegre ahead of schedule, as the federal prosecutors' recommendation records ([Ministério Público Federal, Recomendação PRDC/RS nº 21/2017, 28 September 2017](https://www.conjur.com.br/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/recomendacao-mpf-rs-reabertura-mostra.pdf)). The bank's statement, as quoted by the newspaper Gazeta do Povo, said: "Entendemos que algumas das obras da exposição Queermuseu desrespeitavam símbolos, crenças e pessoas, o que não está em linha com a nossa visão de mundo" (we understand that some of the works disrespected symbols, beliefs and people, which is not in line with our view of the world) ([Gazeta do Povo, 29 September 2017](https://www.gazetadopovo.com.br/ideias/mp-cita-nazismo-e-esquece-insulto-contra-religiao-em-pedido-de-reabertura-do-queermuseu-9yu2ls65k2kooikkkf68oxlfz)).

Online accusations had charged the show with promoting paedophilia. The state prosecutor for children and youth in Porto Alegre, Júlio Almeida, opened an inquiry, visited the exhibition more than once and, according to the Ministério Público do Rio Grande do Sul (MPRS), "reafirma que não há casos de crime de pedofilia na referida mostra" (reaffirms that there are no cases of the crime of paedophilia in the exhibition); he wrote that the images "não contém criança ou adolescente na cena captada ou produzida" (contain no child or adolescent in the scene) and that some images circulating on social networks "não fazem parte da exposição" (are not part of the exhibition), while recommending measures on children's access and leaving moral, religious and artistic questions outside his remit ([MPRS, 15 September 2017](https://www.mprs.mp.br/noticias/infancia/45185/)). On 28 September the federal prosecutor Fabiano de Moraes recommended reopening, writing that the abrupt closure "possuem um impacto negativo tanto em relação à liberdade artística, quanto em relação ao respeito à diversidade" (had a negative impact on artistic freedom and on respect for diversity) ([Ministério Público Federal, 2017](https://www.conjur.com.br/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/recomendacao-mpf-rs-reabertura-mostra.pdf)).

The critics' side was also argued in public. Gazeta do Povo faulted the federal recommendation for ignoring the communion wafers inscribed with sexual words, which it argued could fall under article 208 of the Penal Code, "vilipendiar publicamente ato ou objeto de culto religioso" (publicly vilifying an act or object of religious worship), and for comparing the closure with the Nazi destruction of so-called degenerate art ([Gazeta do Povo, 2017](https://www.gazetadopovo.com.br/ideias/mp-cita-nazismo-e-esquece-insulto-contra-religiao-em-pedido-de-reabertura-do-queermuseu-9yu2ls65k2kooikkkf68oxlfz)). On Goode and Ben-Yehuda's criteria, <Claim status="established" by="by the MPRS finding of 15 September 2017">the accusation of a crime against children was not borne out by the prosecutors who examined it</Claim>, and images from outside the show were circulated as if part of it; whether offence to religious symbols justified the closure is a dispute of values that prosecutors declined to settle, and so is the label of moral panic itself.

## Witch hunts now

Witch hunts are not only history. On 12 July 2021 the United Nations Human Rights Council adopted, without a vote, resolution 47/8 on the elimination of harmful practices related to accusations of witchcraft and ritual attacks, expressing concern that they "have resulted in various forms of violence, including killings, mutilation, burning", especially against women, children, older persons, persons with disabilities and persons with albinism, and that "these forms of violence are often committed with impunity" ([United Nations Human Rights Council, resolution 47/8, 12 July 2021](https://documents.un.org/api/symbol/access?s=A/HRC/RES/47/8&l=en&t=pdf)). In India, <Claim status="reported" by="by ETV Bharat, citing the National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB)">72 killings motivated by witchcraft accusations were registered nationwide in 2023, 22 of them in the state of Jharkhand</Claim>; a senior Jharkhand police officer answered that the report is from 2023 and that "An awareness campaign has been launched vigorously across the state" ([ETV Bharat, October 2025](https://www.etvbharat.com/en/bharat/jharkhands-witch-hunting-crisis-100-per-cent-rise-in-killings-despite-awareness-campaigns-enn25101503381)). Scotland's First Minister made the same link when she apologized in 2022: "There are parts of our world where, even today, women and girls face persecution and sometimes death because they have been accused of witchcraft" ([Scottish Government, 2022](https://www.gov.scot/publications/international-womens-day-2022-first-ministers-statement-8-march-2022/)). In Brazil, the 2014 lynching of a woman in Guarujá after a rumour that she kidnapped children for black magic is examined in the [part on rumour, panic and lynching](/learn/rumour-panic-and-lynching).

Official reckonings with the old hunts have multiplied. Scotland's First Minister extended in 2022 "a formal, posthumous apology to all those accused, convicted, vilified or executed under the Witchcraft Act 1563", partly in response to public petition PE1855 ([Scottish Government, 2022](https://www.gov.scot/publications/international-womens-day-2022-first-ministers-statement-8-march-2022/); [Scottish Government, FOI release, 28 April 2026](https://www.gov.scot/publications/foi-202600509137/)). On 26 January 2022 the Parliament of Catalonia approved, by 114 votes to 14 with 6 abstentions, a resolution recognizing the women condemned for witchcraft as "vítimas de perseguição misógina" (victims of misogynist persecution) and urging municipalities to name streets after them, with right-wing and far-right deputies opposed ([Diário de Notícias, 2022](https://www.dn.pt/internacional/catalunha-perdoa-centenas-de-bruxas-torturadas-e-executadas-durante-tres-seculos-14532804.html)); Ara noted that such resolutions are political statements without legal effect ([Ara, 2022](https://es.ara.cat/politica/parlament-disculpa-brujas-catalanas_1_4248823.html)).

## What to remember

- Medieval church authorities doubted witches existed; the doctrine of a Devil-worshipping sect took hold in the fifteenth century, carried by the bull of 1484 and the Malleus Maleficarum, whose date, authorship and Cologne endorsement are still disputed.
- The Malleus made confession the goal and taught deceptive promises, prolonged torture and the demand for accomplices; Bamberg's records show the chain of names this produced.
- About 100,000 to 110,000 trials and roughly 50,000 to 60,000 executions is the range historians give; recorded deaths understate, local research raises counts, and the "nine million" figure has no basis.
- Salem's 19 hangings ended when the rules of evidence changed, not belief in witchcraft; Massachusetts cleared the last convicted woman in 2022 and in 2026 was considering earlier victims.
- The Portuguese Inquisition's visit to Bahia and Pernambuco (1591 to 1595) prosecuted heresy, blasphemy, sodomy and Judaism far more than sorcery, and Lisbon called one sorcery case mere trickery.
- Cohen's moral panic names the same reaction without the gallows; Goode and Ben-Yehuda's five criteria made it testable, and its critics question disproportionality, the media model and the concept's looseness.

<ReaderQuestions>

- If a court requires a confession and rewards those who name others, how much of the resulting "evidence" says anything about the accused rather than about the procedure?
- The 1711 act blamed the evil spirits acting on the accusers and shielded every official: what does an official reversal owe the victims beyond clearing their names?
- Estimates of the European dead range from tens of thousands to a myth of millions: what should a reader ask before repeating any of them?
- Calling a reaction a moral panic implies it is out of proportion to the threat: who should judge proportion when the size of the threat is itself the dispute?
- When public prosecutors find no crime but part of the public still sees an offence to its values, is the label "moral panic" a finding or a position?

</ReaderQuestions>
