Not every influence operation is run by a ministry or an intelligence service. Some are bought. A political party, a government, a lobby or a company hires a firm, and the firm supplies fake accounts, borrowed voices, harvested data or a bulk messaging service, with the client's name kept off the work. Meta has called this "IO as a service" and says it offers "deniability to their customers" ([Meta, 2021](https://about.fb.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/IO-Threat-Report-May-20-2021.pdf)). This part of the [series](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign), after [state campaigns on the record](/learn/state-influence-campaigns-on-the-record), follows the firms through the documents: academic inventories, platform takedown reports, regulators' findings, a parliamentary report, a court judgment in Brazil and an undercover investigation, each with the firm's reply where one was published. After reading, you can tell what was established from what was claimed, and what remains open about whether the product works.

## Counting a market: the Oxford inventories

Researchers at the Oxford Internet Institute (OII) began counting in 2017. Samantha Bradshaw and Philip Howard defined "cyber troops" as "government, military or political-party teams committed to manipulating public opinion over social media", and found them in 28 countries ([Bradshaw and Howard, 2017](https://demtech.oii.ox.ac.uk//wp-content/uploads/sites/12/2017/07/Troops-Trolls-and-Troublemakers.pdf)). By the 2019 inventory the count was 70 countries, and in 25 of them the authors found "evidence of state actors working with private companies or strategic communication firms who offer computational propaganda as a service" ([Bradshaw and Howard, 2019](https://demtech.oii.ox.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/sites/12/2020/10/CyberTroop-Report19_V2NOV.pdf)).

The 2020 inventory, published in January 2021 by Bradshaw, Hannah Bailey and Howard, counted 81 countries and reported that <Claim status="reported" by="by the Oxford researchers">"we found private firms operating in forty-eight countries deploying computational propaganda on behalf of a political actor"</Claim>, with "more than 65 firms offering computational propaganda as a service" since 2018 and "almost US $60 million" spent on hiring them since 2009 ([Bradshaw, Bailey and Howard, 2021](https://demtech.oii.ox.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/sites/8/2021/02/CyberTroop-Report20-Draft9.pdf)). The firms, they write, "often create sock puppet accounts, identify audiences for micro-targeting, or use bot or other amplification strategies to prompt the trending of certain political messages".

**What the numbers rest on.** The method is a systematic content analysis of news articles, a review of secondary literature, country case studies and expert consultations; the authors name media bias and language as the two main limitations, and say the money figures "are only from confirmed reports: we suspect the actual amount is much higher" ([Bradshaw, Bailey and Howard, 2021](https://demtech.oii.ox.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/sites/8/2021/02/CyberTroop-Report20-Draft9.pdf)). The inventory therefore counts what journalists and platforms exposed, not the market itself: a country with a weak press, or a firm that was never caught, does not appear. Brazil is listed among the 81 countries.

## What the platforms found: influence operations for hire

Meta's review of its takedowns from 2017 to 2020 says it removed influence operations run by "media, marketing and public relations companies", in Myanmar, the United States, the Philippines, Ukraine, the United Arab Emirates and Egypt, some for domestic political interests and some selling services "to paying clients both at home and abroad" ([Meta, The State of Influence Operations 2017-2020, May 2021](https://about.fb.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/IO-Threat-Report-May-20-2021.pdf)). It added a judgement about the business: firms working across many regions may lack the local context to be convincing, risk losing their assets when caught, and "this does not make for a sustainable business model in the long run". The cases below are firms that platforms named.

**Archimedes Group (Israel, May 2019).** Meta removed a network aimed at Nigeria, Senegal, Togo, Angola, Niger and Tunisia, with some activity in Latin America and Southeast Asia, and said <Claim status="attributed" by="by Meta">"some of this activity was linked to an Israeli commercial entity, Archimedes Group"</Claim>; the firm and its subsidiaries were banned and sent a cease-and-desist letter ([Meta, 16 May 2019](https://about.fb.com/news/2019/05/removing-coordinated-inauthentic-behavior-from-israel/)). The network had spent around $812,000 on Facebook ads, "paid for in Brazilian reals, Israeli shekel, and US dollars", from December 2012 to April 2019. No statement by the firm was found in the sources read.

**CLS Strategies (United States, September 2020).** Meta removed 46 Facebook accounts, 41 Pages and 24 Instagram accounts "linked to US-based strategic communications firm CLS Strategies", focused on Venezuela, Mexico and Bolivia ([Meta, 1 September 2020](https://about.fb.com/news/2020/09/august-2020-cib-report/)). Bolivia's interim government said it had hired the firm in December 2019 for lobbying "in search of backing for Bolivian democracy" and had not asked it for any other service, and Meta's head of security policy, Nathaniel Gleicher, wrote that the case "continues a trend we have seen of PR firms around the world making a business out of this type of deception" ([Stabroek News, from Reuters, 4 September 2020](https://stabroeknews.com/2020/09/04/news/world/bolivia-says-it-hired-u-s-lobbying-firm-linked-by-facebook-to-fake-news)). A published reply by CLS Strategies was not found in the sources read.

**Rally Forge (United States, October 2020).** After a Washington Post investigation, Facebook permanently banned an Arizona marketing firm that, it concluded, was "working on behalf of Turning Point USA"; it removed 200 accounts, 55 Pages and 76 Instagram accounts, many operated by teenagers paid to post from a shared document, and did not penalize the client organization, saying it could not determine what its leaders knew ([The Washington Post, via the Anchorage Daily News, 8 October 2020](https://www.adn.com/nation-world/2020/10/08/facebook-bans-marketing-firm-running-troll-farm-for-pro-trump-youth-group/)). Turning Point Action, the affiliate that financed the effort, called the comparison with a troll farm a "gross mischaracterization" and said in a statement: "This is sincere political activism conducted by real people who passionately hold the beliefs they describe online, not an anonymous troll farm in Russia". Philip Howard of the OII objected that "If, once exposed, there are no consequences, others will try it too"; Gleicher replied that the company had to anchor enforcement in "evidence we can observe on the platform" and called on regulators to set standards about "what is acceptable advocacy and what is deception in politics".

**Fazze (registered in the United Kingdom, operating from Russia, August 2021).** Meta removed 65 Facebook accounts and 243 Instagram accounts linked to Fazze, "a subsidiary of a UK-registered marketing firm, whose operations were primarily conducted from Russia", and banned it ([Meta, 10 August 2021](https://about.fb.com/news/2021/08/july-2021-coordinated-inauthentic-behavior-report/)). The network offered influencers money to post content disparaging the Pfizer and AstraZeneca vaccines, for an unknown client; the offer urged them not to say they were paid, and the ploy "backfired when influencers in Germany and France exposed the network's offer", the Associated Press reported, adding that messages seeking comment from the company were not returned ([Associated Press, via WBFF, 10 August 2021](https://foxbaltimore.com/news/coronavirus/facebook-bans-firm-behind-pfizer-astrazeneca-smear-campaign)).

**STOIC (Israel, May 2024).** The Tel Aviv political marketing firm whose network Meta and OpenAI removed, and whose reported government contract Israel's Ministry of Diaspora Affairs denies, is set out in [the state campaigns part](/learn/state-influence-campaigns-on-the-record).

**Comm&Sense (the Philippines, June 2025).** OpenAI banned ChatGPT accounts that generated bulk short comments in English and Taglish for TikTok and Facebook, praising President Marcos, whom the report calls Bongbong Marcos, and criticizing Vice-President Sara Duterte, and wrote that <Claim status="attributed" by="by OpenAI">"This activity was connected to Comm&Sense Inc, a commercial marketing company in the Philippines"</Claim> ([OpenAI, June 2025](https://cdn.openai.com/threat-intelligence-reports/5f73af09-a3a3-4a55-992e-069237681620/disrupting-malicious-uses-of-ai-june-2025.pdf)). The operators also used the model to write sales pitches for the campaign, a mix OpenAI called "consistent with a PR firm working on behalf of multiple clients"; none of the comments it found online received more than single-digit replies, likes or shares, and it rated the operation category 2 of 6 on the Breakout Scale. The Philippine Star reported the findings ([The Philippine Star, 10 June 2025](https://www.philstar.com/headlines/2025/06/10/2449403/ai-used-political-influence-campaign-philippines-report)); a reply by the company was not found in the sources read.

## Cambridge Analytica: what the regulators established

Cambridge Analytica described itself as a "data-science consultancy and marketing agency" ([FTC, 2019](https://search.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2019/12/commission-issues-opinion-cambridge-analytica-case)); with its parent SCL Elections it worked for political campaigns, among them Donald Trump's in 2016, and both ceased trading in 2018 ([Al Jazeera, from Reuters, 2020](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2020/9/25/uk-bans-ex-cambridge-analytica-boss-for-unethical-services)). The documents below show what was proved, what was settled without admission, and what was found to be exaggerated.

**The data.** On 4 April 2018 Facebook said that the information of up to 87 million people, mostly in the United States, "may have been improperly shared with Cambridge Analytica" ([Facebook, 4 April 2018](https://about.fb.com/news/2018/04/restricting-data-access/)). The United Kingdom's Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) later put the number of app users at some 300,000 worldwide, with up to 87 million affected through their friends, as Facebook estimated, and at least one million in the United Kingdom ([ICO letter to the Digital, Culture, Media and Sport Committee, 2 October 2020](https://ico.org.uk/media2/migrated/2618383/20201002_ico-o-ed-l-rtl-0181_to-julian-knight-mp.pdf)).

**The United States: an order by default.** The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) alleged that the app's users were told "we will NOT download your name or any other identifiable information", while it collected Facebook identifiers from at least 250,000 users and data on 50 million to 65 million of their friends; the company's chief executive, Alexander Nix, and the app developer, Aleksandr Kogan, signed settlements, and Cambridge Analytica, bankrupt since May 2018, did not answer the complaint ([FTC, Business Blog, 6 December 2019](https://search.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2019/12/commission-issues-opinion-cambridge-analytica-case)). The Commission therefore issued an opinion finding that <Claim status="established" by="by the FTC, on the company's default">Cambridge Analytica violated the FTC Act by deceiving consumers</Claim> and ordered it to delete the data; the Commission's own release says the opinion, adopted by a vote of 5 to 0, also found that the company deceived consumers about its participation in the EU-U.S. Privacy Shield ([FTC, press release, 6 December 2019](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2019/12/ftc-issues-opinion-order-against-cambridge-analytica-deceiving-consumers-about-collection-facebook); [FTC, case page](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/cases-proceedings/182-3107-cambridge-analytica-llc-matter)). The finding concerns deception about data collection; it says nothing about whether the targeting worked.

**The United Kingdom: a fine settled without admission.** The ICO's report to Parliament of 6 November 2018 recorded a penalty on Facebook of "the maximum monetary penalty of £500,000 available under the previous data protection law" ([ICO, 2018](https://ico.org.uk/media2/migrated/2260271/investigation-into-the-use-of-data-analytics-in-political-campaigns-final-20181105.pdf)). Facebook appealed, and in October 2019 the two sides withdrew their appeals: <Claim status="established" by="by the ICO's statement on the settlement">"Facebook has agreed to pay the £500,000 fine but has made no admission of liability"</Claim> ([Society for Computers and Law, reproducing the ICO statement, 30 October 2019](https://www.scl.org/10716-ico-and-facebook-reach-agreement-regarding-misuse-of-data-in-political-campaigns/)).

**The parliamentary report.** The House of Commons Digital, Culture, Media and Sport (DCMS) Committee published its final report on disinformation on 18 February 2019, describing the company as behaving like "digital gangsters", NPR reported ([NPR, 18 February 2019](https://www.npr.org/2019/02/18/695729829/facebook-has-been-behaving-like-digital-gangsters-u-k-parliament-report-says)). In the Committee's own summary, it found that "It is evident that Facebook intentionally and knowingly violated both data privacy and anti-competition laws", repeated its call for the National Crime Agency to investigate links between SCL Elections and a successor company, Emerdata, and urged a review of self-regulation in the strategic communications industry ([House of Commons, DCMS Committee summary, 2019](https://houseofcommons.shorthandstories.com/dcms-committee-disinformation-fake-news/)). The report itself, HC 1791, describes an inquiry of over 18 months that included an "International Grand Committee" with representatives of eight countries, and says "The big tech companies must not be allowed to expand exponentially, without constraint or proper regulatory oversight" ([DCMS Committee, Final Report, HC 1791, 18 February 2019](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm201719/cmselect/cmcumeds/1791/1791.pdf)). Facebook said it "share[s] the Committee's concerns about false news and election integrity", and its UK public policy manager, Karim Palant, said "We are open to meaningful regulation and support the committee's recommendation for electoral law reform" ([NPR, 18 February 2019](https://www.npr.org/2019/02/18/695729829/facebook-has-been-behaving-like-digital-gangsters-u-k-parliament-report-says)).

**The ICO's final account.** In its letter of 2 October 2020 closing the investigation, the ICO concluded that SCL and Cambridge Analytica had bought large volumes of commercial personal data, "at one estimate over 130 billion data points", and combined it with the Facebook-derived data; that their models were mainly built from "'off the shelf' analytical tools"; and that "there was evidence that their own staff were concerned about some of the public statements the leadership of the company were making about their impact and influence" ([ICO, 2 October 2020](https://ico.org.uk/media2/migrated/2618383/20201002_ico-o-ed-l-rtl-0181_to-julian-knight-mp.pdf)). It found "no further evidence to change my earlier view that SCL/CA were not involved in the EU referendum campaign in the UK", beyond initial enquiries, and "We did not find any additional evidence of Russian involvement" in the company's servers. On effectiveness, the annex says the methods were "in the main, well recognised processes using commonly available technology", that the marketing claim of "Over 5,000 data points per individual on 230 million adult Americans" appeared to be "an exaggeration", and that <Claim status="established" by="by the ICO's investigation">the real-world accuracy of the predictions on people outside the training data "was likely much lower"</Claim>.

**The director.** On 24 September 2020 the Insolvency Service announced that Nix had accepted a seven-year disqualification as a company director; its chief investigator said "SCL Elections had repeatedly offered shady political services to potential clients over a number of years", and the companies had marketed "bribery or honey trap stings" and "voter disengagement campaigns" ([Al Jazeera, from Reuters, 25 September 2020](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2020/9/25/uk-bans-ex-cambridge-analytica-boss-for-unethical-services)). Nix told Reuters he had not admitted any wrongdoing and had not been accused of breaking any law, but had decided to close the matter to "avoid an unnecessary, lengthy and expensive court case".

**Brazil.** In March 2018 the public prosecutor's office of the Federal District opened an inquiry into whether Cambridge Analytica, working in Brazil since 2017 with the consultancy A Ponte Estratégia, used personal data illegally to profile voters ([Consultor Jurídico, 22 March 2018](https://www.conjur.com.br/2018-mar-22/mp-df-investiga-facebook-repassou-dados-brasileiros/)); Ponte said "in 10 years of acting, we have always been guided by ethics and transparency in our business" ([MercoPress, from Reuters, 22 March 2018](https://en.mercopress.com/2018/03/22/cambridge-analytica-partner-in-brazil-under-prosecutor-investigation)). Two days earlier, on 20 March 2018, the Brazilian firm, by then using the name A Ponte Estratégia again and run by André Torretta, had announced it was suspending the partnership: according to BuzzFeed News, it denied knowledge of any illegal conduct, said "the situation did not happen in Brazil" and decided "not to renew the partnership with CA, until everything has been clarified", and it had negotiated a contract with the Brazilian Social Democracy Party (PSDB) at the end of 2017 that the party did not finalize ([BuzzFeed News, 20 March 2018](https://www.buzzfeednews.com/article/mauroalbano/cambridge-analytica-brazil-suspend-partnership)). In December 2019 the Ministry of Justice's consumer protection department fined Facebook R$6.6 million, finding that data on about 443,000 Brazilian users had been at the disposal of the app's developers "para finalidades, no mínimo, questionáveis" (for purposes that were, at the least, questionable); the ministry also said the app had no link to Cambridge Analytica ([Forbes Brasil, from Reuters, 30 December 2019](https://forbes.com.br/last/2019/12/brasil-multa-facebook-em-r-66-mi-em-apuracao-sobre-compartilhamento-de-dados/)). The outcome of the 2018 inquiry was not found in the sources read.

**Does psychological targeting work?** The question behind the scandal is contested in peer-reviewed research. In three field experiments reaching over 3.5 million people, S. C. Matz and colleagues reported that ads matched to a person's extraversion or openness produced "up to 40% more clicks and up to 50% more purchases" ([Matz et al., PNAS, 2017, PubMed abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=29133409&rettype=abstract&retmode=text)). D. Eckles, B. R. Gordon and G. A. Johnson replied that <Claim status="disputed" by="by Eckles, Gordon and Johnson">"Field studies of psychologically targeted ads face threats to internal validity"</Claim>, disclosing past employment by or contracts with Facebook ([Eckles et al., PNAS, 2018](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=29777091&rettype=abstract&retmode=text)), and the original authors answered that "Facebook's optimization algorithms are highly unlikely to explain the effects of psychological targeting" ([Matz et al., reply, 2018](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=29777090&rettype=abstract&retmode=text)). Clicks and purchases are not votes, and no study in the sources here measured the effect of Cambridge Analytica's own work on an election.

## "Team Jorge": an undercover pitch (2023)

On 15 February 2023 a consortium of more than 100 journalists from 30 outlets, coordinated by Forbidden Stories, published "Story Killers", including an investigation of a team of Israeli contractors ([The Guardian, 15 February 2023](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2023/feb/15/revealed-disinformation-team-jorge-claim-meddling-elections-tal-hanan)). Reporters posing as intermediaries for a prospective African client ([OCCRP, 2023](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/story-killers/hacks-bots-and-blackmail-how-secret-cyber-mercenaries-disrupt-elections)) recorded more than six hours of meetings with a man using the pseudonym "Jorge", whom the Guardian identifies as Tal Hanan, "a 50-year-old former Israeli special forces operative" ([The Guardian, 2023](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2023/feb/15/revealed-disinformation-team-jorge-claim-meddling-elections-tal-hanan)).

**What was claimed.** Hanan told the reporters: <Claim status="alleged" by="by Hanan, in a recorded sales pitch">"[We have completed] 33 presidential-level campaigns, 27 of which were successful"</Claim>; on screen he showed the reporters how he could penetrate Telegram and Gmail accounts, and demonstrated Advanced Impact Media Solutions (AIMS), software that the Guardian says controls thousands of fake social media profiles ([The Guardian, 2023](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2023/feb/15/revealed-disinformation-team-jorge-claim-meddling-elections-tal-hanan)). The Guardian reported that he quoted between €6 million and €15 million for interference in an election, and the Organized Crime and Corruption Reporting Project (OCCRP) that he claimed to have sold AIMS to the intelligence services of more than 10 countries ([OCCRP, 2023](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/story-killers/hacks-bots-and-blackmail-how-secret-cyber-mercenaries-disrupt-elections)).

**What was verified, and what was not.** The OCCRP says the reporters "were able to verify Team Jorge's claims to access messaging accounts of important political targets and deploy social media campaigns orchestrated via fake accounts" ([OCCRP, 2023](https://www.occrp.org/en/project/story-killers/hacks-bots-and-blackmail-how-secret-cyber-mercenaries-disrupt-elections)), and the Guardian and its partners tracked AIMS-linked bot activity in about 20 countries, "mostly involving commercial disputes". The Guardian also cautions that "It was not possible to verify all of Team Jorge's claims in the undercover meetings, and Hanan may have been embellishing them in order to secure a lucrative deal with prospective clients" ([The Guardian, 2023](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2023/feb/15/revealed-disinformation-team-jorge-claim-meddling-elections-tal-hanan)); Forbidden Stories calls the election tally "a claim that is difficult to verify" ([Forbidden Stories, 15 February 2023](https://forbiddenstories.org/story-killers/team-jorge-disinformation/)).

**The replies.** Hanan did not respond to detailed questions, the Guardian wrote, but said: "To be clear, I do deny any wrongdoing" ([The Guardian, 2023](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2023/feb/15/revealed-disinformation-team-jorge-claim-meddling-elections-tal-hanan)). Israel's Ministry of Defense, on whose export-promotion website one of his companies was registered, did not respond to requests for comment (same); a technology company whose products Hanan presented told Forbidden Stories that it had never had business dealings with him or his associates ([Forbidden Stories, 2023](https://forbiddenstories.org/story-killers/team-jorge-disinformation/)). In Brazil, the Agence France-Presse (AFP) account of the investigation ran in the Folha de Pernambuco under the headline "Empresa israelense influenciou dezenas de eleições, diz pesquisa" ([Folha de Pernambuco, from AFP, 15 February 2023](https://www.folhape.com.br/noticias/empresa-israelense-influenciou-dezenas-de-eleicoes-diz-pesquisa/258415/)). The consortium also obtained a 2015 pitch from Hanan to Cambridge Analytica, which Forbidden Stories describes as a document of just over three pages.

## Brazil, 2018: the mass-messaging vendors in the court record

Brazil's own case of commercial influence work went through its electoral court. A later part of the series, on Brazil's mass messaging, the courts and Congress, takes up the politics; the record of the vendors is set out here.

**The report.** On 18 October 2018 Folha de S.Paulo reported that <Claim status="reported" by="by Folha de S.Paulo">businesses were buying packages of mass WhatsApp messages against the Workers' Party (PT), with contracts of up to R$12 million</Claim>, and named four agencies offering the service: Quickmobile, Yacows, Croc Services and SMS Market ([Folha de S.Paulo, 18 October 2018](https://www1.folha.uol.com.br/poder/2018/10/empresarios-bancam-campanha-contra-o-pt-pelo-whatsapp.shtml)).

**What WhatsApp told the court.** The Superior Electoral Court (Tribunal Superior Eleitoral, TSE) judgment of 28 October 2021, in two cases brought against the winning 2018 presidential ticket of Jair Bolsonaro and Hamilton Mourão, records that WhatsApp found in October 2018 that the four companies <Claim status="established" by="by the TSE, on WhatsApp's evidence">offered mass-messaging services in breach of its terms of service</Claim>, that their advertisements described three work shifts and up to 75,000 sends a day, and that concrete signs of mass sending by two of them led to the banning of accounts linked to them ([TSE, AIJE 0601968-80 and 0601771-28, 2021](https://sjur-servicos.tse.jus.br/sjur-servicos/rest/download/pdf/2673321)). A year later a WhatsApp manager, Ben Supple, said at a conference in Colombia: "Na eleição brasileira do ano passado houve a atuação de empresas fornecedoras de envios maciços de mensagens, que violaram nossos termos de uso para atingir um grande número de pessoas" (in last year's Brazilian election, companies supplying mass message sending acted in violation of our terms of use to reach a large number of people) ([A Gazeta, from Folha, 8 October 2019](https://www.agazeta.com.br/brasil/whatsapp-confirma-envio-ilegal-de-mensagens-em-massa-nas-eleicoes-1019)).

**The vendors' defences.** The partners of Yacows argued that its platform worked only with individual sends chosen by clients, that the users were responsible for the content, and that 150 million messages would have been technically impossible under WhatsApp's anti-spam controls; the partners of Croc Services said they had made no contract to serve Bolsonaro and Mourão, whether directly, through the retailer named in the press report or through its owner, and that all their election-messaging services in 2018 had been hired directly by candidates' campaigns, invoiced and declared in the campaign accounts; a former partner of Quick Mobile said the company sent only text messages ([TSE, 2021](https://sjur-servicos.tse.jus.br/sjur-servicos/rest/download/pdf/2673321)).

**What the court decided.** The TSE held that mass messaging with disinformation through instant-messaging apps can amount to abuse of economic power or misuse of the media, but it dismissed both cases because the evidence did not show the messages' content, their reach, how they affected voters, the candidates' degree of participation, or <Claim status="established" by="by the TSE">"se a campanha foi financiada por empresas com essa finalidade"</Claim> (whether the campaign was financed by companies for that purpose); WhatsApp had told the court in November 2019 that it kept user records for six months at most ([TSE, 2021](https://sjur-servicos.tse.jus.br/sjur-servicos/rest/download/pdf/2673321)).

**The congressional inquiry.** A joint parliamentary commission of inquiry (Comissão Parlamentar Mista de Inquérito, CPMI) on fake news was installed in September 2019 to investigate the 2018 mass messaging; paralysed from the first quarter of 2020, it was expected to end without a conclusion, CartaCapital reported from O Globo in April 2022 ([CartaCapital, 8 April 2022](https://www.cartacapital.com.br/politica/congresso-enterra-cpmi-das-fake-news-sem-chegar-a-uma-conclusao/)), and the Senate lists it as closed ([Senado Federal, CPMI das Fake News](https://legis.senado.leg.br/comissoes/composicao_comissao?codcol=2292)). No final report was found.

## How the business works, and who buys

**The product.** Across the cases, firms sell the same few things: fake or rented accounts and the software to run them (AIMS; the Archimedes and Fazze networks), real people paid to post (the teenagers of Rally Forge; the influencers Fazze approached), mass delivery through messaging apps (the 2018 Brazilian vendors), data matched to voter files (SCL and Cambridge Analytica, in the [ICO's description](https://ico.org.uk/media2/migrated/2618383/20201002_ico-o-ed-l-rtl-0181_to-julian-knight-mp.pdf)), and increasingly content written with generative models (Comm&Sense, STOIC) ([OpenAI, June 2025](https://cdn.openai.com/threat-intelligence-reports/5f73af09-a3a3-4a55-992e-069237681620/disrupting-malicious-uses-of-ai-june-2025.pdf)). The Oxford inventory lists the same repertoire of sock puppets, micro-targeting and amplification ([Bradshaw, Bailey and Howard, 2021](https://demtech.oii.ox.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/sites/8/2021/02/CyberTroop-Report20-Draft9.pdf)).

**The customers.** The documented buyers include a political action committee's affiliate (Rally Forge), a caretaker government that says it bought only lobbying (CLS Strategies), clients in six African countries (Archimedes Group), an unknown client against two vaccine makers (Fazze), a presumed political client in the Philippines (Comm&Sense) and, according to The New York Times, a ministry that denies it (STOIC). Team Jorge's tracked bot activity was mostly about commercial disputes ([The Guardian, 2023](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2023/feb/15/revealed-disinformation-team-jorge-claim-meddling-elections-tal-hanan)). The Oxford figures concern state actors and parties only.

**The price.** The published numbers are few and of mixed quality: about $812,000 of Facebook ads over six years for Archimedes ([Meta, 2019](https://about.fb.com/news/2019/05/removing-coordinated-inauthentic-behavior-from-israel/)), up to R$12 million per contract in Folha's 2018 report, contested by the vendors in court, €6 million to €15 million per election in Team Jorge's pitch, which the Guardian thought may have been inflated, and almost $60 million in confirmed contracts worldwide since 2009 in the Oxford count.

**The selling point.** For the client, the value is distance: Meta's 2021 report says commercial operators provide "deniability to their customers" and could let "sophisticated actors" hide behind private firms, "making attribution more challenging" ([Meta, 2021](https://about.fb.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/IO-Threat-Report-May-20-2021.pdf)). For the seller, the claims of success are part of the product, which is why the Guardian and the ICO both found the firms' own descriptions of their reach hard to take at face value.

Meta distinguishes two tiers: for domestic, non-government campaigns it removes the accounts and Pages directly involved, and for operations "on behalf of a government entity or by a foreign actor" it applies "the broadest enforcement measures including the removal of every on-platform property connected to the operation itself and the people and organizations behind it" ([Meta, 2021](https://about.fb.com/news/2021/08/july-2021-coordinated-inauthentic-behavior-report/)). Platform enforcement reaches the vendor's accounts on one service; it does not reach the vendor's other services, its contracts or its clients unless a regulator or a court does.

## What is still unknown

Whether bought influence changes what people believe or how they vote is not shown in any of these records. The firms' own claims are sales material; the platforms' engagement counts, such as OpenAI's single-digit replies for Comm&Sense, measure activity on one service ([OpenAI, 2025](https://cdn.openai.com/threat-intelligence-reports/5f73af09-a3a3-4a55-992e-069237681620/disrupting-malicious-uses-of-ai-june-2025.pdf)); the ICO found the Cambridge Analytica models less accurate than advertised ([ICO, 2020](https://ico.org.uk/media2/migrated/2618383/20201002_ico-o-ed-l-rtl-0181_to-julian-knight-mp.pdf)); and the peer-reviewed dispute over psychological targeting concerns clicks and purchases. The size of the market is known only through exposures, and the Oxford authors expect the real spending to be higher than the confirmed figure ([Bradshaw, Bailey and Howard, 2021](https://demtech.oii.ox.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/sites/8/2021/02/CyberTroop-Report20-Draft9.pdf)). Who the clients are is often the least documented part of each case.

<ReaderQuestions>

- When a firm is banned but its client is not, who has been held to account for the operation?
- If the main evidence of a firm's power is its own sales pitch, how should a reader weigh both its boasts and the alarm they cause?
- Where is the line between a campaign hiring a digital agency and a campaign buying an influence operation, and who should draw it: platforms, electoral courts or legislators?
- A court found that mass messaging took place but could not measure its content or reach because the records were gone; what should be kept, by whom and for how long, and at what cost to privacy?
- Should firms that sell political communication services be required to disclose their clients, as lobbyists are in some countries?

</ReaderQuestions>

## What to remember

- The Oxford inventories counted firms selling "computational propaganda as a service" in 48 countries in 2020 and almost $60 million in confirmed contracts since 2009, from news reports, so the real figures are likely higher.
- Platforms have named and banned marketing and public relations firms in Israel, the United States, Russia via a UK company, and the Philippines; the firms' replies were rarely published.
- The FTC found by default that Cambridge Analytica deceived consumers; Facebook paid the ICO's £500,000 fine without admitting liability; the ICO found the firm's models "off the shelf", its claims exaggerated and its predictions likely much less accurate in the real world.
- Team Jorge's claims to have worked on 33 presidential campaigns came from a recorded sales pitch; some capabilities were verified, the tally was not, and Tal Hanan denies wrongdoing.
- Brazil's electoral court accepted, on WhatsApp's evidence, that four vendors offered mass messaging in breach of its terms in 2018, but dismissed the cases because content, reach and financing were not proven; the congressional inquiry ended without a report.
- Whether bought influence works remains unmeasured in these records.
