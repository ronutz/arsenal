When an order is late, rejected or filled at a worse price than expected, three parties will each have their own account of what happened: the firm's trading application, its broker and the exchange. The network has a fourth, and it is the one that none of them wrote. On 15 October 2014, when the United States (US) Treasury market moved violently for twelve minutes, the official report found that the futures exchange slowed as message traffic peaked and noted that "sudden changes in latency would cause them to adjust their behavior", "them" being the participants who monitor latency as an input to their trading ([staffs of the US Treasury and four other agencies, Joint Staff Report, 13 July 2015](https://home.treasury.gov/system/files/276/joint-staff-report-the-us-treasury-market-on-10-15-2014.pdf)). Watching trading on the wire is how firms see such changes as they happen and prove them afterwards.

This part of the series opened by [Electronic trading: from the pit to the microsecond](/learn/electronic-trading-from-the-pit-to-the-microsecond) goes deeper into a subject that [Colocation, microwaves and the race for latency](/learn/colocation-microwaves-and-the-race-for-latency) introduced: how latency is measured on the wire, the European and American clock rules, and the purchase of Corvil by Pico. After reading, you will be able to explain where packets are captured and who stamps the time, read the header of a capture file with a nanosecond timestamp, tell hardware stamps from software stamps, decode the Financial Information eXchange (FIX) protocol and binary trading protocols on the wire, follow one order through the measure points that B3 (Brasil, Bolsa, Balcão) publishes and say which latencies a clock error makes meaningless, place Corvil, Pico and the other vendors from their own pages, and list the record-keeping rules this record helps a firm to meet. The site's general guides to capture, [Capture points before packets](/learn/capture-points-before-packets) and [Wireshark and the capture stack](/learn/wireshark-and-the-capture-stack), cover the tools; this part covers what trading does with them.

## Why watch the wire

### A record nobody wrote

An application can only log what it sees, and only when it gets round to logging it. A copy of the packets is independent of every application involved. Pico, which sells the best-known product of this kind, describes Corvil Analytics as "Pico's network-derived analytics platform for financial markets" that captures packet data "without agents, application changes, or latency overhead" to create "one independent, time-synchronized golden source of truth" ([Pico, Corvil Analytics, undated page](https://www.pico.net/corvil-analytics/)). Those are a vendor's words about its product; the underlying point, that a passive copy of the traffic does not depend on the systems it observes, is why the Financial Conduct Authority's researchers took their data from an optical tap outside an exchange's firewall, as [the part on colocation](/learn/colocation-microwaves-and-the-race-for-latency) describes.

### What the watchers look for

Central banks that study fast markets set out what has to be watched. A study group of the Markets Committee of the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) wrote that "Market conditions are best captured by indicators based on high-frequency (eg millisecond sampling) or even tick-by-tick data from primary venues", and that "Monitoring of market impact and transaction costs can require additional data on timestamps and characteristics of own trade executions" ([BIS Markets Committee, Monitoring of fast-paced electronic markets, September 2018](https://www.bis.org/publications/monitoring-fast-paced-electronic-markets.pdf)). The vendors list the operational side. When SIX, the Swiss exchange and data group, extended its use of Corvil to its market data business in 2025, Pico said that "Corvil's gap detection, latency and microburst analytics enable clients to detect if any incoming data is incomplete, delayed, or at risk of exceeding capacity" ([Pico, 6 August 2025](https://www.pico.net/about/news/pico-announces-corvil-analytics-has-been-selected-by-six-to-strengthen-data-transparency-and-operational-performance/)). Pico also says its in-memory analytics deliver "1-second metrics for congestion, TCP retransmissions, round-trip times, and errors" ([Pico, Corvil Analytics](https://www.pico.net/corvil-analytics/)). A microburst is a very short spike of traffic that average counters miss; the site's glossary entry on [microbursts](/glossary/microburst) explains it, and the trading term for the time from a market data update to the order sent in response is [tick-to-trade](/glossary/tick-to-trade).

### Venues watch too

Exchanges use the same tools on themselves. Pico pitches Corvil to venues as a way to "Protect market confidence with an independent record of venue performance, member experience, and market-data quality" ([Pico, Corvil Analytics](https://www.pico.net/corvil-analytics/)). When Japannext, which Pico describes as the operator of Japan's largest alternative equity trading venue, with 31 active members, chose Corvil Analytics, Japannext's chief information officer was quoted as saying that "Corvil's support for our trading protocols allows us to correlate data across participants, trading sessions, or market data feeds per our needs" ([Pico, 7 June 2023](https://www.pico.net/about/news/japannext-selects-corvil-analytics-to-measure-and-analyze-trading-performance/)). B3's timestamp guide, described below, is the opposite move: instead of only watching its own wire, the exchange publishes its internal clocks so that members can measure it.

### What millisecond data reveals

Fine timestamps show behaviour that averages hide. The BIS study group found that when EBS increased the speed of its spot feed to five milliseconds in 2017, "a visible change in the frequency of activity occurred not only on the platform itself, but also on other electronic venues including futures exchanges", which it read as a sign of the platform's importance for price discovery ([BIS Markets Committee, September 2018](https://www.bis.org/publications/monitoring-fast-paced-electronic-markets.pdf)); its graphs bucket quotes by the millisecond within each 100-millisecond cycle to show the pattern. The US report on the Treasury market used the same resolution to find trades at the Chicago futures exchange followed by trades in New Jersey about five milliseconds later, as [Interdealer brokers and electronic fixed income and FX](/learn/interdealer-brokers-and-electronic-fixed-income-and-fx) describes. Neither finding is visible in data stamped to the second.

The other copy of the truth comes from the trading system itself. Exchanges send drop copies, duplicate execution reports for risk and back-office systems, as [The life of an order in FIX](/learn/the-life-of-an-order-in-fix) explains; drop copies say what the exchange decided, while the wire says when each message actually passed each point. The two are complementary, and the pre-trade controls they feed are the subject of [Pre-trade risk and market access](/learn/pre-trade-risk-and-market-access-controls).

## Getting a copy of every packet

### Taps, mirrors and layer 1 switches

There are two ordinary ways to copy traffic: a switch can mirror a port, which the site's glossary describes under [port mirroring](/glossary/port-mirroring), or a passive device, a tap, can split the signal on the cable itself. [Capture points before packets](/learn/capture-points-before-packets) explains how to choose where to look. Trading added a third: switches that work at the physical layer and can copy, aggregate and stamp packets in a few nanoseconds. Arista Networks said on 5 March 2019 that it had launched the first platform based on its acquisition of Metamako, announced in September 2018, with deterministic switching of about five nanoseconds, multiplexing in about 39 nanoseconds on average and a MetaWatch application offering timestamping at sub-nanosecond accuracy for tapping and aggregating packets for analysis or compliance ([Arista Networks, press release, 5 March 2019](https://www.arista.com/en/company/news/press-release/6777-pr-20190305)). Cisco announced on 16 December 2019 its intent to acquire Exablaze, "a privately held, Australia-based designer and manufacturer of advanced network devices aimed at reducing latency and improving network performance", and a later update on the same page says "We have completed the acquisition of Exablaze" ([Cisco, 16 December 2019, with update dated 02/03/2020](https://news-blogs.cisco.com/news/cisco-corporate-news-december-2019)); in September 2020 it wrote that its Nexus 3550 switches' "layer 1 tapping, high-precision timestamping and aggregation capabilities" would work with Pico's capture and analytics systems ([Cisco, financial services blog, 28 September 2020](https://blogs.cisco.com/financialservices/cisco-and-pico-collaborate-to-help-high-frequency-trading)). The latency figures are the companies' own.

### Appliances that keep everything

Behind the taps sit recorders. Pico says its Corvil 12000 appliance delivers "continuous, real-time 200Gbps capture, analysis and publishing to data warehouses" ([Pico, 16 September 2025](https://www.pico.net/about/news/pico-redefines-network-observability-with-200gbps-continuous-network-capture-and-analytics/)). Endace says that "For more than 20 years, Endace has been the gold standard for reliable, scalable, accurate, always-on Packet Capture", recording "weeks or months of full packet capture at 100 Gbps and beyond" ([Endace, home page, undated](https://www.endace.com/)), and cPacket Networks says "Over half of the world's financial market data touches cPacket solutions" ([cPacket, home page, undated](https://www.cpacket.com/)). None of these claims has an independent audit behind it in the pages read; they show how the vendors position themselves.

Captured packets have even become a product. The London Stock Exchange Group (LSEG) sells historical market data as packet captures, which it describes as a repository of data "captured directly in the exchange data centres" with nanosecond timestamps, and in 2023 added its own foreign exchange (FX) venue, FX Matching, which it said was then the only FX venue contributing such data and whose captures show "the latency that is attached to the update" ([LSEG, Tick History PCAP article, September 2023](https://lseg.com/en/insights/fx/revolutionising-fx-price-transparency-with-tick-history-pcap)). The market behind that venue is the subject of [Interdealer brokers and electronic fixed income and FX](/learn/interdealer-brokers-and-electronic-fixed-income-and-fx).

## Time stamps: where, by whom, how fine

### Hardstamps and softstamps

A timestamp is only as good as the point where it is taken. B3's guide to the timestamps of its PUMA Trading System gives the two kinds names: "Hardstamps: timestamps captured in the wire or in dedicated device" and "Softstamps: timestamps captured in the application software" ([B3, PUMA Trading System Timestamps, version 1.1.0, 13 June 2025](https://b3.com.br/data/files/F3/30/BD/D4/E1F87910C2881879AC094EA8/B3-PUMA-Timestamps-1.1.0-enUS.pdf)). It also separates resolution, "the fineness to which the time can be read", precision, "the fineness to which the time can be read repeatably and reliably", and accuracy, "the correctness of the measured time" ([B3, 2025](https://b3.com.br/data/files/F3/30/BD/D4/E1F87910C2881879AC094EA8/B3-PUMA-Timestamps-1.1.0-enUS.pdf)). A nanosecond field in a message is a statement about resolution; it says nothing about accuracy.

B3 is candid about its own stamps: "Currently, all timestamps published in outbound messages (order entry and market data) are softstamps generated by the related PUMA components (binary gateway or matching engine)", taken from system clocks synchronized by the Precision Time Protocol (PTP) with B3's "own stratum-3 atomic master clock", and "the standard deviation for the offset from the master clock (accuracy of our timestamps) is up to one microsecond" ([B3, 2025](https://b3.com.br/data/files/F3/30/BD/D4/E1F87910C2881879AC094EA8/B3-PUMA-Timestamps-1.1.0-enUS.pdf)). On a server, hardware stamps come from the network adapter, which Linux exposes through its timestamping interface, as [the part on colocation](/learn/colocation-microwaves-and-the-race-for-latency) shows; the same part sets out the clock rules, the 100 microseconds that the European Union (EU) asks of high-frequency firms and the 50 milliseconds that the US audit trail plan asks of brokers.

### Stamps that travel with the packet

A capture switch can write its timestamp into the packet itself, usually as a trailer after the Ethernet payload, much as the F5 trailer that [Reading a BIG-IP capture](/learn/reading-a-bigip-capture) decodes records how a load balancer handled each flow. Wireshark ships dissectors for several such trailers: the Metamako trailer, with fields for a sequence number, the source device and port and a flag for a degraded timestamp, since version 2.2.0; the Exablaze trailer, with a timestamp in seconds since the epoch and fractional seconds, the device and the port, since 3.0.0; and trailers from Ixia and VSS Monitoring with their own time stamp fields ([Wireshark, display filter reference: Metamako ethernet trailer](https://www.wireshark.org/docs/dfref/m/metamako.html); [Exablaze trailer](https://www.wireshark.org/docs/dfref/e/exablaze.html); [Ixia Trailer](https://www.wireshark.org/docs/dfref/i/ixiatrailer.html); [VSS Monitoring Ethernet trailer](https://www.wireshark.org/docs/dfref/v/vssmonitoring.html)). The stamp then survives any later copy of the packet, which a stamp kept only by the recorder does not.

### What the file keeps

Captures are stored in the pcap format, the one the site's glossary entry [pcap](/glossary/pcap) describes, or in its successor pcapng. The Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) draft that documents pcap says the file header's magic number tells the reader the resolution: "If the value is 0xA1B2C3D4, timestamps in Packet Records (see Figure 3) are in seconds and microseconds; if it is 0xA1B23C4D, timestamps in Packet Records are in seconds and nanoseconds", and the 16-byte record header in front of each packet holds the seconds elapsed since midnight on 1 January 1970 in Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) as a 32-bit unsigned integer, the fraction as another, then the captured and original lengths ([IETF, draft-ietf-opsawg-pcap-09, September 2026, intended status Historic](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-opsawg-pcap/)). In pcapng the resolution is an option of each interface, and "If this option is not present, a resolution of 10^-6 is assumed" ([IETF, draft-ietf-opsawg-pcapng-06, September 2026](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-opsawg-pcapng/)). A capture written in microseconds cannot be made to say nanoseconds later; the resolution is decided when it is written. [Seconds, milliseconds, microseconds, nanoseconds](/learn/epoch-units-seconds-to-nanoseconds) explains how to tell the units apart.

### Worked example: the first bytes of a nanosecond capture

Take an order captured at 13:00:00.000010000 UTC on 16 November 2026, carried in an Ethernet frame of 171 bytes: 14 bytes of Ethernet header, 20 of IPv4, 20 of TCP without options, and the 117-byte binary SimpleNewOrder that [the part on FIX encodings](/learn/fix-encodings-and-successors-fixml-fast-sbe-and-exchange-binary-protocols) decodes from B3's own documentation. A little-endian file in nanoseconds begins as follows; the bytes were computed by two independent programs, and the field layout is the draft's ([IETF, draft-ietf-opsawg-pcap-09](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-opsawg-pcap/)).

| Bytes (hexadecimal) | Field | Value |
|---|---|---|
| `4d 3c b2 a1` | Magic number, written little-endian | 0xA1B23C4D: seconds and nanoseconds |
| `02 00` `04 00` | Major and minor version | 2.4 |
| `00 00 00 00` `00 00 00 00` | Reserved | 0 |
| `00 00 04 00` | SnapLen | 262,144 bytes |
| `01 00 00 00` | LinkType | 1, Ethernet |
| `50 fe fa 6a` | Record: timestamp, seconds | 1,794,834,000 = 2026-11-16 13:00:00 UTC |
| `10 27 00 00` | Record: timestamp, fraction | 10,000 nanoseconds |
| `ab 00 00 00` `ab 00 00 00` | Record: captured and original length | 171 and 171 bytes |

LinkType 1 is Ethernet in the registry that tcpdump's maintainers publish ([tcpdump.org, link-layer header types](https://www.tcpdump.org/linktypes.html)). The seconds field is unsigned and 32 bits wide, so by the same arithmetic it runs out at 2106-02-07 06:28:15 UTC, a limit pcapng's 64-bit timestamps do not share; [Why Unix time ignores leap seconds](/learn/unix-time-and-leap-seconds) explains why such counts are not true elapsed seconds. The [epoch converter](/tools/epoch) turns the seconds field into a date.

## Decoding the conversation

### FIX in plain text

Once the packets are kept, they have to be read as trading messages. FIX in its classic tag=value form is text, laid out field by field in [Anatomy of a FIX message and the session layer](/learn/anatomy-of-a-fix-message-and-the-session-layer). Wireshark's dissector for it has existed since version 1.0.0 and exposes each tag as a field: MsgType (35) as `fix.MsgType`, ClOrdID (11) as `fix.ClOrdID`, SendingTime (52) as `fix.SendingTime` and TransactTime (60) as `fix.TransactTime`, among some 1,640 fields in all ([Wireshark, display filter reference: Financial Information eXchange Protocol](https://www.wireshark.org/docs/dfref/f/fix.html)). With Wireshark's display filter operators, where `==` means equal ([Wireshark User's Guide, building display filter expressions](https://www.wireshark.org/docs/wsug_html_chunked/ChWorkBuildDisplayFilterSection.html)), a filter such as `fix.MsgType == "D"` keeps only new orders. Matching a NewOrderSingle to its ExecutionReport by ClOrdID, and comparing the tap's time with the SendingTime the sender wrote in the message, is the simplest latency measurement there is.

### Binary protocols need their schemas

Binary protocols are not readable without a description of each message. Wireshark includes dissectors for Nasdaq's OUCH order entry protocol since version 1.10.0, for its SoupBinTCP session layer, for MoldUDP64, the multicast framing of its market data, since 1.8.0, and for Eurex's Enhanced Order Book Interface market data since 4.0.0 ([Wireshark, OUCH](https://www.wireshark.org/docs/dfref/o/ouch.html); [SoupBinTCP](https://www.wireshark.org/docs/dfref/s/soupbintcp.html); [MoldUDP64](https://www.wireshark.org/docs/dfref/m/moldudp64.html); [Enhanced Order Book Interface 10.0](https://www.wireshark.org/docs/dfref/e/eobi.html)). Simple Binary Encoding (SBE), used by B3 and by CME Group, the parent of the Chicago Mercantile Exchange, is not self-describing: the reader needs the venue's schema of the right version, as [the part on FIX encodings](/learn/fix-encodings-and-successors-fixml-fast-sbe-and-exchange-binary-protocols) shows. Commercial analysers sell exactly this library: Pico advertises "600+ decoders" for Corvil Analytics, and says its release 10.2 of September 2026 improves decoding throughput by "up to a 30-50 percent" ([Pico, Corvil Analytics](https://www.pico.net/corvil-analytics/); [Pico, 15 September 2026](https://www.pico.net/about/news/picos-corvil-analytics-unlocks-trusted-network-data-for-ai-workflows-with-102-software-release/)). Every new message version at a venue means a new decoder, and a decoder one version behind silently misreads fields that moved.

## Worked example: one order through B3's measure points

### What B3 publishes

B3 publishes its PUMA timestamp guide so that, in its words, "clients may better track latency of these messages and identify missed opportunities" ([B3, PUMA Trading System Timestamps, 1.1.0](https://b3.com.br/data/files/F3/30/BD/D4/E1F87910C2881879AC094EA8/B3-PUMA-Timestamps-1.1.0-enUS.pdf)). Each measure point is a field in B3's binary messages; their default resolution is nanoseconds.

| Measure point | Feed | Field | Set by | Meaning, in B3's words |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T1 | Private | InboundBusinessHeader.sendingTime | Client | "Provided by the client." |
| T2 | Private | receivedTime | Gateway | assigned after the message is received from the client's socket |
| T3 | Private | none | LiNe | assigned by LiNe, B3's risk layer, at the start of market segment processing; not published |
| T4 | Private | marketSegmentReceivedTime | Matching engine | when the message is received from the internal bus |
| T5 | Private and public | transactTime | Matching engine | execution or order creation; the same value in the order entry and market data flows |
| T6 | Private | OutboundBusinessHeader.sendingTime | Gateway | just before the gateway queues the message to the client's socket |
| T10 | Public | mDEntryTimestamp | Matching engine | during the assembly of market data statistics messages |
| T11 | Public | packetHeader.sendingTime | Matching engine | just before the market data packet is published in UDP multicast |

Source: [B3, PUMA Trading System Timestamps, version 1.1.0, 13 June 2025, section 3.4](https://b3.com.br/data/files/F3/30/BD/D4/E1F87910C2881879AC094EA8/B3-PUMA-Timestamps-1.1.0-enUS.pdf). The document's change log shows the record improving: in October 2024 outbound sendingTime moved from milliseconds to nanoseconds, synchronized with PTP; from 9 December 2024 receivedTime (T2) appeared in execution reports; in June 2025 transactTime (T5) replaced the older field in the order book messages ([B3, 2025, change log](https://b3.com.br/data/files/F3/30/BD/D4/E1F87910C2881879AC094EA8/B3-PUMA-Timestamps-1.1.0-enUS.pdf)). LiNe, the risk layer behind T3, is described in [Pre-trade risk and market access](/learn/pre-trade-risk-and-market-access-controls).

### One order, invented numbers

The numbers below are invented for the example; they are not B3 measurements. They are microseconds after 13:00:00 UTC on an illustrative day, and "tap A" is a hardware-stamping capture point on the firm's side of its cross connect, synchronized to the firm's own PTP clock. The legs between the rack and the gateway were chosen near the laboratory figures B3 publishes for its colocation network, 22 microseconds for orders and 11 for market data, which [the part on colocation](/learn/colocation-microwaves-and-the-race-for-latency) tabulates.

| Event | Clock | Time (µs) |
|---|---|---|
| T1: the firm's application stamps the order | firm's application | 8.7 |
| Order passes tap A | tap A (firm's PTP) | 10.0 |
| T2: B3's gateway receives it | B3 | 32.4 |
| T4: the matching engine receives it | B3 | 180.6 |
| T5: the order is created in the book | B3 | 181.9 |
| T11: market data packet with the new order leaves | B3 | 183.0 |
| Market data packet passes tap A | tap A | 194.1 |
| T6: the gateway queues the execution report | B3 | 335.2 |
| Execution report passes tap A | tap A | 357.6 |

Computed by two independent programs from those inputs:

| Measurement | Arithmetic | Result (µs) | Trust |
|---|---|---|---|
| Round trip seen on the wire | 357.6 − 10.0 | 347.6 | one clock (tap A): good |
| Time inside B3 | T6 − T2 = 335.2 − 32.4 | 302.8 | one clock (B3): good |
| of which gateway to matching engine, including LiNe | T4 − T2 | 148.2 | one clock |
| of which in the matching engine | T5 − T4 | 1.3 | one clock |
| of which matching engine back to the gateway | T6 − T5 | 153.3 | one clock |
| Both network legs together | 347.6 − 302.8 | 44.8 | a difference of durations: good |
| Inbound leg alone | T2 − tap A | 22.4 | two clocks: only as good as their offset |
| Market data, B3 to tap A | 194.1 − 183.0 | 11.1 | two clocks |
| Application to wire | 10.0 − 8.7 | 1.3 | two clocks, unless the application and tap share one |

The lesson is in the last column. A duration measured on one clock, or the difference of two such durations, does not depend on whether that clock is set right, only on how steadily it ticks. A one-way leg compares two clocks, so its error is the sum of their offsets from true time. B3 states its own offset as up to about a microsecond ([B3, 2025](https://b3.com.br/data/files/F3/30/BD/D4/E1F87910C2881879AC094EA8/B3-PUMA-Timestamps-1.1.0-enUS.pdf)). If the firm's clock were only as good as the 100 microseconds that the EU's clock standard requires of high-frequency trading firms ([Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2017/574, annex, table 2](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32017R0574)), the inbound leg of 22.4 microseconds could truly be anything from −77.6 to 122.4: a negative leg would mean the order arrived before it left. Meeting the legal minimum is not enough to measure one-way latency inside a colocation hall; that takes clocks far better than the rule.

### Standardizing the measure points

The industry once tried to make such measurements comparable across firms. WatersTechnology reported on 4 January 2010 that FIX Protocol Limited, now the FIX Trading Community, had "set up a FIX Inter-Party Latency (FIPL) working group to provide standards for data and trading latency monitoring and information sharing", co-chaired by Henry Young of TS-Associates ([WatersTechnology, 4 January 2010](https://www.waterstechnology.com/trading-tech/1615516/fpl-preps-latency-standards-group)), and on 12 September 2011 that a prototype "has completed testing with a number of firms involved" ahead of a production release planned for the fourth quarter ([WatersTechnology, 12 September 2011](https://waterstechnology.com/regulation/2108290/fipl-latency-standard-nears-completion)). Markets Media reported support from Corvil, whose chief technology officer and founder, Raymond Russell, spoke for a standard, and wrote: "One of the major technological challenges in latency monitoring is the lack of an official latency measurement standard that allows comparison of latency measurements", while Donal Byrne, then Corvil's chief executive, said "Latency transparency provides the critical information that allows a trader to assess the level of price risk for a trade opportunity and take the appropriate action" ([Markets Media, Latency monitoring standards at hand, undated](https://articles.marketsmedia.com/latency-monitoring-standards-at-hand)). The specification itself was not found on the FIX Trading Community's current standards pages, so this part does not describe its contents. B3's numbered measure points do part of the same job for one venue.

## Who sells it: Corvil, Pico and the others

### Corvil and Pico, from Pico's own pages

Pico's own release of 9 July 2019 announced that it "has signed a definitive agreement to acquire Corvil", that the combined company would serve "over 400" banks, exchanges, asset managers, technology vendors and trading firms with "over 375 staff", that Corvil's chief executive, Donal Byrne, would become chief technology officer, and that "The company will operate under the Pico brand while the Corvil name will continue to be used for its portfolio of products and services"; it listed Goldman Sachs, J.P. ## The rules that make firms keep the record

No rule read for this part requires packet capture as such. The rules require records, times and recordings, and a capture is one way, among others, to produce or check them.

### Europe

The revised Markets in Financial Instruments Directive (MiFID II) requires an investment firm to keep records "of all services, activities and transactions undertaken by it which shall be sufficient to enable the competent authority to fulfil its supervisory tasks", and adds that "Records shall include the recording of telephone conversations or electronic communications relating to, at least, transactions concluded when dealing on own account" and to client orders ([Directive 2014/65/EU, article 16(6) and (7)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32014L0065)). For high-frequency firms, the regulatory technical standard on algorithmic trading, Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2017/589, known as RTS 6, says a firm "shall immediately after order submission record the details of each submitted order" in prescribed tables, keeps them "for five years from the date of the submission of an order", and numbers each event with a sequence number that must be "timestamped by the investment firm and persistent for the date that the event occurs" ([Delegated Regulation (EU) 2017/589, article 28 and annex II](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32017R0589)); the same regulation's real-time monitoring duties are covered in [Pre-trade risk and market access](/learn/pre-trade-risk-and-market-access-controls). Trading venues keep order data under Delegated Regulation (EU) 2017/580, whose date and time field says "The number of digits after the 'seconds' shall be determined in accordance with Article 2 of the Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2017/574", the clock regulation ([Delegated Regulation (EU) 2017/580, annex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32017R0580)).

### United States

The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) required broker-dealers for years to keep electronic records "in a non-rewriteable, non-erasable format (also known as a write once, read many ('WORM') format)"; amendments adopted on 12 October 2022 "add an audit-trail alternative to the WORM requirement", under which the system must allow "the recreation of an original record if it is modified or deleted" ([SEC, Release 34-96034, 12 October 2022](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/final/2022/34-96034.pdf)). The Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) amended its recordkeeping rule in 2017, effective 28 August 2017, to "modernize and make technology neutral the form and manner in which regulatory records must be kept" ([CFTC, Recordkeeping, final rule, Federal Register, 30 May 2017](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2017-05-30/pdf/2017-11014.pdf)). Both moves let firms keep records in ordinary storage with a provable audit trail, which is how large capture archives are usually kept; the clock rule of the Consolidated Audit Trail is in [the part on colocation](/learn/colocation-microwaves-and-the-race-for-latency).

### Brazil

Brazil's securities regulator, the Comissão de Valores Mobiliários (CVM), sets the intermediaries' rules in Resolução CVM 35 of 26 May 2021. Every order must be recorded with its time: "Todas as ordens devem ser registradas, identificando-se o horário do seu recebimento, o cliente que as tenha emitido e as condições para a sua execução", all orders must be recorded, identifying the time they were received, the client who sent them and the conditions for their execution; an intermediary on an organized market "deve manter sistema de gravação de todos os diálogos mantidos com seus clientes", must keep a system recording all its conversations with clients; and records, recordings, audit trails and the records of the origin of direct market access (DMA) orders must be kept "pelo prazo mínimo de 5 (cinco) anos", for at least five years ([CVM, Resolução CVM 35, consolidated text, articles 12, 14 and 48](https://conteudo.cvm.gov.br/export/sites/cvm/legislacao/resolucoes/anexos/001/resol035consolid.pdf)). The DMA origin and pre-trade risk duties of the same resolution are in [Pre-trade risk and market access](/learn/pre-trade-risk-and-market-access-controls). On the exchange's side, B3's timestamp guide is the published reference for when each event happened inside PUMA, and [Brazil: BM&F, Bovespa, B3 and electronic access](/learn/brazil-bmf-bovespa-b3-and-electronic-access) describes the colocation hall where most of the hardware discussed here sits.

Clocks are the subject of a second resolution, on organized markets. Resolução CVM 135 of 10 June 2022, in force since 1 September 2022, requires the operator of an organized market, to record the trades on its systems and any events whose history must be kept, to "sincronizar os relógios utilizados adotando o padrão UTC", synchronize the clocks it uses to the Coordinated Universal Time standard; leaves the accuracy and precision of the stamps to the CVM, to be set according to each market's characteristics such as the volume of orders and trades, the types of participant or client, and the volatility and liquidity of the securities; and requires the operator's rules for admitting participants to provide for "a obrigatoriedade de adoção do padrão UTC para sincronização de relógios bem como a acurácia e precisão exigidas", mandatory UTC clock synchronization and the accuracy and precision required ([CVM, Resolução CVM 135, consolidated text, articles 17, 84 and 193](https://conteudo.cvm.gov.br/export/sites/cvm/legislacao/resolucoes/anexos/100/resol135consolid.pdf)). B3 added the requirement to its access rules from 22 May 2023 and announced that a later circular would specify the accuracy and precision to be adopted and the deadline to adapt ([B3, Ofício Circular 075/2023-PRE, 18 May 2023](https://www.b3.com.br/data/files/16/92/7A/0E/25038810779C3088DC0D8AA8/OC%20075-2023%20PRE%20Adequa%C3%A7%C3%B5es%20%C3%A0%20Resolu%C3%A7%C3%A3o%20CVM%20135%20%28PT%29.pdf)). Its access manual of 30 June 2025 still requires participants to synchronize their clocks with Brazilian legal time (Hora Legal Brasileira) and to store the information in UTC, following "as orientações, inclusive acerca de acurácia e precisão, previstas em Ofício Circular", the guidance, including on accuracy and precision, set out in a circular ([B3, Manual de Acesso da B3, 30 June 2025, section 2.1.7](https://sistemasweb.b3.com.br/normativos/MANUAL_DE_ACESSO_DA_B3-30062025.pdf)). A circular setting those figures was not found for this article. The European clock regulation, by contrast, writes its tolerances into the regulation itself, such as the 100 microseconds used in the worked example above.

## What the record cannot do

A capture shows what crossed one point of the network; it does not show what an application intended, what happened inside a box with no tap, or what a clock did between synchronizations. The worked example shows the main trap: durations on one clock are robust, one-way legs across clocks are only as good as the worse of the two clocks. B3's own stamps are softstamps taken by software, by its own description ([B3, 2025](https://b3.com.br/data/files/F3/30/BD/D4/E1F87910C2881879AC094EA8/B3-PUMA-Timestamps-1.1.0-enUS.pdf)). Decoders lag new message versions. And a full capture of order traffic is a record of clients' instructions, subject to the same retention and confidentiality duties as the orders themselves under the rules above.

## What to remember

- Network-derived analytics copies every packet at a tap or layer 1 switch, stamps it, decodes the trading protocols and reports on each order and market data message; it is independent of the applications it watches.
- Hardstamps are taken on the wire or in a dedicated device; softstamps in application software. B3 publishes softstamps from PTP-synchronized clocks with an offset of up to about a microsecond.
- A pcap file declares its resolution in the magic number (0xA1B23C4D for nanoseconds); pcapng defaults to microseconds unless told otherwise.
- FIX tag=value can be read by Wireshark's FIX dissector; binary protocols need the venue's schema, and decoders must track each new version.
- Durations measured on one clock are robust; one-way legs across two clocks carry both clocks' errors, so measuring them in microseconds needs clocks much better than the legal minimum.
- Pico agreed to acquire Corvil on 9 July 2019 and keeps the Corvil name; Beeks bought Velocimetrics in 2020, Arista bought Metamako in 2018 and Cisco bought Exablaze in 2019 and 2020.
- MiFID II, RTS 6, SEC Rule 17a-4, CFTC Rule 1.31 and Resolução CVM 35 require records, times and recordings, not packet capture itself; capture is one way to meet and check them.
- In Brazil, Resolução CVM 135 requires market operators, and through their access rules the participants, to synchronize clocks to UTC; the accuracy and precision were left to a later B3 circular, which was not found for this article.
