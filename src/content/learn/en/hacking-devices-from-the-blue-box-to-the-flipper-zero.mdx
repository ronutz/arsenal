A hacking device is a piece of hardware built, or bought, to speak a protocol in a way its designers did not intend: an oscillator that plays the telephone network's control tones, a dialler that rings ten thousand numbers to find the modems, a Universal Serial Bus (USB) stick that announces itself as a keyboard, a radio that records a remote control and plays it back. This series is about those devices: where each came from, how it works in principle, what it cannot do, how defenders detect and stop it, and what the law says about owning and using it, in the United States (US), the European Union (EU) and Brazil. This opener draws the map. After reading, you will be able to place any hacking gadget in a lineage that starts with the blue box, name the trust it exploits and the defence that closes it, read a dated timeline of the field from 1949 to 2025, and tell the line the law draws between a tool and a crime.

The series describes devices, mechanisms and cases from the makers' documentation, standards, court and regulator records and research papers. It gives no step-by-step recipe against a real system; where a vendor publishes its own examples, they are described and cited.

## Why special-purpose hardware keeps appearing

The same pattern runs through the documents of every era: a protocol accepts whatever arrives on its medium as legitimate, and a small device that produces the right signals becomes, as far as the protocol can tell, a legitimate participant.

The telephone network said it about itself. Its 1980 operating manual, published by the American Telephone and Telegraph Company (AT&T), noted that "pure tones near 2600 Hz will cause the far-end (receiving) unit to go on-hook" ([AT&T, Notes on the Network, 1980, section 5, paragraph 7.36](https://archive.org/details/bitsavers_westernEleesonTheNetwork1980_26249557)), and Bell Laboratories engineers wrote in 1978 that signalling on "the circuits which are used by customers" made the network "vulnerable to interference and susceptible to fraud" ([A. E. Ritchie and J. Z. Menard, "Common Channel Interoffice Signaling: An Overview", Bell System Technical Journal, February 1978](https://archive.org/details/bstj57-2-221)). A patent of 1992 described how fraudsters played coin-deposit tones "via the transmitter of the coin telephone" ([H. Weaver Jordan, US Patent 5,150,403, granted 22 September 1992](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5150403A/en)). The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) warned in 2008 that "a single compromise via a modem could allow an attacker direct, undetected access to a network that avoids perimeter security" ([NIST, Special Publication (SP) 800-115, Technical Guide to Information Security Testing and Assessment, September 2008, appendix D](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-115.pdf)). Security Research Labs (SRLabs) called the versatility of the Universal Serial Bus its Achilles heel: "Since different device classes can plug into the same connectors, one type of device can turn into a more capable or malicious type without the user noticing" ([SRLabs, "USB peripherals can turn against their users", 31 July 2014](https://srlabs.de/blog/usb-peripherals-turn)). And the designer of the Proxmark3, an instrument for radio-frequency identification (RFID), wrote of a trace read from a 125 kHz proximity card that "we might just replay it verbatim, to clone the card" ([Jonathan Westhues, Proxmark3 page](https://cq.cx/proxmark3.pl)).

| Medium | What it trusted | Device that spoke to it | What closed or narrowed it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Long-distance trunks | Any pure tone on the voice path | Blue box | Common-channel signalling, from 1976 ([Dahlbom and Ryan, 1978](https://archive.org/details/bstj57-2-225)) |
| Payphone coin lines | Coin tones arriving through the handset | Red box | Coin-tone sensing at the transmitter, electronic coin sets ([US Patent 5,150,403](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5150403A/en)) |
| Dial-up lines | Modems answering behind the perimeter | War dialer | Inventory and yearly war dialing by the owner ([NIST SP 800-115](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-115.pdf)) |
| Wi-Fi | Management frames without protection | Rogue access points, deauthentication tools | Protected Management Frames, required by Wi-Fi Protected Access 3 ([Wi-Fi Alliance](https://www.wi-fi.org/discover-wi-fi/security)) |
| Universal Serial Bus | Whatever device class a plugged device claims | Keystroke injectors, BadUSB | Device-class and device-identifier allow lists, which SRLabs says "can easily be bypassed" ([SRLabs, 2014](https://srlabs.de/blog/usb-peripherals-turn)) |
| 125 kHz cards | A fixed identifier read over the air | Proxmark and its descendants | Cards with cryptography, covered in part 9 |

The fixes in the last column share a design: they stop the medium itself from carrying authority. Common-channel signalling moved control off the voice path; Protected Management Frames sign frames that anyone in radio range could once forge; a cryptographic card proves a secret instead of reciting a number. Where the fix is a list or a filter rather than a change of design, as with USB, the sources describe it as partial.

## The five eras

### 1. Tone boxes (1950s to 1990s)

The long-distance network signalled in the voice band because its long trunks had no direct-current path: a steady 2600 Hz meant an idle trunk, and digits travelled as pairs of tones between 700 and 1700 Hz, both described in the *Bell System Technical Journal* in 1954 and 1960 ([A. Weaver and N. A. Newell, "In-Band Single-Frequency Signaling", November 1954](https://archive.org/details/bstj33-6-1309); [C. Breen and C. A. Dahlbom, "Signaling Systems for Control of Telephone Switching", November 1960](https://archive.org/details/bstj39-6-1381)). The blue box generated those tones; in Ron Rosenbaum's 1971 *Esquire* article, a phreak explained that "as long as you get the frequency within thirty cycles per second of the phone company's tones, the phone equipment thinks it hears its own voice talking to it" ([Ron Rosenbaum, "Secrets of the Little Blue Box", Esquire, October 1971, republished by Slate on 7 October 2011](https://www.slate.com/articles/technology/the_spectator/2011/10/the_article_that_inspired_steve_jobs_secrets_of_the_little_blue_.html)). Payphones joined in 1977, when Automated Coin Toll Service began listening for dual-frequency coin beeps, first in Phoenix on 26 November 1977 ([M. Berger and others, "Traffic Service Position System No. 1: Automated Coin Toll Service", Bell System Technical Journal, July to August 1979](https://archive.org/details/bstj58-6-1207)), and the red box imitated those beeps. The people of this era are in [Phreaking: the network that whistled its own passwords](/learn/phone-phreaking-and-the-blue-box); the signalling tables, every colour of box, the defences, the end of the boxes and Brazil's own token-and-card story are in part 1, [Tone boxes: the blue box, the red box and in-band signalling](/learn/tone-boxes-the-blue-box-the-red-box-and-in-band-signalling).

The era ended by design. Common Channel Interoffice Signaling (CCIS) entered service in May 1976 between Madison, Wisconsin, and Chicago ([Ritchie and Menard, 1978](https://archive.org/details/bstj57-2-221)), and its designers wrote that "fraudulent manipulation of the telephone network is eliminated in an all CCIS environment" ([C. A. Dahlbom and J. S. Ryan, "Common Channel Interoffice Signaling: History and Description of a New Signaling System", Bell System Technical Journal, February 1978](https://archive.org/details/bstj57-2-225)).

### 2. War dialers and wardriving (1980s to 2000s)

When computers answered telephones, the device became the dialler. The Jargon File, the hacker community's dictionary, derives "war dialer" from "wargames dialer", after the film *WarGames*, and defines it as a program "that calls a given list or range of phone numbers and records those which answer with handshake tones", one that evolved from the earlier "demon dialers" of the blue-box days ([The Jargon File, version 4.4.7, entries "war dialer" and "demon dialer"](https://jargon-file.org/archive/jargon-4.4.7.dos.txt)). Its existing glossary entry is [war dialing](/glossary/war-dialing). By 2008 the technique was official practice: NIST's guide to security testing says that software packages let network administrators, and attackers, dial large blocks of numbers in search of modems, that "a computer with four modems can dial 10,000 numbers in a matter of days", and that "organizations should conduct war dialing at least once per year" to find their unauthorized modems ([NIST SP 800-115, September 2008, appendix D](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-115.pdf)).

Wireless networks moved the search from the telephone book to the street. The Jargon File defines war-driving, a play on war dialer, as "driving around looking for unsecured wireless Internet access points to connect to" ([The Jargon File, 4.4.7](https://jargon-file.org/archive/jargon-4.4.7.dos.txt)), and at DEF CON 9, held from 13 to 15 July 2001 at the Alexis Park in Las Vegas, Peter Shipley spoke on "802.11b War Driving and LAN Jacking" ([DEF CON, DEF CON 9 archive](https://defcon.org/html/links/dc-archives/dc-9-archive.html)). See the glossary entry [war driving](/glossary/war-driving). From there the device became the rogue access point, the subject of part 3, [The WiFi Pineapple: rogue access points and the Karma attack](/learn/the-wifi-pineapple-rogue-access-points-and-the-karma-attack), and the defence became cryptographic: the Wi-Fi Alliance says Wi-Fi Protected Access 3 (WPA3) networks "require use of Protected Management Frames (PMF)" and that "PMF is required for all new certified devices" ([Wi-Fi Alliance, "Security"](https://www.wi-fi.org/discover-wi-fi/security)); the protocol background is in [KRACK and Dragonblood](/learn/krack-and-the-wifi-handshake) and the glossary entry [WPA3](/glossary/wpa3).

### 3. USB and implants (2010 onwards)

The third era moved inside the building, through the USB port. Hak5, the company that is the subject of part 2, [Hak5: from a video show to a hacking-gear company](/learn/hak5-from-a-video-show-to-a-hacking-gear-company), says keystroke injection was pioneered "in 2010 with the USB Rubber Ducky", a technique its founder Darren Kitchen used to automate mundane tasks at his information technology job; the same documentation dates the first Rubber Ducky hardware to 2011, gives DuckyScript 1.0 as 2010 in one paragraph and 2011 in another, and dates the Bash Bunny to 2017 and the second Rubber Ducky with DuckyScript 3.0 to 2022 ([Hak5, USB Rubber Ducky documentation](https://docs.hak5.org/hak5-usb-rubber-ducky)). The principle is the trust a computer gives a keyboard. In July 2014 Karsten Nohl and Jakob Lell of SRLabs showed that the controller chips in ordinary USB devices could be reprogrammed so that "a device can emulate a keyboard and issue commands on behalf of the logged-in user", and presented it at Black Hat USA 2014 as "BadUSB - On Accessories that Turn Evil" ([SRLabs, 31 July 2014](https://srlabs.de/blog/usb-peripherals-turn); [InfoCon, Black Hat USA 2014, "BadUSB - On Accessories that Turn Evil"](https://infocondb.org/con/black-hat/black-hat-usa-2014/badusb-on-accessories-that-turn-evil)). The same idea later went into cables: Hak5's documentation credits Mischief Gadgets with the O.MG platform "of malicious cables and adapters" ([Hak5, USB Rubber Ducky documentation](https://docs.hak5.org/hak5-usb-rubber-ducky)), and the O.MG site describes O.MG Cables as "hand made USB cables with an advance WiFi implant inside", "Designed to allow Red Teams to emulate sophisticated attack scenarios" ([O.MG, o.mg.lol](https://o.mg.lol/)). Part 4, [Keystroke injection: the Rubber Ducky, the Bash Bunny, BadUSB and the O.MG Cable](/learn/keystroke-injection-rubber-ducky-bash-bunny-badusb-and-the-om-g-cable), reads a DuckyScript listing line by line; part 5, [Keeping rogue devices off the network: 802.1X, NAC, port security and physical audits](/learn/keeping-rogue-devices-off-the-network-802-1x-nac-port-security-and-physical-audits), covers the defences against unauthorized boxes on a network cable or between a keyboard and a computer, from [802.1X](/glossary/802-1x) to physical audits, and the last part, [Network implants: LAN Turtle, Packet Squirrel, Shark Jack and Key Croc](/learn/network-implants-lan-turtle-packet-squirrel-shark-jack-and-key-croc) (in preparation), the boxes themselves.

### 4. Radio and badges (2007 onwards)

The fourth era made radio cheap. In May 2007 Jonathan Westhues released the design of the Proxmark3, which "can do almost anything involving almost any kind of low-(~125 kHz) or high-(~13.56 MHz) frequency RFID tag": read it, eavesdrop on a reader, or pretend to be a tag itself; his page notes that a group in Nijmegen used a Proxmark3 to demonstrate several attacks against MIFARE Classic ([Westhues, Proxmark3](https://cq.cx/proxmark3.pl)). In early 2012 a television dongle became a software-defined radio (SDR) receiver: Antti Palosaari found that the ezcap EzTV 668, built on the Realtek RTL2832U chip, could stream raw in-phase and quadrature (I/Q) samples to the computer, and Steve Markgraf of the OsmoSDR project soon wrote the rtl-sdr software to tune it, as the radio software developer Alexandru Csete recorded on 2 April 2012 ([Alexandru Csete, "RTL2832U based software defined radios", 2 April 2012](https://www.oz9aec.net/radios/gnu-radio/rtl2832u-based-software-defined-radios)). Great Scott Gadgets describes its HackRF One as "a Software Defined Radio peripheral capable of transmission or reception of radio signals from 1 MHz to 6 GHz" and "an open source hardware platform" ([Great Scott Gadgets, HackRF One](https://greatscottgadgets.com/hackrf/one/)). At DEF CON 23, held from 6 to 9 August 2015, Samy Kamkar presented "Drive It Like You Hacked It: New Attacks and Tools to Wirelessly Steal Cars" ([DEF CON, DEF CON 23 archive](https://defcon.org/html/links/dc-archives/dc-23-archive.html)). Radio, badges and near-field communication (NFC) are taken in depth by part 8, [Radio for hackers: SDR, HackRF, RTL-SDR and rolling codes](/learn/radio-for-hackers-sdr-hackrf-rtl-sdr-and-rolling-codes), and part 9, [Badges and cards: RFID, NFC, the Proxmark and MIFARE](/learn/badges-and-cards-rfid-nfc-the-proxmark-and-mifare), with the [replay attack](/glossary/replay-attack) as their common thread and the spectrum background in [The radio spectrum](/learn/radio-spectrum).

### 5. The pocket multitool (2020 onwards)

The fifth era put the previous ones in one case. Flipper Zero, a portable tool that supports, in BleepingComputer's description, "RFID emulation, digital access key cloning, radio communications, NFC, infrared, Bluetooth, and more" ([BleepingComputer, 11 March 2023](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/brazil-seizing-flipper-zero-shipments-to-prevent-use-in-crime/)) raised "a whopping $1 million" on its first Kickstarter day in 2020 and "nearly $5 million" by 28 August 2020, when Pavel Zhovner, later named as Flipper Devices' chief executive, posted that the campaign had 20 hours left ([Pavel Zhovner, Flipper Zero project log, Hackaday.io, 28 August 2020](https://hackaday.io/project/170875-flipper-zero-multi-tool-device-for-hackers/log/182857-20-hours-till-the-end-of-campaign); [BleepingComputer, 7 April 2023](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/technology/flipper-zero-banned-by-amazon-for-being-a-card-skimming-device-/amp/)). Restrictions followed. In March 2023 Brazil's Agência Nacional de Telecomunicações (Anatel) began holding imported units at customs and rejecting every request to homologate it, citing the rule that an application must be refused when "o produto se prestar a fins ilícitos, ou concorrer à facilitação de crime ou contravenção penal" (the product lends itself to unlawful ends or contributes to facilitating a crime or misdemeanour) ([Anatel, Resolução 715, 23 October 2019, article 60, II](https://informacoes.anatel.gov.br/legislacao/resolucoes/2019/1350-resolucao-715); [BleepingComputer, "Brazil seizing Flipper Zero shipments to prevent use in crime", 11 March 2023](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/brazil-seizing-flipper-zero-shipments-to-prevent-use-in-crime/)); by 17 March 2023 Anatel, working with the Receita Federal, had seized more than 340 units ([Convergência Digital, "Anatel apreendeu mais de 340 Flipper Zero importados por suposto uso criminoso", 17 March 2023](https://convergenciadigital.com.br/telecom/anatel-apreendeu-mais-de-340-flipper-zero-importados-por-suposto-uso-criminoso)). In April 2023 Amazon removed it from its store, telling sellers that "Amazon policy prohibits the sale or listing of card skimming devices" ([BleepingComputer, "Flipper Zero banned by Amazon for being a 'card skimming device'", 7 April 2023](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/technology/flipper-zero-banned-by-amazon-for-being-a-card-skimming-device-/amp/)). On 8 February 2024 Public Safety Canada announced that the government would pursue "all avenues to ban devices used to steal vehicles by copying the wireless signals for remote keyless entry", naming the Flipper Zero ([Public Safety Canada, news release on the National Summit on Combatting Auto Theft, 8 February 2024](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-safety-canada/news/2024/02/government-of-canada-hosts-national-summit-on-combatting-auto-theft.html)).

Restriction produced alternatives. In Brazil the High Boy, a project created on Hackaday.io on 15 April 2025 and built on the ESP32-S3, describes itself as "open source, open hardware" and says it began because "in some countries especially in Brazil the Flipper has become restricted"; by November 2025 it had reached Kickstarter ([High Boy, "High Boy: The Brazilian Answer to the Flipper Zero", Hackaday.io, project page and logs of 2025](https://hackaday.io/project/202872-high-boy-the-brazilian-answer-to-the-flipper-zero)). Part 6, [The Flipper Zero: sub-GHz, RFID, NFC, infrared and the panic](/learn/the-flipper-zero-sub-ghz-rfid-nfc-infrared-and-the-panic), reads Flipper Devices' own documentation and each ban; part 7, [High Boy, ESP32 Marauder and the Flipper's alternatives](/learn/high-boy-esp32-marauder-and-the-flipper-alternatives), follows the Brazilian project, its maker and the ESP32 devices around it.

## The dual-use question

Every device in this series is used by attackers and by the people paid to stop them, and the sources say so in both directions. NIST describes war dialers as tools that network administrators and attackers alike use, and tells organizations to run them against themselves ([NIST SP 800-115, 2008](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-115.pdf)). Hak5 states that its material "is for education, authorized auditing and analysis purposes where permitted subject to local and international laws" and that "Users are solely responsible for compliance" ([Hak5, USB Rubber Ducky documentation](https://docs.hak5.org/hak5-usb-rubber-ducky)). The O.MG site presents its cables as red-team equipment ([o.mg.lol](https://o.mg.lol/)).

The case against the devices is made from what they are used for. Anatel, in a note quoted by Convergência Digital, said its preliminary studies found that "o Flipper Zero e produtos similares podem possuir potencial lesivo aos usuários" (the Flipper Zero and similar products may have the potential to harm users), and that copying gate remotes, home security systems and car keys without the owner's consent "pode resultar em prejuízos pessoais e financeiros" (can result in personal and financial losses) ([Convergência Digital, 17 March 2023](https://convergenciadigital.com.br/telecom/anatel-apreendeu-mais-de-340-flipper-zero-importados-por-suposto-uso-criminoso)). Canada's industry minister, François-Philippe Champagne, wrote that week that "we are banning the importation, sale and use of consumer hacking devices, like flippers, used to commit these crimes", as BleepingComputer reported ([BleepingComputer, "Canada to ban the Flipper Zero to stop surge in car thefts", 9 February 2024](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/canada-wants-to-ban-the-flipper-zero-to-stop-surge-in-car-thefts/)).

The case for the devices is made from what they reveal. The Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) argued against Anatel's seizures that the Flipper Zero "has clear uses: penetration testing to facilitate hardening of a home network or organizational infrastructure, hardware research, security research, protocol development, use by radio hobbyists, and many more", and that "the creation, possession or distribution of tools related to security research should not be criminalized or otherwise restricted" ([BleepingComputer, 11 March 2023, quoting the EFF](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/brazil-seizing-flipper-zero-shipments-to-prevent-use-in-crime/)). Flipper Devices' chief operating officer, Alex Kulagin, told BleepingComputer that the device "can't be used to hijack any car, specifically the ones produced after the 1990s, since their security systems have rolling codes", and that capturing a car's signal would require "actively blocking the signal from the owner", which its hardware cannot do ([BleepingComputer, 9 February 2024](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/canada-wants-to-ban-the-flipper-zero-to-stop-surge-in-car-thefts/)). Those are a manufacturer's claims, examined in parts 6 and 8.

### Where the law draws the line

Most legislators settled the question by intent rather than by device.

- **European Union.** Directive 2013/40/EU of 12 August 2013 requires member states to punish the production, sale, procurement for use, import or distribution of tools "with the intention that it be used to commit" the offences of illegal access, system or data interference and interception ([EU, Directive 2013/40/EU on attacks against information systems, article 7](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32013L0040)). Its recital 16 explains why: a tool may have been "produced for a legitimate purpose", and to avoid criminalizing tools made "to test the reliability of information technology products or the security of information systems", a "direct intent" that they be used for an offence must be proved; recital 17 excludes liability for "mandated testing or protection of information systems" ([Directive 2013/40/EU, recitals 16 and 17](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32013L0040)). The directive says it "builds on" the Council of Europe's 2001 Convention on Cybercrime ([recital 15](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32013L0040)).
- **Brazil.** Lei 12.737 of 30 November 2012 added article 154-A to the Código Penal, the offence of invading a computer device, and its paragraph 1 applies the same penalty to whoever "produz, oferece, distribui, vende ou difunde dispositivo ou programa de computador com o intuito de permitir a prática da conduta definida no caput", that is, produces, offers, distributes, sells or spreads a device or program with the intent of enabling the invasion ([Senado Federal, Lei 12.737, 30 November 2012](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/588113/publicacao/15773241)). Lei 14.155 of 27 May 2021 rewrote the offence and raised its penalty to "reclusão, de 1 (um) a 4 (quatro) anos, e multa", one to four years' imprisonment and a fine, without amending paragraph 1 ([Senado Federal, Lei 14.155, 27 May 2021](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/34016289/publicacao/34016562)). Brazil also promulgated the Convention on Cybercrime by Decreto 11.491 of 12 April 2023; it entered into force for Brazil on 1 March 2023 ([Senado Federal, Decreto 11.491, 12 April 2023](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/36966539/publicacao/36967744)). Separately, Anatel decides which radio products may be sold at all, through homologation ([Anatel, Resolução 715, 2019](https://informacoes.anatel.gov.br/legislacao/resolucoes/2019/1350-resolucao-715)); see the glossary entry [Anatel](/glossary/anatel).
- **United States.** 18 U.S.C. 1029 punishes, among other things, whoever "knowingly and with intent to defraud" possesses or deals in "a telecommunications instrument that has been modified or altered to obtain unauthorized use of telecommunications services", or a "scanning receiver", and whoever knowingly deals in hardware or software configured to alter a telecommunications instrument's identifying information; for that last offence, except when the charge is producing or trafficking, the statute gives an affirmative defence that the conduct "was engaged in for research or development in connection with a lawful purpose" ([Legal Information Institute, 18 U.S. Code 1029, paragraphs (a)(7) to (a)(9) and (g)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1029)).

Anatel's homologation rule and Canada's 2024 announcement are different instruments: they act on the product's entry into the market rather than on a user's intent. A later part of this series, on owning hacking gear legally, reads those rules in full: Anatel homologation and Receita Federal imports, the Código Penal's article 154-A and Lei 12.737, 18 U.S.C. 1029, the Federal Communications Commission (FCC), the EU Radio Equipment Directive, Canada's 2024 move and the rules of engagement for red teams. This summary reflects the texts as read on 16 November 2026 and is not legal advice.

## What defenders take from the whole series

- **Take authority off the medium.** Each lasting fix in the sources changed the design: control moved to a separate channel ([Dahlbom and Ryan, 1978](https://archive.org/details/bstj57-2-225)), management frames gained protection ([Wi-Fi Alliance](https://www.wi-fi.org/discover-wi-fi/security)), cards that recite a number gave way to cards that prove a secret (part 9).
- **Test yourself with the same tools.** NIST's guide asks organizations to war dial themselves yearly and to scan for wireless rogue devices ([NIST SP 800-115, 2008](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-115.pdf)).
- **Treat lists and filters as partial.** SRLabs says device-class allow lists "can easily be bypassed" and that "No effective defenses from USB attacks are known" as of 2014 ([SRLabs, 2014](https://srlabs.de/blog/usb-peripherals-turn)); parts 4 and 5 read what operating systems and network access control have added since.
- **Watch the money and the logs.** The telephone companies caught blue-box users through billing anomalies and coin-call records long before they redesigned the network ([Rosenbaum, 1971](https://www.slate.com/articles/technology/the_spectator/2011/10/the_article_that_inspired_steve_jobs_secrets_of_the_little_blue_.html); [Berger and others, 1979](https://archive.org/details/bstj58-6-1207)).

## What to remember

- A hacking device appears wherever a protocol trusts whatever reaches its medium: tones on a trunk, beeps from a handset, a modem answering behind the firewall, a frame in the air, a keyboard on a USB port, a number on a card.
- The five eras: tone boxes (1950s to 1990s), war dialers and wardriving (1980s to 2000s), USB and implants (2010 onwards), radio and badges (2007 onwards), the pocket multitool (2020 onwards).
- The lasting fixes changed the design, taking authority off the medium; lists and filters were partial.
- The same device serves attack and defence: NIST tells organizations to war dial themselves, and vendors sell to red teams.
- The EU, Brazil and the United States punish tools by intent; Anatel's homologation and Canada's 2024 announcement act on the market instead.

<ReaderQuestions>

- If every device in this series exploits a protocol that trusts its medium, should the law target the devices, the people who misuse them, or the makers of protocols that still trust the medium?
- Brazil refused homologation of the Flipper Zero in 2023 while its own makers built an ESP32-based alternative. Does a market restriction reduce misuse, or move it to devices that are harder to see?
- NIST told organizations in 2008 to war dial themselves every year. What would the equivalent instruction be today for USB ports, badges and the radio around a building?
- Flipper Devices says rolling codes protect cars made after the 1990s, and Canada named the device in its auto-theft plan. Which evidence would settle that dispute, and who holds it?
- The EU directive protects tools made for "mandated testing". How should a researcher who is not mandated by anyone show that intent?
