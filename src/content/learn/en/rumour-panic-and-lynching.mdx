A rumour needs no author. It needs a subject people care about and a gap where the facts should be, and it fills the gap with a story that travels from person to person, changing as it goes. Most rumours fade. Some move money: depositors queue at a bank because others are queuing, and the bank that was sound fails because they queued. Some move shoppers, who empty shelves of goods that were never scarce until everyone feared they would be. And some move crowds to kill. This part of the series introduced by [the opener on mass hysteria](/learn/mass-hysteria-how-groups-come-to-believe-anything) follows those three outcomes. It starts with the first systematic psychology of rumour, published by Gordon Allport and Leo Postman in 1947, and with what later research kept of it; then it follows a countryside in panic in France in 1789, a radio play in the United States (US) in 1938 and its imitators in Ecuador and Brazil, bank runs from the theory that won a Nobel prize to Northern Rock, Silicon Valley Bank and a run on Brazil's main welfare programme in 2013, the panic buying of March 2020, and lynchings that began with a rumour, from the US South of the 1890s to Guarujá, on the São Paulo coast, in 2014, and to India in 2017 and 2018. How networks carry rumours today is the subject of [the part on reach](/learn/from-the-lodge-to-the-network-how-reach-changed); the mechanisms of conformity and cascades are in [the part on the mechanisms](/learn/conformity-cascades-and-contagion-the-mechanisms). After reading, you can say what makes a rumour spread, tell a run that fulfilled its own prophecy from a panic that was mostly a newspaper story, and read what police, courts and inquiries found in each case.

## What a rumour is: Allport and Postman, 1947

### The definition and the "basic law"

The psychologists Gordon Allport and Leo Postman, drawing largely on the rumours of the Second World War, defined a rumour as "a specific (or topical) proposition for belief, passed along from person to person, usually by word of mouth, without secure standards of evidence being present" ([Allport and Postman, The Psychology of Rumor, New York, Henry Holt, 1947, 1948 printing](https://archive.org/download/psychology_of_rumor/psychology_of_rumor_djvu.txt)). Two conditions, they argued, are necessary: the subject "must have some importance to speaker and listener", and "the true facts must be shrouded in some kind of ambiguity". They wrote this as a formula, R ~ i × a, the amount of rumour in circulation varying "with the importance of the subject to the individuals concerned times the ambiguity of the evidence"; the relation is multiplicative because "if either importance or ambiguity is zero, there is no rumor". Their examples: no American spreads rumours about the price of camels in Afghanistan, which is ambiguous but unimportant to him, and nobody spreads rumours about the size of a legacy whose amount he knows. They also noted that "a person who is "rumor wise," who understands that he is likely under conditions of importance and ambiguity to believe and spread rumors, is for that very reason less likely to do so!" ([Allport and Postman, 1947](https://archive.org/download/psychology_of_rumor/psychology_of_rumor_djvu.txt)).

### Leveling, sharpening, assimilation

Their experiments used serial reproduction: one person described a picture to another, who passed the description on, six or seven times. "The curve, based on eleven experiments, shows that about 70 percent of the details are eliminated in the course of five or six mouth-to-mouth transmissions even when there is virtually no time lapse"; initial descriptions of twenty or more details shrank to about five ([Allport and Postman, 1947](https://archive.org/download/psychology_of_rumor/psychology_of_rumor_djvu.txt)). They called the loss *leveling*, the selective emphasis of what remained *sharpening*, and the bending of the story towards the teller's expectations *assimilation*. Their most cited result is an example of assimilation to prejudice: in a picture of a subway car in which a white man held a razor, "in more than half the experiments the razor in Figure 7 moves (in the telling) from the white man's hand to the black man's"; the authors would not say whether this reflected hatred, noting that the falsification "might well occur even among subjects who have no active anti-Negro bias" because the stereotype was so widely and unthinkingly accepted.

They were open about the limits of the laboratory. Part of the rapid leveling, they wrote, was "due in part, as we have said, to the audience effect in our experimental design", and real rumours often keep "a "kernel of truth"" that is overlaid "with fanciful elaboration" ([Allport and Postman, 1947](https://archive.org/download/psychology_of_rumor/psychology_of_rumor_djvu.txt)). They also used field data. A classification by Robert Knapp of 1,000 rumours collected across the US in the summer of 1942 found that "approximately two-thirds of all rumors current in 1942 were hostile in their intent and divisive in their effects", "wedge drivers" aimed at Jews, Black Americans, the administration or the armed forces; against them, the *Boston Herald-Traveler* ran a "rumor clinic" from March 1942 to December 1943, and the idea "was imitated in more than 40 newspapers" in the US and Canada.

### What later research kept

Two generations later, researchers at Facebook and Stanford University used Snopes.com, which they describe as "a popular website documenting memes and urban legends", to "track the propagation of thousands of rumors appearing on Facebook" ([Friggeri and others, Rumor Cascades, 2014](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/14559/14559-28-18077-1-2-20201228.pdf)). Adrien Friggeri, Lada Adamic, Dean Eckles and Justin Cheng found that "rumor cascades run deeper in the social network than reshare cascades in general", that a comment linking to a Snopes article "increases the likelihood that a reshare of a rumor will be deleted", but that "large cascades are able to accumulate hundreds of Snopes comments while continuing to propagate", and that rumours copied from one status update to another "change over time" in variants ([Friggeri, Adamic, Eckles and Cheng, Rumor Cascades, 2014](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/14559/14559-28-18077-1-2-20201228.pdf)). The larger studies of true and false news on Twitter, and the dispute over them, are set out in [the part on reach](/learn/from-the-lodge-to-the-network-how-reach-changed); why a rumour survives its correction is the subject of [the part on why misbeliefs persist](/learn/why-misbeliefs-persist).

### Rumour and riot

Allport and Postman went further on violence than on anything else. "In no case can we claim that hearsay is the sole or original cause of a riot, yet it seems always to play an important ancillary role"; they proposed "as a law of social psychology that no riot ever occurs without rumors to incite, accompany, and intensify the violence" ([Allport and Postman, 1947](https://archive.org/download/psychology_of_rumor/psychology_of_rumor_djvu.txt)). They described four stages from the riots of the summer of 1943: murmurs of unrest; rumours that "assume a specifically threatening form"; an inflammatory rumour as the spark, as in Harlem in August 1943, where a Black soldier wounded in an altercation was rumoured to have been shot in the back and killed; and, during the riot, rumours that "fly faster than ever", with the Detroit police "flooded with calls reporting alleged incidents", one of which, a woman's eyewitness report of a killing, turned out on inspection to be girls playing hopscotch. They also recorded a counter-example: in Washington the same summer, police who took "a firm public stand in advance" and protected a parade that rumour said would be the occasion of an uprising "were able to forestall the threatened clash".

## The Great Fear, July and August 1789

### Brigands who never came

In the summer of 1789 a fear of brigands swept much of rural France. The *Encyclopædia Universalis* entry by the historian Jean Tulard summarizes it: in countryside suffering from bad harvests, "se propagent alors d'étranges rumeurs : des bandes de vagabonds sont transformées par l'imagination populaire en armées de brigands" (strange rumours spread: bands of vagrants were turned by popular imagination into armies of brigands); the alarm passed from village to village, the tocsin rang, peasants armed and formed militias, and "Faute de brigands, les campagnards se retournent contre les châteaux", for want of brigands they turned on the châteaux, pillaging them and burning the charters that recorded feudal dues ([Tulard, Grande Peur (1789), Encyclopædia Universalis](https://www.universalis.fr/encyclopedie/grande-peur/)). Georges Lefebvre's study of 1932, *La Grande Peur de 1789*, distinguished, in Tulard's summary, six main centres (Franche-Comté, Champagne, the Beauvaisis, Maine, the region of Nantes and the South-West) from which panic spread between 20 July and 6 August "dans la plus grande partie de la France", while Lorraine, most of Normandy, Brittany, Flanders and much of the South largely escaped.

### What the archives keep

The local record shows how a single event could start a regional panic. A document in the departmental archives of Haute-Saône, published by the archives of the Jura, describes the "drame de Quincey": on the evening of 19 July 1789, during celebrations of Necker's recall, an explosion at the château of Quincey killed four people and destroyed the building, and "Cette sanglante catastrophe donne le signal de la Grande Peur dans la province" ([Archives départementales du Jura, La Révolution dans les archives comtoises, document 9, AD Haute-Saône 454 L 2/3](https://archives39.fr/document/la-revolution-dans-les-archives-comtoises)). In the South-West, a local history society found the panic in municipal registers: at Verfeil, on 30 July 1789, the consuls met in emergency session because "plusieurs personnes mal intentionnées" were said to threaten the area, and the society's conclusion is that the Great Fear "a été un événement bien réel qui a été pris très au sérieux", though relatively moderate around Toulouse ([Lettre 60, local history bulletin of the 2a31 association](https://www.2a31.net/lettres/Lettre_60.pdf)).

### Who was behind it

Contemporaries saw a plot. The publisher's summary of Lefebvre's book puts it plainly: "Aux contemporains déconcertés, elle apparut comme un mystère. Ceux qui voulurent, à toutes forces, en improviser une explication l'attribuèrent à un complot qu'ils rapportèrent, suivant leurs opinions, à l'aristocratie ou aux révolutionnaires": to bewildered contemporaries it was a mystery, and those who insisted on explaining it blamed a plot by the aristocracy or by the revolutionaries, according to their opinions ([Dunod, La grande peur de 1789, 3rd edition, January 2021](https://dunod.com/histoire-geographie-et-sciences-politiques/grande-peur-1789)). <Claim status="disputed" by="by historians since the 1930s">Lefebvre read it as a spontaneous collective movement; Bernard Faÿ attributed it to agitators working for the Duke of Orléans</Claim> ([Universalis](https://www.universalis.fr/encyclopedie/grande-peur/)). Timothy Tackett's later article on the Great Fear and the idea of an aristocratic plot, in the *Annales historiques de la Révolution française*, could not be read for this part.

The consequence is better documented than the cause. On the evening of 4 August 1789 the Vicomte de Noailles told the National Assembly that the draft decree before it aimed "d'arrêter l'effervescence des provinces", to halt the ferment in the provinces, and asked how this could be done "sans connaître quelle est la cause de l'insurrection qui se manifeste dans le royaume"; his first proposals were that taxes be paid by everyone in proportion to income and that public charges be borne equally by all ([Archives parlementaires, tome VIII, séance du 4 août 1789, motion du vicomte de Noailles](https://www.persee.fr/doc/arcpa_0000-0000_1875_num_8_1_4791_t2_0343_0000_7)). Deputies from province after province renounced their privileges that night ([Archives parlementaires, tome VIII, pp. 346 to 349](https://www.persee.fr/doc/arcpa_0000-0000_1875_num_8_1_4793_t2_0346_0000_16)). Tulard's verdict is that the revolt, "en provoquant à l'Assemblée nationale la célèbre séance de la nuit du 4 août 1789, a entraîné la ruine de l'Ancien Régime" ([Universalis](https://www.universalis.fr/encyclopedie/grande-peur/)).

## The War of the Worlds: a panic and its size

### What the newspapers said the next morning

On the evening of Sunday 30 October 1938, Orson Welles's Mercury Theatre broadcast an adaptation of H. G. Wells's *The War of the Worlds* on the Columbia Broadcasting System (CBS), told as a series of news bulletins about Martians landing in New Jersey. The Washington *Evening Star* carried on its front page the next day the story "U. S. May Act to Control Horror Radio Plays After War Scare": the Associated Press (AP) carried "reports of the terror" from "practically every State", newspapers and police were "swamped with telephone calls", and "The Star alone received more than 400 queries" ([Evening Star, Washington, 31 October 1938, p. A-1](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045462/1938-10-31/ed-1/?sp=1)). The continuation reported people praying in groups, church services broken up, motorists fleeing the New York area, an actress who "became panic-stricken", fell and broke her arm, and a Senator, Clyde Herring of Iowa, who said the matter was not "washed up" as far as he was concerned ([Evening Star, 31 October 1938, p. A-3](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045462/1938-10-31/ed-1/?sp=3)). The following day the paper reported that the chairman of the Federal Communications Commission (FCC), Frank McNinch, had received a transcript of the broadcast, and quoted a psychologist who traced the "widespread hysteria" to the same "mass case of jitters" that produced jitterbug dancers ([Evening Star, 1 November 1938, p. A-3](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045462/1938-11-01/ed-1/?sp=3)).

### What the audience data show

The size of the panic has been disputed since. In 1940 the Princeton psychologist Hadley Cantril, in *The Invasion from Mars*, estimated that about a million people had been frightened, relying on a survey by the American Institute of Public Opinion (AIPO) made six weeks after the broadcast; the media historians Jefferson Pooley and Michael Socolow note that Cantril himself admitted the survey gave an audience rating "over 100 per cent higher than any other known measure of this audience", and that he conflated being "frightened," "disturbed," or "excited" with panic ([Pooley and Socolow, Slate, 29 October 2013](https://slate.com/culture/2013/10/orson-welles-war-of-the-worlds-panic-myth-the-infamous-radio-broadcast-did-not-cause-a-nationwide-hysteria.html)). On the night itself, the C. E. Hooper ratings service telephoned 5,000 households, and "Only 2 percent answered a radio "play" or "the Orson Welles program,"", with none saying a news broadcast; several CBS affiliates had not carried the programme; researchers working under Cantril found no record of any case caused by the broadcast in six New York hospitals; and the FCC issued no ruling or sanction ([Pooley and Socolow, 2013](https://slate.com/culture/2013/10/orson-welles-war-of-the-worlds-panic-myth-the-infamous-radio-broadcast-did-not-cause-a-nationwide-hysteria.html)). <Claim status="attributed" by="by Pooley and Socolow">Their explanation is that newspapers, losing advertising to radio, sensationalized the panic to discredit a rival medium</Claim>.

A. Brad Schwartz read "the almost 2,000 surviving letters concerning the broadcast" sent to the FCC, CBS, Welles and the producer, and found, in Socolow's account of the book, that "Out of 1,974 letters, a total of six people wrote of actually fleeing their homes in terror "because of the broadcast."", with "at least another thirteen" ready to ([Socolow, review of Broadcast Hysteria, Reason, 20 June 2015](https://reason.com/2015/06/20/fake-news-and-false-memories/)). Schwartz does not deny fear: "Even for fully frightened listeners, panic was the exception, not the rule", and "Many were concerned, even terrified, but most were not" ([David Holahan, review of Broadcast Hysteria, The Christian Science Monitor, 21 May 2015](https://www.csmonitor.com/Books/Book-Reviews/2015/0521/Broadcast-Hysteria-sheds-new-light-on-Orson-Welles-War-of-the-Worlds)). <Claim status="disputed">How many Americans panicked</Claim> remains a matter of estimate; that the "nationwide hysteria" of the headlines was far larger than anything the audience data, the hospitals or the letters record is the finding of the research cited here.

### Quito, 1949, and São Luís, 1971

The broadcast was imitated, with worse results. On 12 February 1949 Radio Quito, housed in the building of the newspaper *El Comercio*, broadcast a localized version; the AP reported two days later that "Fifteen persons were killed and at least 15 injured by enraged mobs, angry at the panic that had been caused", that the army used tanks and tear gas, that the newspaper's building was wrecked, and that the government indicted the station's art and drama editors and arrested ten other people ([Evening Star, Washington, 14 February 1949, p. A-2, Associated Press](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045462/1949-02-14/ed-1/?sp=2); [Daily Alaska Empire, 14 February 1949, p. 2](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045499/1949-02-14/ed-1/?sp=2)). A 2021 study in the communication journal *Chasqui* gives a lower toll, "seis muertos", citing a 2006 history, and records that the programme's director, Leonardo Páez, later exiled himself to Venezuela and told his side in a novel ([Rodrigo-Mendizábal, Chasqui 146, April to July 2021](https://revistachasqui.org/index.php/chasqui/article/download/4536/3393/12368)). <Claim status="disputed">The number of dead in Quito</Claim> is not settled: the later historiography gives fewer than half the deaths of the contemporary wire report.

In Brazil, Rádio Difusora of São Luís, in Maranhão, broadcast its own version on 30 October 1971. A 2015 student paper presented at a regional communication conference says it led "toda a população da cidade de São Luís a entrar em pânico", but relies, by its own account, on a single book of interviews published by the Federal University of Maranhão in 2011 ([Sousa and others, Guerra dos Mundos em São Luís, Expocom 2015](https://portalintercom.org.br/anais/nordeste2015/expocom/EX47-1373-1.pdf)). <Claim status="reported" by="by a 2015 student paper drawing on interviews">The São Luís panic</Claim> has not been checked against the press of the time for this part; it is a case to tell with its sourcing problem.

## Bank runs: when the rumour makes itself true

### The model that won a Nobel prize

A bank run is the case in which a rumour can be true because people believe it. Douglas Diamond and Philip Dybvig showed in 1983 that ordinary demand-deposit contracts "have multiple equilibria, one of which is a bank run", and that runs "cause real economic damage, rather than simply reflecting other problems" ([Diamond and Dybvig, Journal of Political Economy 1983, reprinted in the Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis Quarterly Review, Winter 2000](https://www.minneapolisfed.org/research/qr/qr2412.pdf)). Which equilibrium prevails could depend on "a bad earnings report, a commonly observed run at some other bank, a negative government forecast, or even sunspots"; "The observed variable need not convey anything fundamental about the bank's condition. The problem is that once agents have deposited, anything that causes them to anticipate a run will lead to a run." Their remedy was deposit insurance. When Diamond and Dybvig shared the 2022 economics prize with Ben Bernanke, the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences wrote that their analysis showed how banks are "vulnerable to rumours about their imminent collapse", and that if many savers run at once "the rumour may become a self-fulfilling prophecy" ([Nobel Prize, press release, 10 October 2022](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2022/press-release/)).

### Northern Rock, 2007

The run on Northern Rock followed an announcement meant to reassure. The Bank of England's *Financial Stability Report* of October 2007 records that the mortgage lender Northern Rock, unable to fund itself in frozen markets, sought liquidity support; on 14 September a statement confirmed that the Bank stood ready to lend to it, and "While the announcement contained the positive news that Northern Rock had access to a new source of funding, it also confirmed the extent of its difficulties and that led to a retail deposit run"; on the evening of 17 September the Chancellor announced that the government would guarantee all of Northern Rock's existing deposits ([Bank of England, Financial Stability Report, October 2007](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/financial-stability-report/2007/october-2007.pdf)). The report of the House of Commons Treasury Committee, *The run on the Rock* (January 2008), could not be read for this part.

### Silicon Valley Bank, 2023: a run at the speed of a phone

The run on Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) in March 2023 was, by the Federal Reserve's own comparison, far faster than the runs of 2008. Its vice chair for supervision, Michael Barr, wrote that "On March 9, SVB lost over $40 billion in deposits", and that the management of its holding company, SVB Financial Group, "expected to lose over $100 billion more on March 10", about 85% of the deposit base, whereas the failure of Wachovia in 2008 involved about $10 billion of outflows over eight days ([Federal Reserve, Review of the Federal Reserve's Supervision and Regulation of Silicon Valley Bank, 28 April 2023](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/svb-review-20230428.pdf)). The review describes withdrawals by a "concentrated network" of venture capital investors and technology firms "who, fueled by social media, withdrew uninsured deposits in a coordinated manner at an unprecedented rate", and concludes that "the combination of social media, a highly networked and concentrated depositor base, and technology may have fundamentally changed the speed of bank runs". It is equally clear about the cause beneath the run: "Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) failed because of a textbook case of mismanagement by the bank".

Signature Bank failed two days later. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) wrote that after SVB's stock crashed on 9 March "panic spread through texts and social media", and that the same day a short seller alleged that Signature Bank (SBNY) "was involved with FTX and Silvergate in a money laundering scheme", a claim discussed on a Twitter audio conversation and more widely reported on 10 March; withdrawal requests reached 20% of deposits. The bank was, in the FDIC's words, "well known as a bank that provided deposit services to crypto businesses", and FTX, one of those businesses, had failed in November 2022 ([FDIC, FDIC's Supervision of Signature Bank, 28 April 2023](https://www.fdic.gov/sites/default/files/2024-03/pr23033a.pdf)). The FDIC did not treat the rumour as the cause: "the root cause of SBNY's failure was poor management". In both reviews, rumour set the speed and timing of runs at banks that regulators had found weak.

### Brazil, May 2013: a run on a welfare programme

On Saturday 18 and Sunday 19 May 2013 a rumour that the Bolsa Família cash-transfer programme, the government's main social programme, would end sent beneficiaries to branches of the state bank Caixa and to lottery shops in at least twelve states; according to Caixa, about 900,000 withdrawals totalling R$152 million were made ([Congresso em Foco, 12 July 2013](https://congressoemfoco.com.br/noticias/pf-descarta-crime-sobre-boatos-do-fim-do-bolsa-familia)). The Minister of Social Development, Tereza Campello, said that on the Saturday "o movimento (no total de saques) foi cinco vezes maior", five times the usual, and that the closure of branches over the weekend made it harder to inform people ([Exame, 21 May 2013](https://exame.com/brasil/movimento-na-caixa-quintuplicou-com-boato-do-bolsa-familia)).

The government at first saw a plot. President Dilma Rousseff called the author of the rumour "desumano e criminoso"; the justice minister, José Eduardo Cardozo, said the action had been deliberate and orchestrated; the human rights minister, Maria do Rosário, blamed the opposition ([Congresso em Foco, 2013](https://congressoemfoco.com.br/noticias/pf-descarta-crime-sobre-boatos-do-fim-do-bolsa-familia)). The Federal Police (PF) investigation, closed on 12 July 2013, found otherwise. It heard 181 people, among them the first beneficiaries to withdraw, and 64 Caixa managers, traced the first abnormal withdrawals to the towns of Ipu (Ceará) and Cajazeiras (Paraíba) in the first hours of Saturday 18 May, found that a social-network post about an early payment "não foi a origem dos boatos", and concluded that the rumour <Claim status="established" by="by the Federal Police's final report">"foi espontâneo não havendo como afirmar que apenas uma pessoa ou grupo tenha os causado"</Claim>, with "inexistência de elementos que possam configurar crime ou contravenção penal" ([Estado de Minas, 12 July 2013](https://www.em.com.br/app/noticia/politica/2013/07/12/interna_politica,421761/pf-conclui-que-boato-do-bolsa-familia-foi-espontaneo.shtml)). The police listed three reasons the rumour took hold: an advance in the payment date "por motivos diversos", talk of an extra payment for Mother's Day, and news that the programme might be cancelled. Caixa, after giving what Congresso em Foco called "informações erradas e confusas" (wrong and confused information), admitted that it had brought forward the date for withdrawals ([Congresso em Foco, 2013](https://congressoemfoco.com.br/noticias/pf-descarta-crime-sobre-boatos-do-fim-do-bolsa-familia)); on 27 May its president, Jorge Hereda, said that the bank's operational area had begun paying on Friday 17 May, the eve of the rumour, to meet a re-registration schedule, that the board had not known, and that beneficiaries had not been told of the advance ([Exame, 27 May 2013](https://exame.com/brasil/caixa-diz-que-nao-sabia-de-antecipacao-do-bolsa-familia)). Here the steering was alleged by the government and not found by its own police.

## Panic buying, March 2020

When the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic reached Europe, supermarket shelves emptied of pasta, rice and toilet paper. A systematic review by Kum Fai Yuen and colleagues grouped the causes proposed in the literature into four: "individuals' perception of the threat of the health crisis and scarcity of products", "fear of the unknown", "coping behaviour, which views panic buying as a venue to relieve anxiety and regain control over the crisis", and "social psychological factors, which account for the influence of the social network of an individual" ([Yuen and others, International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health 17(10), May 2020](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7277661/fullTextXML)).

Purchase records show a different picture from the images of trolleys piled high. Martin O'Connell, Áureo de Paula and Kate Smith used the scanner data of a nationally representative panel of 17,000 British households and found that spending on staples "rose sharply at the end of February, peaking on 14 March at over 80 per cent above the level on the same day in 2019", and on household supplies at nearly 75%; but the spike "was driven by most households increasing their demands rather than by a small number of extreme purchasers", with about 70% of the increase in the most affected categories due to more frequent purchases ([O'Connell, de Paula and Smith, Fiscal Studies 42(2), June 2021](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8209877/fullTextXML)). Wealthier households stocked up more (54% against 33% for the least well-off), and a two-unit limit would have cut the average increase in the 30 most affected categories only from 44% to 34%. In nationally representative surveys in the United Kingdom (UK) and Ireland, Richard Bentall and colleagues found that "About three quarters of participants reported minimal over-purchasing", and that over-purchasing was predicted by income, children at home, distress and mistrust of others ([Bentall and others, PLoS One 16(1), January 2021](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7840055/fullTextXML)). Jonathan David, Shanara Visvalingam and Melissa Norberg, surveying 359 people in Australia and the US, warned that "Some reports inaccurately referred to the panic buying as hoarding", a distinct problem of extreme saving behaviour ([David, Visvalingam and Norberg, Psychiatry Research 303, 2021](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8520319/fullTextXML)). Nobody steered these purchases; the record shows many people each buying a little more because they expected others to.

## Lynching: when a rumour becomes a verdict

### The US South: Ida B. Wells and the counting of lynchings

The journalist Ida B. Wells documented how an accusation, true or false, served as a licence to kill. On 21 May 1892 her Memphis newspaper, *Free Speech*, reported eight lynchings since its last issue, five "on the same old racket--the new alarm about raping white women", and wrote that "Nobody in this section of the country believes the old thread-bare lie"; a meeting at the Cotton Exchange followed, and the paper was destroyed ([Wells, Southern Horrors: Lynch Law in All Its Phases, New York, 1892](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/14975/pg14975.txt)). Citing figures compiled by the *Chicago Tribune*, she counted "728 Afro-Americans lynched during the past eight years", and noted "the fact that only one-third of the 728 victims to mobs have been charged with rape, to say nothing of those of that one-third who were innocent of the charge".

The Equal Justice Initiative (EJI) later "documented 4084 racial terror lynchings in twelve Southern states between the end of Reconstruction in 1877 and 1950, which is at least 800 more" than previously reported; of these victims "nearly 25 percent were accused of sexual assault and nearly 30 percent were accused of murder", and it found more than 300 more in other states ([Equal Justice Initiative, Lynching in America: Confronting the Legacy of Racial Terror, third edition](https://lynchinginamerica.eji.org/drupal/sites/default/files/2021-05/report.pdf)). EJI's website gives the twelve-state total as 4,075 ([EJI, Lynching in America](https://eji.org/reports/lynching-in-america/)). <Claim status="attributed" by="by EJI">EJI characterizes these killings as terrorism, "a widely supported campaign to enforce racial subordination and segregation"</Claim>; the rumours that preceded them were the occasion, and the social order that believed them was the steersman.

### Tulsa, 1921: one newspaper story

The Oklahoma Commission to Study the Tulsa Race Riot of 1921 reported in 2001 on how an incident in an elevator became a massacre. On 30 May 1921 Dick Rowland, a young Black man, entered an elevator operated by Sarah Page; "What happened next is anyone's guess", the most common later explanation being that he tripped and grabbed her arm ([Oklahoma Commission to Study the Tulsa Race Riot of 1921, report, 28 February 2001](https://www.okhistory.org/research/forms/freport.pdf)). On 31 May the *Tulsa Tribune* ran a front-page story headed "Nab Negro for Attacking Girl in Elevator", and, according to witnesses, an editorial about a lynching, torn out of the surviving microfilm; a manager in the city wrote that afterwards "the talk of lynching spread like a prairie fire". The commission found that people, "some of them agents of government", destroyed "homes credibly estimated to have numbered 1,256", that "many people, likely numbering between one and three hundred, were killed", and that "Not one of these criminal acts was then or ever has been prosecuted or punished by government at any level".

### Guarujá, May 2014: a portrait on a Facebook page

On the evening of 3 May 2014, in Morrinhos, a community of 20,000 people on the outskirts of Guarujá, on the São Paulo coast, residents beat Fabiane Maria de Jesus, a 33-year-old housewife and mother, whom they took for a woman said to be kidnapping children for black magic; she died two days later in hospital ([Gazeta do Povo, 6 May 2014](https://www.gazetadopovo.com.br/vida-e-cidadania/mulher-morre-apos-ser-linchada-no-guaruja-8vady88nurzxdlxgekawegvv2)). The Military Police said there was no evidence that she had committed such a crime and no record of the supposed kidnappings. The family's lawyer blamed a local news page on Facebook, "Guarujá Alerta", which had circulated the rumour with a police sketch of the supposed kidnapper; <Claim status="denied" by="by the page's administrators">the page said it had always called the kidnappings "boatos", that the sketch carried the warning "Se é boato ou não, vamos ficar alertas", and that it condemned taking justice into one's own hands</Claim> ([Gazeta do Povo, 2014](https://www.gazetadopovo.com.br/vida-e-cidadania/mulher-morre-apos-ser-linchada-no-guaruja-8vady88nurzxdlxgekawegvv2)). The sketch, it later emerged, had been made two years earlier in Rio de Janeiro for another case ([Jornal da Unicamp, 26 September to 2 October 2016](https://www.unicamp.br/unicamp/sites/default/files/jornal/paginas/ju_670_pagina_12_web.pdf)). Accounts of the crowd differ: a 2023 newspaper retrospective speaks of "cerca de 100 pessoas" attacking her ([Diário do Nordeste, 21 June 2023](https://diariodonordeste.verdesmares.com.br/ultima-hora/pais/linchamento-de-fabiane-maria-de-jesus-e-o-caso-do-linha-direta-desta-semana-relembre-historia-1.3383603)), while the sociologist José de Souza Martins, interviewed by the magazine of the São Paulo Research Foundation (FAPESP), Pesquisa FAPESP, describes her as "surrounded by thousands of raving, violent people" ([Pesquisa FAPESP, Days of fury](https://revistapesquisa.fapesp.br/en/days-of-fury-2/)).

The courts dealt with the attackers and the platform separately. In a civil suit brought by her widower and daughters against Facebook, the São Paulo state court of appeal (Tribunal de Justiça de São Paulo, TJSP) unanimously upheld the dismissal on 1 September 2020 (appeal 1087431-85.2019.8.26.0100): the claim of the widower was time-barred, and for the daughters the court found <Claim status="established" by="by the TJSP's Second Private Law Chamber">no unlawful conduct by the company and no causal link, since no one had notified it to remove the post, the attackers "já foram condenadas criminalmente", and they acted "através de seu livre arbítrio"</Claim>; the court added that any duty to monitor content "deve ser objeto de inovação legal" ([TJSP, Apelação Cível 1087431-85.2019.8.26.0100, judgment of 1 September 2020](https://internetlab.org.br/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/tjsplinchamento.pdf)). The family had sought R$36 million. The same retrospective reports that one of the men convicted for the killing was sentenced to 30 years and that the family's appeal against Facebook awaited the Supreme Federal Court ([Diário do Nordeste, 2023](https://diariodonordeste.verdesmares.com.br/ultima-hora/pais/linchamento-de-fabiane-maria-de-jesus-e-o-caso-do-linha-direta-desta-semana-relembre-historia-1.3383603)); the criminal judgments themselves could not be read for this part. Even before the Marco Civil da Internet of 2014, the court noted, Brazilian case law made a platform liable for users' content only if it failed to act after a judicial or extrajudicial notice; how the Supreme Federal Court reshaped that law's liability rule in 2025 is set out in [the psychological operations series opener](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign).

### Brazil's lynchings in numbers

Martins, professor emeritus at the University of São Paulo (USP), compiled "a catalog of 2,028 cases, which focused primarily on the years between 1945 and 1998", in which 2,579 people were targets of attempted or completed lynchings; 1,150 were rescued, "by police in more than 90% of the cases", and of the 1,221 who were attacked, 782 died ([Pesquisa FAPESP, Days of fury](https://revistapesquisa.fapesp.br/en/days-of-fury-2/)). He estimates that over the past 60 years more than a million Brazilians have taken part in or attempted a lynching, cites the University of São Paulo's Center for the Study of Violence (NEV-USP) count of 580 lynchings in São Paulo state, 204 in Rio de Janeiro and 180 in Bahia between 1980 and 2006, and found that 7.8% of the cases he studied "involved the lynching of an innocent party". "A lynching is attempted every day in Brazil," he told the magazine.

### India, 2017 and 2018: child-lifting rumours

A wave of killings in India followed rumours, spread on WhatsApp and other social media, that strangers were abducting children. An analysis by IndiaSpend of news reports counted "Between January 1, 2017, and July 5, 2018, 33 persons have been killed and at least 99 injured in 69 reported cases", against a single similar killing recorded in 2012; "In all cases, the victims were assaulted on mere suspicion and no evidence of child lifting was found later" ([IndiaSpend analysis, republished by Business Standard, 9 July 2018](https://business-standard.com/article/current-affairs/69-mob-attacks-on-child-lifting-rumours-since-jan-17-only-one-before-that-118070900081_1.html)). On 3 July 2018 the government told WhatsApp that lynchings had followed "irresponsible and explosive messages filled with rumours and provocation" ([Press Information Bureau, 3 July 2018](https://pib.gov.in/newsite/PrintRelease.aspx?relid=180364)); the company's response, its forwarding limits and the fieldwork on why people forward, are in [the part on reach](/learn/from-the-lodge-to-the-network-how-reach-changed).

The Supreme Court of India addressed the wave in *Tehseen S. Poonawalla v. Union of India* on 17 July 2018: "Lynching and mob violence are creeping threats that may gradually take the shape of a Typhon-like monster as evidenced in the wake of the rising wave of incidents of recurring patterns by frenzied mobs across the country instigated by intolerance and misinformed by circulation of fake news and false stories", aggravated by "bystander apathy" and the "inertia of the law enforcing machinery" ([Supreme Court of India, judgment of 17 July 2018](https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2016/27263/27263_2016_Judgement_17-Jul-2018.pdf)). The court held that "The horrendous acts of mobocracy cannot be permitted to inundate the law of the land", issued directions to the states, and recommended that Parliament "create a separate offence for lynching and provide adequate punishment for the same".

## Who steered the rumour

Most of these rumours had no author anyone could find. The Brazilian police found none in 2013, Lefebvre found a spontaneous movement in 1789, and the panic buyers of 2020 were steered by nothing more than each other. Others had identifiable sources without a proven intent: a Facebook page in Guarujá, a newspaper story in Tulsa, a short seller's allegation about Signature Bank, a radio station in Quito. And in the US South the rumour of rape served a system: Wells showed that most victims were never even charged with it, and EJI calls the result terrorism. Where someone deliberately uses fear of this kind, the techniques are those described in [the part on how manipulators harness collective panic](/learn/how-manipulators-harness-collective-panic); what ends an episode, and what the evidence says about remedies such as deposit guarantees, rumour clinics and policing in advance, is the subject of [the part on what ends an outbreak](/learn/what-ends-an-outbreak-and-what-helps). The repetition that makes a rumour feel true is set out in [the part on the human attack surface](/learn/the-human-attack-surface-why-repetition-works).

## What to remember

- Allport and Postman (1947) defined rumour as a proposition passed on without secure evidence, and proposed that its spread varies with importance times ambiguity; in their experiments about 70% of details were lost in five or six retellings, and stories bent towards stereotypes.
- They held that no riot occurs without rumours; the riots of 1943 and the lynchings in this part fit that pattern, and early, visible policing forestalled one feared clash.
- The Great Fear of 1789 spread from six centres between 20 July and 6 August; contemporaries blamed a plot, Lefebvre found a spontaneous movement, and the night of 4 August answered the unrest.
- The 1938 War of the Worlds panic was far smaller than newspapers reported; in Quito in 1949 the imitation ended in deaths, whose number differs between sources.
- A bank run can make a rumour true: Diamond and Dybvig's model, Northern Rock in 2007, SVB in 2023 (over $40 billion in a day) and the Bolsa Família run of 2013, which the Federal Police found spontaneous.
- Panic buying in 2020 was driven by most households buying somewhat more, not by a few hoarders, and unit limits would have done little.
- Lynchings followed rumours in the US South, in Tulsa in 1921, in Guarujá in 2014 and in India in 2017 and 2018; courts convicted the attackers in Guarujá and held the platform not liable, and India's Supreme Court asked Parliament for a specific offence.
