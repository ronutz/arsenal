Every feed is a ranking. Of the hundreds of millions of posts that could appear on a screen at any moment, a handful are shown, in an order chosen by software from what the person, and people like them, did before. The earlier parts of this series, opened by [the map from the small lie to the campaign](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign), dealt with who tries to manipulate an audience and how ([bots, sockpuppets and astroturfing](/learn/bots-sockpuppets-and-astroturfing), [state campaigns on the record](/learn/state-influence-campaigns-on-the-record)). This part looks at the machinery they all pass through: what the largest platforms say about their recommender systems and the profiles that feed them, what European and Brazilian law now forces them to disclose, what regulators found when they looked, and what researchers measured about whether recommendations lead people somewhere they would not have gone. Whether exposure to like-minded content polarizes is a separate dispute, treated in **[Echo chambers and filter bubbles: the evidence and the dispute](/learn/echo-chambers-and-filter-bubbles-the-evidence-and-the-dispute)**; what platforms remove, rather than rank, is the subject of **[Content moderation at scale](/learn/content-moderation-at-scale)**.

## Two definitions

The European Union (EU) gave the machinery a legal name in Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, the Digital Services Act (DSA): a "recommender system" is "a fully or partially automated system used by an online platform to suggest in its online interface specific information to recipients of the service or prioritise that information, including as a result of a search initiated by the recipient of the service or otherwise determining the relative order or prominence of information displayed" ([DSA, article 3(s), 19 October 2022](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065)). The definition covers feeds, "up next" panels and search results alike: anything that decides order or prominence.

The input is usually a profile. The DSA borrows its definition from the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), under which "profiling" means "any form of automated processing of personal data consisting of the use of personal data to evaluate certain personal aspects relating to a natural person, in particular to analyse or predict aspects concerning that natural person's performance at work, economic situation, health, personal preferences, interests, reliability, behaviour, location or movements" ([GDPR, article 4(4), 27 April 2016](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32016R0679)). The GDPR also gives a person "the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, which produces legal effects concerning him or her or similarly significantly affects him or her", with exceptions for contracts, laws and explicit consent ([GDPR, article 22](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32016R0679)). A recommendation is rarely a decision with legal effects; the DSA, as shown below, regulates it by other means.

## What the platforms say about their own systems

### YouTube: signals, satisfaction and "borderline" content

YouTube's most detailed public account came in September 2021 from Cristos Goodrow, its vice-president of engineering, who wrote that "Recommendations drive a significant amount of the overall viewership on YouTube, even more than channel subscriptions or search" ([YouTube, On YouTube's recommendation system, 15 September 2021](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/on-youtubes-recommendation-system/)). Mozilla's researchers, citing a 2018 statement by YouTube's chief product officer, put the share of watch time coming from recommendations at "at least 70%" ([Mozilla Foundation, YouTube Regrets, July 2021](https://assets.mofoprod.net/network/documents/Mozilla_YouTube_Regrets_Report.pdf)).

Goodrow described a system "constantly evolving, learning every day from over 80 billion pieces of information we call signals", which is why, he wrote, "providing more transparency isn't as simple as listing a formula for recommendations" ([YouTube, 15 September 2021](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/on-youtubes-recommendation-system/)). He named the signals: clicks; watch time, added in 2012, after which the company "saw an immediate 20% drop in views"; survey responses used to measure "valued watchtime", with a machine-learning model trained to predict the answers of people who were never surveyed; and shares, likes and dislikes, each weighted differently for each person. The same post says YouTube has used recommendations "to limit low-quality content from being widely viewed since 2011", first with classifiers for racy or violent videos and, from 2019, for "borderline content", defined as "content that comes close to, but doesn't quite violate our Community Guidelines", identified by classifiers trained on the judgments of human evaluators who follow public rating guidelines.

When YouTube announced that change in January 2019, it said it would "begin reducing recommendations of borderline content and content that could misinform users in harmful ways", that the shift would "apply to less than one percent of the content on YouTube", and that it would affect only recommendations, "not whether a video is available on YouTube" ([YouTube, Continuing our work to improve recommendations on YouTube, 25 January 2019](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/continuing-our-work-to-improve/)). In 2021 Goodrow reported the result in the company's own numbers: <Claim status="reported" by="by YouTube">a "70% drop in watchtime on non-subscribed, recommended borderline content in the U.S." and consumption of borderline content coming from recommendations "significantly below 1%"</Claim>, with a stated goal of keeping such views "below 0.5% of overall views on YouTube" ([YouTube, 15 September 2021](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/on-youtubes-recommendation-system/)). No outside party is cited in the post as having verified those figures.

### TikTok: the For You feed

TikTok explained its main feed in June 2020. Recommendations, it wrote, rest on "User interactions", "Video information" such as captions, sounds and hashtags, and "Device and account settings", the last receiving "lower weight"; a strong signal such as watching a longer video to the end "would receive greater weight than a weak indicator", and "neither follower count nor whether the account has had previous high-performing videos are direct factors in the recommendation system" ([TikTok, How TikTok recommends videos #ForYou, 18 June 2020](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you)). The company named the risk itself: "One of the inherent challenges with recommendation engines is that they can inadvertently limit your experience", which, it added, "is sometimes referred to as a 'filter bubble'", and said it intersperses diverse content and keeps some videos, such as graphic medical procedures, out of recommendation.

Ahead of the DSA deadline in August 2023, TikTok said European users would be able "to turn off personalisation", so that their For You feed would show popular videos from where they live and around the world "rather than recommending content to them based on their personal interests"; it added that accounts of users under 16 "are set to private by default and their content cannot be recommended in For You feeds", and that users aged 13 to 17 in Europe would "no longer see personalised advertising based on their activities on or off TikTok" ([TikTok, An update on fulfilling our commitments under the Digital Services Act, 4 August 2023](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-eu/fulfilling-commitments-dsa-update)).

### Meta: system cards and their limits

Meta's president of global affairs, Nick Clegg, announced in June 2023 the release of "22 system cards for Facebook and Instagram", which explain how artificial intelligence (AI) systems rank content in Feed, Stories and Reels, together with the categories of "signals" used in Facebook Feed ranking ([Meta, How AI Influences What You See on Facebook and Instagram, 29 June 2023](https://about.fb.com/news/2023/06/how-ai-ranks-content-on-facebook-and-instagram/)). The same post set the boundary: "But there's a limit to what we can disclose safely", because some signals "might make it easier for people to circumvent our defenses". It pointed users to the in-app feature "Why Am I Seeing This?" and to chronological alternatives: "you can use the Feeds tab on Facebook or select Following on Instagram".

Two months later Meta said it had "over 1,000 people currently working on the DSA", that it would expand its Ad Library "to display and archive all ads that target people in the EU", that since February 2023 "teens aged 13-17 globally no longer see advertising based on their activity on our apps", and that European users would be able to view Stories, Reels and search results "not ranked by Meta using these systems" ([Meta, New Features and Additional Transparency Measures as the Digital Services Act Comes Into Effect, 22 August 2023](https://about.fb.com/news/2023/08/new-features-and-additional-transparency-measures-as-the-digital-services-act-comes-into-effect/)).

### X: the published code

Twitter, now X, went further in one respect: in 2023 its engineering team wrote "We've released the code powering our recommendations", describing a pipeline that takes "the roughly 500 million Tweets posted daily down to a handful of top Tweets", extracts "the best 1500 Tweets from a pool of hundreds of millions" for each request, ranks them with "a ~48M parameter neural network" and builds a For You timeline of "50% In-Network Tweets and 50% Out-of-Network Tweets on average" ([X Engineering blog, Twitter's Recommendation Algorithm, 2023, read through a fetch tool](https://blog.x.com/engineering/en_us/topics/open-source/2023/twitter-recommendation-algorithm)). In January 2026 the European Commission said it was examining "the impact of its recently announced switch to a Grok-based recommender system" ([European Commission, IP/26/203, 26 January 2026](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_203)).

### What the disclosures have in common

| Platform | What it published | Signals it names | Option without profiling | What it says about the limits of disclosure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| YouTube | Blog posts (2019, 2021), rating guidelines | Clicks, watch time, surveys, shares, likes, dislikes; "authoritative" and "borderline" scores | History can be paused or deleted | No formula to list: the system "doesn't follow a set formula" |
| TikTok | Newsroom explainer (2020), DSA update (2023) | Interactions, video information, device settings, completion | Non-personalised feed in Europe (2023) | Not stated; the post describes weights only as "strong" and "weak" indicators |
| Meta | 22 system cards, signal categories (2023) | Predictions of sharing, survey feedback, many others | Chronological Feeds and Following; unranked options in the EU | "a limit to what we can disclose safely" |
| X | Source code (2023) | Candidate sources, a ranking network, filters | Not stated in the post read | Not stated in the post read |

All four describe inputs and goals. YouTube says its system has no set formula to publish, Meta that some signals would help people evade its defences, and X released code; none of the posts read gives the relative weight of each signal, which is what European law now asks platforms to explain.

## What the law now requires

### The European Union

The DSA's general obligations apply to online platforms; the heaviest apply to "very large online platforms" (VLOPs) and search engines with at least 45 million average monthly active recipients in the EU ([DSA, article 33](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065)). The Commission says it has designated 25 such platforms and 3 search engines, that the regulation's general date of applicability was 17 February 2024 and that designated services are bound from four months after designation ([European Commission, The enforcement framework under the Digital Services Act, last updated 6 November 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/dsa-enforcement)). The provisions that bear on recommendation and profiling are these ([DSA](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065)):

- **Explain the main parameters (article 27).** Platforms "shall set out in their terms and conditions, in plain and intelligible language, the main parameters used in their recommender systems", including "the criteria which are most significant" and "the reasons for the relative importance of those parameters", and where options exist, a functionality to change them that is "directly and easily accessible".
- **Offer a feed without profiling (article 38).** VLOPs "shall provide at least one option for each of their recommender systems which is not based on profiling".
- **Limit profiling for ads (articles 26 and 28).** No ads "based on profiling" using special categories of data such as health or religion, and none based on profiling when the platform is "aware with reasonable certainty that the recipient of the service is a minor".
- **Assess and mitigate systemic risks (articles 34 and 35).** At least once a year, VLOPs assess risks including "any actual or foreseeable negative effects on civic discourse and electoral processes", and must take into account "the design of their recommender systems and any other relevant algorithmic system".
- **Submit to independent audits (article 37).** At their own expense, at least once a year, with an opinion that is "'positive', 'positive with comments' or 'negative'".
- **Keep an ad repository (article 39).** A searchable public repository, with an application programming interface, holding each ad's content, who paid, the targeting parameters and reach, until one year after the ad last ran.
- **Open the system to scrutiny (article 40).** On request, VLOPs must "explain the design, the logic, the functioning and the testing of their algorithmic systems, including their recommender systems", and give vetted researchers access to data.
- **Pay for failures (article 74).** Fines "not exceeding 6 % of its total worldwide annual turnover".

### Brazil

Brazil has no statute equivalent to the DSA's recommender rules, but three sets of rules touch the same ground. The electoral court, the Tribunal Superior Eleitoral (TSE), amended its propaganda resolution in 2024 to require providers that sell boosting of political content to keep a repository allowing monitoring "em tempo real" (in real time) of the content, amounts, payers and the audience characteristics bought, which the text calls "perfilamento" (profiling), searchable by keyword and advertiser and through an application programming interface, as a condition of accreditation and of "cumprimento permanente" (permanent compliance); item III of its article 9-D refers to "o aprimoramento de seus sistemas de recomendação de conteúdo", the improvement of providers' content recommendation systems ([TSE, Resolution 23.610/2019, consolidated text with Resolution 23.732/2024, articles 9-D and 27-A, read through a fetch tool](https://www.tse.jus.br/legislacao/compilada/res/2019/resolucao-no-23-610-de-18-de-dezembro-de-2019)). The resolution also bars companies that sell boosting from offering it for "fato notoriamente inverídico ou gravemente descontextualizado que possa atingir a integridade do processo eleitoral", notoriously false or seriously decontextualized facts that may affect the integrity of the electoral process ([A Gazeta (Folhapress), 24 April 2024](https://www.agazeta.com.br/brasil/google-passara-a-vetar-anuncio-politico-apos-regra-eleitoral-do-tse-0424)).

The Estatuto Digital da Criança e do Adolescente (ECA Digital), Law 15.211 of 2025, entered into force on 17 March 2026 and was regulated by Decree 12.880 of 2026; according to the Senate's news agency, it bans profiling children and adolescents for advertising, requires products that curb compulsive use, including the end of infinite scroll and autoplay for minors, and names the Autoridade Nacional de Proteção de Dados (ANPD), the national data protection authority, as regulator ([Agência Senado, ECA Digital: o que cabe às empresas, às famílias e ao Estado, 20 March 2026](https://www12.senado.leg.br/noticias/materias/2026/03/20/eca-digital-o-que-cabe-as-empresas-as-familias-e-ao-estado)). "A proibição de perfilamento de crianças e adolescentes para fins publicitários" (the ban on profiling children and adolescents for advertising) was already in effect, the Senate's legislative consultant Luiz Fernando Fauth told the agency; Meta, Google and TikTok told it they were expanding protections for minors. The law's own text puts the ban in article 22: "é vedada a utilização de técnicas de perfilamento para direcionamento de publicidade comercial a crianças e a adolescentes" (the use of profiling techniques to target commercial advertising at children and adolescents is forbidden), along with emotional analysis and augmented, extended or virtual reality for that purpose; article 17 lists among its minimum safeguards the "limitação de recursos para aumentar, sustentar ou estender artificialmente o uso do produto ou serviço pela criança ou pelo adolescente, como reprodução automática de mídia" (limiting features that artificially extend a child's use, such as media autoplay); and article 41-A sets its entry into force six months after publication ([Lei 15.211/2025](https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/_ato2023-2026/2025/lei/L15211.htm)).

### The United States

The United States has no federal law of this kind. In September 2024 the staff of the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) published a study of nine companies, among them Meta, YouTube, X, Snap, ByteDance (TikTok's owner), Discord, Reddit, Amazon (Twitch) and WhatsApp, finding that they had "engaged in vast surveillance of consumers in order to monetize their personal information", had "fed users' and non-users' personal information into their automated systems", depended on targeted advertising that "accounts for most of their revenue", and were "failing to adequately protect users online, especially children and teens"; the Commission voted 5-0 to issue the report ([Federal Trade Commission, press release, 19 September 2024, read through a fetch tool](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/09/ftc-staff-report-finds-large-social-media-video-streaming-companies-have-engaged-vast-surveillance)). Two Republican commissioners dissented in part: Andrew Ferguson wrote, "I do not share the Report's apparent view that the display of targeted advertising to adults is, on balance, harmful", and Melissa Holyoak that its recommendations "seek to regulate private conduct through a sub-regulatory guidance document" ([Venable LLP, Amid Pushback, the FTC Issues Anticipated Report on Data Practices of Social Media and Video Streaming Services, September 2024](https://www.venable.com/insights/publications/2024/09/amid-pushback-the-ftc-issues-anticipated-report)).

## What the first audits showed

The first DSA audit reports were published at the end of 2024. Daniel Holznagel, writing for the DSA Observatory at the University of Amsterdam, found that most were produced by the Big Four accounting firms: Ernst & Young (EY) for Facebook, Instagram, YouTube, Pinterest and Snapchat, KPMG for TikTok, Deloitte for LinkedIn, while X was audited by FTI Consulting ([DSA Observatory, Shortcomings of the first DSA Audits, and how to do better, 11 June 2025](https://dsa-observatory.eu/2025/06/11/shortcomings-of-the-first-dsa-audits-and-how-to-do-better/)). He quotes EY's report on YouTube: <Claim status="reported" by="by the DSA Observatory, quoting the audit report">"we do not express an opinion, conclusion, nor any form of assurance on the design, operation, and monitoring of the algorithmic systems"</Claim>, with similar statements in other reports; he reads this as a withdrawal from substantive auditing of recommender systems. He also lists the provisions auditors skipped because the Commission was investigating, among them the risk assessment articles for Facebook and TikTok, and notes that FTI Consulting did audit X's risk provisions despite an open proceeding and documented shortcomings. The audit firms' reasoning, as he quotes it, was that ongoing investigations might "present potentially significant contrary evidence" and so they did "not express a conclusion".

## What regulators found

### The European Commission

Apart from the fine on X and the decisions accepting commitments and action plans, the Commission's findings below are preliminary; it repeats in each that they "do not prejudge the outcome of the investigation" ([European Commission, IP/26/2361, 6 November 2026](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_2361)). The proceedings, as listed by the Commission, run as follows ([European Commission, Supervision of the designated very large online platforms and search engines under DSA, updated 6 November 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/list-designated-vlops-and-vloses)).

**X.** In December 2023 the Commission opened its first DSA proceedings, against X, covering risk management, notice and action, the effectiveness of Community Notes, the ad repository, researchers' data access and the "Blue checks" ([European Commission, IP/23/6709, 18 December 2023](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_23_6709)). On 5 December 2025 it adopted the first non-compliance decision under the DSA, fining X €120 million for the deceptive design of the blue checkmark, an ad repository with "excessive delays in processing" that lacked "the content and topic of the advertisement, as well as the legal entity paying for it", and terms of service that "prohibit eligible researchers from independently accessing its public data, including through scraping" ([European Commission, IP/25/2934, 5 December 2025](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_25_2934)). Elon Musk called the fine "Bullshit" and vowed a "very public battle in court", United States Vice-President JD Vance said "the EU should be supporting free speech, not attacking American companies over garbage", and a Commission spokesman, Thomas Regnier, said "The decision is about transparency obligations under the DSA, not about freedom of speech", according to Creatives Unite ([Creatives Unite, EU Fines X €120 Million; Musk And Top US Officials Respond With Fury, 8 December 2025](https://creativesunite.eu/article/eu-fines-x-million-musk-and-top-us-officials-respond-with-fury)). In July 2026 the Commission accepted X's action plan to improve its repository and researchers' access, although the European Board for Digital Services had deemed "X's audit measures and, as a result, the overall action plan insufficient" ([European Commission, Commission accepts X's action plan to comply with Digital Services Act, 16 July 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-accepts-xs-action-plan-comply-digital-services-act)). The part of the 2023 proceedings on X's recommender systems remains open and was extended in January 2026 ([European Commission, IP/26/203, 26 January 2026](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_203)).

**TikTok.** In May 2025 the Commission preliminarily found that TikTok's ad repository did not provide "the necessary information about the content of the advertisements, the users targeted by the ads, and who paid for the advertisements" ([European Commission, IP/25/1223, 15 May 2025](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_25_1223)); in December 2025 it made binding TikTok's commitments to show full ad content, update the repository "within a maximum of 24 hours" and publish "the targeting criteria selected by advertisers" with aggregated data on who was reached ([European Commission, IP/25/2940, 5 December 2025](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_25_2940)). In February 2026 it preliminarily found TikTok's "addictive design", including "its highly personalised recommender system", in breach, saying TikTok "needs to change the basic design of its service" ([European Commission, IP/26/312, 6 February 2026](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_312)). On 6 November 2026 it preliminarily found that the For You feed produces a <Claim status="alleged" by="by the European Commission">"rabbit hole effect"</Claim>: "The more a user interacts with a certain type of content, the more TikTok's 'For You' feed algorithm recommends similar content", including content about weight loss and body image; it found that "using the 'not interested' button may not be enough to change the content feed", that "The algorithm prioritises showing users content based on viewing time", and that TikTok should "disable viewing time as an indicator to recommend content" ([European Commission, IP/26/2361, 6 November 2026](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_2361)). TikTok answered that the findings are <Claim status="denied" by="by TikTok">"fundamentally inaccurate and distort our platform's architecture and our commitment to user safety"</Claim>, adding, "We have a strong record of protecting our community while giving users direct and intuitive control over their content experience" ([Associated Press, as republished by NY1, 6 November 2026](https://ny1.com/nyc/all-boroughs/ap-top-news/2026/11/06/eu-says-rabbit-hole-video-feeds-on-tiktok-violate-its-digital-rules)).

**Meta.** In April 2024 the Commission opened proceedings over deceptive advertising, disinformation and Meta's "political content approach" that "demotes political content in the recommender systems of Instagram and Facebook" ([European Commission, IP/24/2373, 30 April 2024](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_24_2373)), and in May 2024 over whether Facebook's and Instagram's systems "may stimulate behavioural addictions in children, as well as create so-called 'rabbit-hole effects'" ([European Commission, IP/24/2664, 16 May 2024](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_24_2664)). In October 2025 it preliminarily found both Meta and TikTok in breach of the obligation to give researchers adequate access to public data ([European Commission, IP/25/2503, 24 October 2025](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_25_2503)). In July 2026 it preliminarily found the addictive design of Instagram and Facebook in breach, asking Meta to make its recommender system "less engagement-oriented" and noting that it continues to investigate "so-called 'rabbit hole' effects" ([European Commission, IP/26/1579, 10 July 2026](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_1579)). Meta's spokesperson Ben Walters said, <Claim status="denied" by="by Meta">"We disagree with these preliminary findings, which don't accurately take into account the significant steps we've taken to protect teens"</Claim> ([Al Jazeera, 10 July 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/7/10/eu-says-addictive-features-on-instagram-and-facebook-breach-its-rules)), and the company cited Teen Accounts that let parents "cap daily screen time at just 15 minutes" ([The Irish Times, 10 July 2026](https://www.irishtimes.com/business/2026/07/10/eu-warns-meta-over-facebook-and-instagrams-addictive-feeds/)).

### A national court: the feed choice that kept resetting

The first judgment on the DSA's recommender articles came not from the Commission but from a Dutch court, in a suit by the digital rights group Bits of Freedom. According to Matteo Fabbri's analysis for the DSA Observatory, the District Court of Amsterdam ruled on 2 October 2025 that Meta did not comply with articles 27(3) and 38 because the non-profiled option on Facebook and Instagram was hard to reach and was reset when users reopened the app or moved between sections; it ordered Meta "to respect and persistently apply user preferences" for Dutch users ([DSA Observatory, Ineffective by design: Bits of Freedom vs Meta, 30 July 2026](https://dsa-observatory.eu/2026/07/30/ineffective-by-design-bits-of-freedom-vs-meta-at-the-forefront-of-enforcing-user-control-for-recommender-systems-under-the-dsa/)). Meta argued that implementing the order "would take at least six to twelve months", implemented the changes on 6 January 2026, lost an appeal on 10 March 2026 in which the maximum penalty was doubled to 10 million euros, and filed for cassation before the Dutch Supreme Court; it said that <Claim status="attributed" by="by Meta, as quoted by the DSA Observatory">"the issue is a matter for the European Commission and regulators at the European levels and not for courts in individual countries"</Claim>.

### Brazil's data protection authority

The ANPD used its powers against a platform's use of profile data in July 2024, when it ordered Meta, as a preventive measure, to suspend a new privacy policy allowing users' data to train generative AI, on pain of R$50,000 a day; the authority said Meta had not given adequate information and that there were "obstáculos excessivos e não justificados" (excessive and unjustified obstacles) to objecting ([Diário do Nordeste, 2 July 2024](https://diariodonordeste.verdesmares.com.br/ultima-hora/tecnologia/governo-determina-suspensao-de-uso-de-dados-de-usuarios-para-treinar-ias-pela-meta-1.3530108)). Meta replied that "Isso é um retrocesso para a inovação e a competitividade no desenvolvimento de IA" (this is a setback for innovation and competitiveness in AI development) and that its approach complied with Brazilian law. On 30 August 2024 the ANPD lifted the measure after approving a compliance plan in which Meta undertook not to collect data from accounts of under-18s and to make objection simpler ([Mobile Time, 30 August 2024](https://www.mobiletime.com.br/noticias/30/08/2024/meta-anpd-suspende/)). With the ECA Digital, the same authority is now responsible for the ban on profiling minors for advertising ([Agência Senado, 20 March 2026](https://www12.senado.leg.br/noticias/materias/2026/03/20/eca-digital-o-que-cabe-as-empresas-as-familias-e-ao-estado)).

## What researchers measured: the rabbit-hole dispute

Whether recommendations push people towards extreme material is the best-studied question about recommenders, and the answer has changed with the platform, the period and the method.

### The first audits from outside

Manoel Horta Ribeiro, Raphael Ottoni, Robert West, Virgílio Almeida and Wagner Meira Jr., of the Federal University of Minas Gerais (UFMG) in Brazil and the École polytechnique fédérale de Lausanne (EPFL), analysed "330,925 videos posted on 349 channels" and "72M+ comments", and found <Claim status="reported" by="by its authors">"that users consistently migrate from milder to more extreme content"</Claim>; probing "more than 2M video and channel recommendations between May/July 2019", they found that "Alt-lite content is easily reachable from I.D.W. channels, while Alt-right videos are reachable only through channel recommendations" ([Ribeiro et al., Auditing Radicalization Pathways on YouTube, arXiv version 4, 21 October 2021, first presented in 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.08313)). They stated the limit themselves: "our recommendations do not account for personalization", since their crawler did not "log in into any account".

Mark Ledwich and Anna Zaitsev, categorizing nearly 800 political channels, reached the opposite conclusion: "we refute the popular radicalization claims", the algorithm "actively discourages viewers from visiting radicalizing or extremist content" and favours "mainstream media and cable news content" ([Ledwich and Zaitsev, Algorithmic Extremism, arXiv, 24 December 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.11211)). Their stated "main limitation is the anonymity of the data set and the recommendations": like Ribeiro's team, they saw what an anonymous visitor is shown, not what a profiled account receives.

### Crowdsourced reports

Mozilla's RegretsReporter study gathered data from "37,380 YouTube users" who flagged "3,362 regrettable videos, coming from 91 countries, between July 2020 and May 2021"; it found that <Claim status="reported" by="by Mozilla">"71% of all Regret reports came from videos recommended to our volunteers"</Claim> and that the rate of regrets was "60% higher in countries that do not have English as a primary language (with Brazil, Germany and France being particularly high)" ([Mozilla Foundation, YouTube Regrets, July 2021](https://assets.mofoprod.net/network/documents/Mozilla_YouTube_Regrets_Report.pdf)). The volunteers chose to take part and chose what to flag, so the figures describe their reports, not YouTube's audience. A YouTube spokesperson replied that "over the past year alone, we've launched over 30 different changes to reduce recommendations of harmful content" ([The Next Web, 7 July 2021](https://thenextweb.com/news/youtube-algorithm-recommends-videos-that-violate-platforms-policies-mozilla-study)), and the company told CNET it had not been able to review the validity of Mozilla's data or its definition of "regrettable" ([Tubefilter, 7 July 2021](https://www.tubefilter.com/2021/07/07/youtubes-algorithm-recommends-regrettable-videos/)).

### Panels of real users

Homa Hosseinmardi and colleagues followed "a representative panel of more than 300,000 Americans" on and off YouTube from January 2016 to December 2019 and found <Claim status="reported" by="by its authors">"no evidence that engagement with far-right content is caused by YouTube recommendations systematically"</Claim>; consumption "appears to reflect individual preferences that extend across the web as a whole" ([Hosseinmardi et al., Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, August 2021, abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:%2210.1073/pnas.2101967118%22&format=json&resultType=core)). Annie Chen, Brendan Nyhan, Jason Reifler, Ronald Robertson and Christo Wilson paired browsing and survey data from 1,181 people in 2020 and found exposure to alternative and extremist channels "heavily concentrated among a small group of people with high prior levels of gender and racial resentment", who often subscribe and follow external links, while "nonsubscribers rarely see or follow recommendations to videos from these channels"; they suggest the algorithms "were not sending people down 'rabbit holes' during our observation window in 2020, possibly due to changes that the company made to its recommender system in 2019" ([Chen et al., Science Advances, 30 August 2023, abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:%2210.1126/sciadv.add8080%22&format=json&resultType=core)).

### Sock puppets and counterfactual bots

Muhammad Haroon and colleagues ran "100,000 sock puppets" and found that "YouTube's algorithm recommends congenial content to its partisan users", that congenial recommendations "increase deeper in the trail for right-leaning users", and that while extremity did not rise meaningfully, the share of recommendations from channels categorized as problematic grew; that share was low ("max of 2.5%") but such videos were "encountered by over 36.1% of users and up to 40% in the case of very-right users" ([Haroon et al., Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, December 2023, abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:%2210.1073/pnas.2213020120%22&format=json&resultType=core)). Hosseinmardi, Ribeiro and colleagues then built "counterfactual bots" that compare a real user's trajectory with one that follows only the recommender, and found that <Claim status="reported" by="by its authors">"relying exclusively on the YouTube recommender results in less partisan consumption"</Claim>, that the sidebar "forgets" a partisan preference "within roughly 30 videos", and that, "at least since the algorithm changes that YouTube implemented in 2019", recommendations play "if anything, a moderating role" ([Hosseinmardi et al., Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, February 2024, abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:%2210.1073/pnas.2313377121%22&format=json&resultType=core)). Ribeiro is an author of both the 2019 audit that found migration towards extreme content and the 2024 study that found a moderating recommender: the change is in the period and the method, not a retraction.

### A platform's own experiment

Twitter's researchers Ferenc Huszár and colleagues used "a long-running, massive-scale randomized experiment" that kept "nearly 2 million daily active accounts" on a reverse-chronological feed and found that <Claim status="reported" by="by its authors">"In six out of seven countries studied, the mainstream political right enjoys higher algorithmic amplification than the mainstream political left"</Claim>, that amplification favoured right-leaning news sources in the United States, and no evidence that algorithms amplify "far-left and far-right political groups more than moderate ones" ([Huszár et al., Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, January 2022, abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:%2210.1073/pnas.2025334119%22&format=json&resultType=core)). It remains one of the few published studies with a true control group, which only a platform could build.

### What insiders said

In October 2021 Frances Haugen, a former Facebook product manager, told a Senate subcommittee that <Claim status="alleged" by="by Frances Haugen">"The result has been a system that amplifies division, extremism, and polarization"</Claim>, that "Only Facebook knows how it personalizes your feed for you", and that the company's design leaves it without oversight "even from its own Oversight Board, which is as blind as the public" ([Statement of Frances Haugen, Senate Committee on Commerce, Science and Transportation, 4 October 2021](https://www.commerce.senate.gov/services/files/FC8A558E-824E-4914-BEDB-3A7B1190BD49)). Mark Zuckerberg answered, <Claim status="denied" by="by Mark Zuckerberg">"At the heart of these accusations is this idea that we prioritize profit over safety and well-being. That's just not true"</Claim>, citing the Meaningful Social Interactions change to News Feed, which he said the company made knowing people would spend less time on the site ([Engadget, 6 October 2021](https://www.engadget.com/mark-zuckerberg-denies-facebook-profit-over-safety-033717690.html)).

### Brazil in the evidence

Brazil appears in this literature mostly through its researchers (the UFMG team above) and as a high-regret country in Mozilla's data. In August 2019 The New York Times reported that YouTube in Brazil "has become more widely watched than all but one TV channel" and opened with the account of a Brazilian teenager who said YouTube's recommendations had led him to far-right channels ([The New York Times, How YouTube Radicalized Brazil, 11 August 2019, preview read](https://www.nytimes.com/2019/08/11/world/americas/youtube-brazil.html)); the full article, including YouTube's response, was behind a paywall when this part was researched. YouTube's general position, stated two years later, is that "recent published papers conclude YouTube recommendations aren't actually steering viewers towards extreme content" ([YouTube, 15 September 2021](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/on-youtubes-recommendation-system/)).

Two points come out of the record. The question the Commission now asks about TikTok and Meta, repeated exposure to content about sadness or body image, is not the same as the political radicalization question most of these studies measured. And several of the later authors attribute part of the difference with the early findings to YouTube's 2019 changes (Chen and colleagues; Hosseinmardi and colleagues in 2024), while the two early audits both measured logged-out recommendations, a limit their authors flagged.

## Ad repositories: profiling made visible

Under the DSA, an ad repository shows, ad by ad, who paid and whom they wanted to reach. The regulation requires that it show "whether the advertisement was intended to be presented specifically to one or more particular groups of recipients of the service and if so, the main parameters used for that purpose including where applicable the main parameters used to exclude one or more of such particular groups" ([DSA, article 39(2)(e)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065)). Meta said in 2023 that it had long offered transparency for "social issue, electoral and political ads" and would extend its Ad Library to every ad targeting people in the EU ([Meta, 22 August 2023](https://about.fb.com/news/2023/08/new-features-and-additional-transparency-measures-as-the-digital-services-act-comes-into-effect/)). The Commission's cases show how far practice can fall short: X's repository was fined for missing content, topics and payers, and TikTok's for missing targeting and payers, until TikTok committed to fix it ([European Commission, IP/25/2934, 5 December 2025](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_25_2934); [IP/25/2940, 5 December 2025](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_25_2940)).

In Brazil, the TSE's 2024 repository rule produced an exit rather than a library at one company: Google said it would update its policies "para não mais permitir a veiculação de anúncios políticos no País" (to no longer allow political ads in the country) from May 2024, preferring, as Mobile Time put it, not to sell political ads rather than meet the court's technical requirements ([Mobile Time, 24 April 2024](https://www.mobiletime.com.br/noticias/24/04/2024/google-proibe-veiculacao-de-anuncios-eleitorais-no-brasil-em-2024/)); the ban covers YouTube and search results ([A Gazeta (Folhapress), 24 April 2024](https://www.agazeta.com.br/brasil/google-passara-a-vetar-anuncio-politico-apos-regra-eleitoral-do-tse-0424)), and Google told CNN Brasil in June 2026 that it would keep it for the 2026 general election, saying "Desde 2024, o Google Ads não permite a veiculação de anúncios políticos no país" (since 2024, Google Ads has not allowed political ads in the country) ([CNN Brasil, 1 June 2026](https://www.cnnbrasil.com.br/eleicoes/google-mantem-proibicao-de-anuncios-politicos-para-eleicoes-de-2026/)). The fact-checking agency Aos Fatos reported that <Claim status="reported" by="by Aos Fatos">scam ads accounted for 11% of Meta's revenue from "political and social" ads in Brazil in the last three months of 2023</Claim> ([Aos Fatos, 15 July 2024](https://www.aosfatos.org/noticias/big-techs-apagao-dados-moderacao/)).

## What readers and defenders can do

- **See why.** Meta's "Why Am I Seeing This?" panel and the targeting information the DSA requires on each ad show part of the profile behind a recommendation or an ad ([Meta, 29 June 2023](https://about.fb.com/news/2023/06/how-ai-ranks-content-on-facebook-and-instagram/); [DSA, article 26](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065)).
- **Change the inputs.** YouTube lets users "pause, edit, or delete" search and watch history ([YouTube, 15 September 2021](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/on-youtubes-recommendation-system/)); TikTok offers "Not Interested" and a reset of the feed, though the Commission found in 2026 that the button "may not be enough to change the content feed" ([European Commission, IP/26/2361](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_2361)).
- **Opt out of profiling where the law allows.** In the EU, VLOPs must offer at least one feed not based on profiling, and a Dutch court has ruled that, for Dutch users of Facebook and Instagram, the choice must persist between sessions ([DSA Observatory, 30 July 2026](https://dsa-observatory.eu/2026/07/30/ineffective-by-design-bits-of-freedom-vs-meta-at-the-forefront-of-enforcing-user-control-for-recommender-systems-under-the-dsa/)).
- **Read the repositories.** Journalists and researchers can query the ad repositories the DSA requires in the EU and, in Brazil, the repositories electoral rules require of platforms that sell political boosting, to see who paid to reach whom ([DSA, article 39](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2065); [TSE, Resolution 23.610/2019, article 27-A](https://www.tse.jus.br/legislacao/compilada/res/2019/resolucao-no-23-610-de-18-de-dezembro-de-2019)).
- **Use the research door.** The Commission said its delegated act on data access, in force from 29 October 2025, grants vetted researchers "access to non-public data from very large online platforms and search engines" ([European Commission, IP/25/2503, 24 October 2025](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_25_2503)).
- **Read studies by their method.** Ask whether accounts were logged in, which year was measured and whether there was a control group: the table above shows how much the answer depends on it.

## What to remember

- A recommender system decides order and prominence; the DSA defines it, and profiling is the GDPR's automated evaluation of a person's interests, behaviour and other traits.
- YouTube, TikTok, Meta and X have published the signals they use (watch time, surveys, interactions, predicted sharing) and X published code, but none publishes the weights that produce an individual ranking, each citing complexity or security.
- The DSA requires platforms to explain main parameters (article 27), very large platforms to offer a feed without profiling (article 38), annual risk assessments and audits, ad repositories and researcher access, with fines up to 6% of worldwide turnover.
- The first audits largely declined to give an opinion on algorithmic systems, according to an analysis of the reports.
- The Commission fined X €120 million in December 2025 for transparency breaches, accepted TikTok's ad-repository commitments, and in 2026 preliminarily found TikTok's and Meta's addictive design, and TikTok's For You "rabbit hole" effect, in breach; both companies dispute the findings.
- Studies of YouTube found migration towards extreme content in logged-out data from 2019 and little or a moderating effect in real-user and counterfactual studies after the 2019 changes; Twitter's own experiment found the mainstream right amplified more in six of seven countries.
- Brazil regulates the edges: the TSE's political-ad repository (which led Google to stop political ads), the ECA Digital's ban on profiling minors for ads, and the ANPD's action on Meta's use of data for AI.

<ReaderQuestions>

- If a platform publishes its code but not the trained model and the data that shape each person's feed, what has the public actually been shown?
- When the same research team finds radicalizing pathways in 2019 and a moderating recommender in 2024, what should a reader conclude: that the system changed, that the methods improved, or both?
- Should a feed without profiling be the default rather than an option, and who should bear the cost if fewer people then stay on the platform?
- When an engagement signal such as viewing time is what keeps a vulnerable person watching content about sadness or weight loss, is the harm in the content, in the signal, or in the business model that rewards it?
- If a company prefers to stop selling political ads rather than publish who paid and whom they targeted, is the public better or worse informed than before?

</ReaderQuestions>
