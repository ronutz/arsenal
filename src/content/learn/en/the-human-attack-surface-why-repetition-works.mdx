A scam, a propaganda campaign and an advertisement aim at the same thing: the shortcuts people use to decide what is true and what to do. Psychology has measured several of them, some more robustly than others. This part covers five: repetition and the illusory truth effect, Robert Cialdini's principles of persuasion, the continued influence of corrected misinformation, the disputed backfire effect, and the debate over how much misinformation people see. For each it names the study, what was measured, the size of the effect where the source gives it, and where the finding stops. After reading, you can separate a replicated result from a contested one.

## Repetition and the illusory truth effect

Emma Henderson, Daniel Simons and Dale Barr, in a preregistered study published in 2021, define the effect: "Repeated statements are rated as subjectively truer than comparable new statements, even though repetition alone provides no new, probative information" ([Henderson et al., 2021](https://journalofcognition.org/articles/10.5334/joc.161)). The predominant explanation, they note, is fluency: a repeated statement is easier to process, and the ease is taken as a sign of accuracy. They link it to influence: "The illusory truth effect could bolster the tactics of propagandists, allowing them to amplify the believability of their message whether or not it is true".

The Debunking Handbook 2020, a consensus text by 22 researchers, cites two studies for the effect ([Debunking Handbook](https://sciencelearning.umd.edu/files/2020/10/DebunkingHandbook2020.pdf)); its Portuguese edition calls it the "efeito da verdade ilusória" ([Manual da Desmistificação](https://sks.to/debunk2020-PT-PDF)). The first is a 1977 paper by Lynn Hasher, David Goldstein and Thomas Toppino, "Frequency and the conference of referential validity" ([Hasher et al., 1977](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(77)80012-1)). Neither its abstract nor its full text could be obtained, so its design, sample and effect size are not available here; Henderson's team lists it among studies whose repetitions were weeks apart.

### How large, and for how long

Alice Dechêne and colleagues pooled 51 studies of the repetition-induced truth effect in 2010 ([Dechêne et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868309352251)). They found the gap between repeated and new statements (between items) larger than the rise in rated truth of the same statement across encounters (within items). Their moderator table, reproduced by Henderson's team, puts the between-items effect at d = .43 to .48 whether the repetition came within a day, a week or later. <Claim status="established" by="by meta-analysis">Repetition raises the rated truth of a statement</Claim>, and the meta-analysis found no link between delay and effect size.

Henderson's team tested delay directly with 608 adults in the United Kingdom (567 analysed), rating trivia statements of uncertain truth on a seven-point scale immediately, one day, one week and one month after first reading them. The effect appeared at all four intervals, with repeated statements averaging 4.52 against 4.14 for new ones, but "The repeated-minus-new difference was largest when tested immediately (0.67) and shrank after one day (0.39), one week (0.27), and one month (0.14)". On delay, experiment and meta-analysis disagree, and the authors call for replication. They also bound the finding: "it is not clear that this result will generalise to topics on which participants hold strong prior beliefs".

### Knowing better does not fully protect

Lisa Fazio, Nadia Brashier, Keith Payne and Elizabeth Marsh tested the assumption that knowledge blocks the effect, so that repeating that the Atlantic is the largest ocean would not make anyone believe it. Comparing normed knowledge with each participant's own answers on a later knowledge check, they report that <Claim status="reported" by="by Fazio's team">"Contrary to prior suppositions, illusory truth effects occurred even when participants knew better"</Claim> ([Fazio et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000098)). The full article gives the first of two experiments: 40 Duke University undergraduates and 176 general-knowledge questions, half answered correctly by 60% of a norming sample on average and half by 5%; overall, "there was no interaction between knowledge and repetition" ([publisher's copy](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/features/xge-0000098.pdf)). They call it "knowledge neglect, or the failure to rely on stored knowledge, in the face of fluent processing experiences", and add that "Experts may be less susceptible to illusory truth, as long as they possess knowledge directly pertinent to the statements". Neither text read here gives effect sizes.

Briony Swire-Thompson, Joseph DeGutis and David Lazer write that "evidence suggests that the illusory truth effect can be eliminated when veracity is made salient to participants", citing a 2020 study by Brashier, Eliseev and Marsh in which asking people to rate accuracy wiped the effect out for items they knew to be false ([Swire-Thompson et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jarmac.2020.06.006)).

## The principles of persuasion, as Cialdini states them

Cialdini's consultancy, Influence At Work, presents his framework on an undated page whose premise is that overloaded people decide by shortcuts and rules of thumb ([Influence At Work](https://www.influenceatwork.com/7-principles-of-persuasion/)). In his words there, "My own research has identified seven of these shortcuts as universals that guide human behavior".

| Principle | The page's wording | The page's example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reciprocity | "people are obliged to give back to others the form of a behavior, gift, or service that they have received first" | a mint with the bill: tips up about 3%, 14% for two mints |
| Scarcity | "people want more of those things they can have less of" | Concorde sales rose the day after its end was announced in 2003 |
| Authority | "people follow the lead of credible, knowledgeable experts" | a requester in uniform gets change for a parking meter more often |
| Consistency | "People like to be consistent with the things they have previously said or done" | a small card first, then four times as many yeses to a large sign |
| Liking | "People prefer to say yes to those that they like" | negotiators who found something in common: 90% agreement against about 55% |
| Social proof | "Especially when they are uncertain, people will look to the actions and behaviors of others to determine their own" | hotel towel cards, below |
| Unity | listed, not described | none |

Consistency is where the page places commitment: it is activated by asking for small initial commitments. The page cites no study for its figures, and Cialdini's books are not among the sources here, so <Claim status="reported" by="by Cialdini's company">the effect sizes in the table</Claim> are the company's account. One example was replicated independently.

### Social proof and the hotel towels

Noah Goldstein, Cialdini and Vladas Griskevicius ran two field experiments with bathroom cards: "Appeals employing descriptive norms (e.g., 'the majority of guests reuse their towels') proved superior to a traditional appeal widely used by hotels that focused solely on environmental protection" ([Goldstein et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1086/586910)). Reuse was 44.1% against 35.1% in the first experiment, and 49.3% for a same-room norm against 37.2% for the standard message in the second, as a later paper summarises ([Bohner and Schlüter, 2014](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0104086)).

That paper, by Gerd Bohner and Lena Schlüter, repeated the design in two German hotels (N = 724 and N = 204). Every message beat no message, but "descriptive norm messages were not more effective than the standard message, and effects of proximity were inconsistent across studies". They report that in a United States resort study a purely descriptive norm did not raise reuse above a control condition, and that a replication in Austria and Switzerland only partly reproduced the original. Their verdict on <Claim status="disputed" by="by later replications">the norm messages' advantage</Claim>: "Taken together, the available evidence for descriptive norm effects on towel reuse behavior is mixed." They point to culture: German reuse ran at roughly 70 to 90 percent under the messages, against 35 to 50 percent in the United States studies.

## Continued influence: why a retraction leaves a gap

Misinformation, write Ullrich Ecker and eight co-authors, "exerts a lingering influence on people's reasoning after it has been corrected", which they name the continued influence effect ([Ecker et al., 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44159-021-00006-y)). The term titles a 1994 paper by Hollyn Johnson and Colleen Seifert ([Johnson and Seifert, 1994](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.20.6.1420)), and Stephan Lewandowsky's 2012 review asked why retractions are so ineffective at updating memory ([Lewandowsky et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612451018)); only its abstract could be read.

The Debunking Handbook's example: someone learns that a relative's food poisoning story was wrong and accepts the correction, yet may still rely on the original story in other contexts, such as avoiding the restaurant. "Fact-checking and corrections appear to 'work' when you ask people directly about their beliefs", while the false version can resurface in indirectly related decisions. Its advice is causal: "Do not rely on a simple retraction"; instead, "Providing a factual alternative, that is an alternative that fills a causal 'gap' in explaining what happened if the misinformation is corrected, is an effective method of debunking."

Nathan Walter and Riva Tukachinsky measured the residue across 32 studies with 6,527 participants: "on average, correction does not entirely eliminate the effect of misinformation" (r = -.05, p = .045) ([Walter and Tukachinsky, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/0093650219854600)). Their moderators link back to repetition: "Corrections are less effective if the misinformation was attributed to a credible source, the misinformation has been repeated multiple times prior to correction, or when there was a time lag between the delivery of the misinformation and the correction." So <Claim status="established" by="by meta-analysis">corrections reduce but do not eliminate the influence of misinformation</Claim>.

## The backfire effect: a finding and its failed replications

The stronger claim was that correction can make things worse. Brendan Nyhan and Jason Reifler's "When Corrections Fail: The Persistence of Political Misperceptions" (2010) corrected the belief that weapons of mass destruction were found in Iraq during the 2003 invasion ([Nyhan and Reifler, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11109-010-9112-2)). As Swire-Thompson's team summarises it, "Liberal individuals, whose worldview aligned with the correction, were able to successfully update their belief, whereas conservatives increased their belief in the misconception", though a second experiment found this only in a subset of conservatives ([Swire-Thompson et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jarmac.2020.06.006)). The 2012 review asked "why efforts to retract misinformation can even backfire and, ironically, increase misbelief".

The authors' accepted manuscript describes four online experiments (fall 2005 and spring 2006) "with undergraduates at a Catholic university in the Midwest", who read a mock news article with a politician's misleading claim, with or without a correction ([accepted manuscript](https://sites.dartmouth.edu/nyhan/files/2021/03/nyhan-reifler.pdf)). In the first (130 participants), conservatives agreeing that Iraq had such weapons before the invasion rose from 32% without the correction to 64% with it (n = 33), while non-conservatives fell from 22% to 13% (n = 97). In the second round (197 respondents), conservatives agreeing that Bush's tax cuts had increased revenue rose from 36% to 67% (n = 60). The repeated Iraq test worked on conservatives overall, but a post hoc look at 34 respondents who ranked Iraq the top issue found "its effects were null for the most strongly committed conservatives". The authors flag "Standard caveats about generalizing from a convenience sample".

Replications did not find it. Kathryn Haglin, repeating a 2015 flu vaccine study by the same authors: "The backfire effect was not replicated in my experiment" ([Haglin, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/2053168017716547)). Thomas Wood and Ethan Porter's "The Elusive Backfire Effect" ([Wood and Porter, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11109-018-9443-y)) was, for Swire-Thompson's team, the largest failure to replicate. In the abstract, its five experiments "enrolled more than 10,100 subjects and tested 52 issues of potential backfire", and "Across all experiments, we found no corrections capable of triggering backfire" ([publisher's page](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11109-018-9443-y)). The four authors then published a joint study, "Taking Fact-Checks Literally But Not Seriously?" ([Nyhan et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11109-019-09528-x)).

Nyhan's later account is that research and media "seized on this finding, distorting its generality and exaggerating its role", and that "an emerging research consensus finds that corrective information is typically at least somewhat effective at increasing belief accuracy when received by respondents" ([Nyhan, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1912440117)). Swire-Thompson's team concludes "that backfire effects are not a robust empirical phenomenon", noting that 81% of the backfire effects they reviewed came from single-item measures, which are noisy and prone to regression to the mean. The handbook, whose authors include Reifler, Porter and Wood, keeps a margin: "Backfire effects are not as common as we used to think. We cannot reliably predict the circumstances under which they occur." <Claim status="disputed" by="by later replications">A general backfire effect</Claim> is contested, while reports under specific conditions remain open.

### What corrections do instead

The measured effects are positive and modest. A meta-analysis of 30 studies (N = 20,963) found that "Fact-checking has a significantly positive overall influence on political beliefs (d = 0.29)", weakened by prior beliefs and ideology ([Walter et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/10584609.2019.1668894)). In Argentina, Nigeria, South Africa and the United Kingdom, "fact-checks reduced belief in misinformation by at least 0.59 points on a 5-point scale", most effects lasting beyond two weeks ([Porter and Wood, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2104235118)). Nyhan adds that such effects often decay rather than accumulate, so that "misperceptions typically persist in public opinion for years after they have been debunked".

## How much misinformation do people see?

Jennifer Allen, Duncan Watts and colleagues combined television, desktop and mobile panels for the United States from January 2016 to December 2018, counting time on 98 listed websites as fake news: "fake news comprises only 0.15% of Americans' daily media diet" ([Allen et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aay3539)). They add that "our results do not imply that fake news is not a problem worthy of attention", and that false content from reliable outlets or unlinked social posts escapes their scheme. On Twitter in 2016, "Only 1% of individuals accounted for 80% of fake news source exposures" among registered voters ([Grinberg et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aau2706)); on Facebook in 2016, sharing such content was relatively rare, and users over 65 shared nearly seven times as many such articles as the youngest group ([Guess et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aau4586)).

Sacha Altay, Manon Berriche and Alberto Acerbi argue that "Alarmist narratives about online misinformation continue to gain traction despite evidence that its prevalence and impact are overstated", citing 0.16% of the French media diet ([Altay et al., 2023](https://iris.unitn.it/bitstream/11572/361723/6/altay-et-al-2023-misinformation-on-misinformation-conceptual-and-methodological-challenges.pdf)). Ceren Budak, Nyhan and colleagues document "a pattern of low exposure to false and inflammatory content that is concentrated among a narrow fringe", adding that evidence is thin outside the United States and Western Europe, where harms may be greater ([Budak et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07417-w)).

Ecker, Lewandowsky, Sander van der Linden and colleagues reply that "the prevalence of misinformation is nonnegligible if reasonably inclusive definitions are applied" and that "misinformation has causal impacts on important beliefs and behaviors" ([Ecker et al., 2024](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/why-misinformation-must-not-be-ignored/)). <Claim status="disputed">The size of the problem</Claim> thus depends on the definition, the population and the outcome measured.

No source here measures misinformation in Brazilian news diets with passive data. The Reuters Institute's 2025 Brazil report records that <Claim status="reported" by="by Rodrigo Carro">"an avalanche of digital misinformation forced the government in January to withdraw a new set of regulations aimed at combating tax evasion"</Claim>, after claims that instant money transfers would be taxed, which the report calls "an entirely false claim" ([Reuters Institute, 2025](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2025/brazil)).

## What to remember

- Repetition raises rated truth (51 studies, 2010); in a 2021 study of 567 United Kingdom adults the effect shrank from 0.67 to 0.14 points on a seven-point scale over one month.
- Knowledge does not fully protect (Fazio et al., 2015), but focusing people on accuracy removed the effect for items they knew to be false.
- Cialdini's company cites no studies for its seven principles' figures; the one example replicated independently, the hotel towels, did not show the norm advantage in Germany.
- Corrections reduce but do not erase misinformation (32 studies, r = -.05), work worse after repetition or delay, and work better with a causal alternative.
- The 2010 backfire effect, measured on students, did not replicate as a general effect: Wood and Porter found none across 52 issues, and Nyhan later wrote that its generality had been distorted.
- Fact-checks reduce false belief (d = 0.29 across 30 studies), but the effects often decay.
- Exposure is disputed: 0.15% of the United States media diet on a list-based definition, non-negligible on inclusive ones per Ecker and colleagues, and unmeasured for Brazil here.
