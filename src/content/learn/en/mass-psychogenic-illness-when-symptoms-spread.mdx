A teacher smells something like petrol and feels sick; that day 80 students and 19 staff go to an emergency department, and the laboratories later find nothing ([Jones and colleagues, 2000](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=10631279)). A cheerleader wakes from a nap with a twitch that will not stop, and months later eighteen students at her school have tics ([National Public Radio (NPR), as published by the station WWNO, 10 March 2012](https://www.wwno.org/2012-03-10/the-curious-case-of-teen-tics-in-le-roy-n-y)). A vaccination round at a school is followed by fainting girls on national television, and a country's vaccine coverage falls for years ([Simas and colleagues, 2019](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6363158/fullTextXML)). Physicians call this pattern mass psychogenic illness (MPI): symptoms that are real, involuntary and shared, spreading through a group by sight, sound and belief rather than by a toxin or a germ. This part of the series opened by [Mass hysteria: how groups come to believe anything](/learn/mass-hysteria-how-groups-come-to-believe-anything) sets out what the term means and where it came from, the features investigators look for, the classic and recent cases with their numbers, the cases where the label was wrong, and the outbreaks where a poisoning is still alleged. The mechanisms of imitation and expectation behind it, the nocebo effect among them, are in [Conformity, cascades and contagion](/learn/conformity-cascades-and-contagion-the-mechanisms); the medieval forms are in [Dancing plagues and possessed convents](/learn/dancing-plagues-and-possessed-convents).

## What the term covers

### A definition and two syndromes

The most cited definition comes from the sociologist Robert Bartholomew and the psychiatrist Simon Wessely: mass sociogenic illness "refers to the rapid spread of illness signs and symptoms affecting members of a cohesive group, originating from a nervous system disturbance involving excitation, loss or alteration of function, whereby physical complaints that are exhibited unconsciously have no corresponding organic aetiology" ([Bartholomew and Wessely, British Journal of Psychiatry 180(4), April 2002](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/protean-nature-of-mass-sociogenic-illness/2BDC2262E104B8A33F3DD49773DA0D8B)). They noted that the psychiatric manuals of the time placed it under "somatoform disorder", as "conversion disorder" (same). The New York State Department of Health (NYSDOH) used a shorter version in 2012: "a group of symptoms suggestive of organic disease but without an identified cause in a group of individuals with shared beliefs about the cause of symptoms" ([NYSDOH, interim report on Le Roy, 31 January 2012](https://www.health.ny.gov/press/releases/2012/leroy/docs/investigation_summary.pdf)). The words mass sociogenic illness, mass psychogenic illness and epidemic hysteria are used for the same thing; neurologists who treated one outbreak write that the older name "mass hysteria" has "fallen out of favor because of its derogatory implications toward women" ([McVige, Masset, Mechtler and colleagues, Journal of Child Neurology 41, April 2026, full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC13018255/fullTextXML)). The word hysteria itself comes from the Greek name of the uterus, which Hippocrates blamed for the disorder ([Tasca, Rapetti, Carta and Fadda, Clinical Practice and Epidemiology in Mental Health 8, 2012, full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC3480686/fullTextXML)); the history of that word is told in the series opener.

In 1987 Wessely divided the outbreaks into two kinds. "Mass anxiety hysteria" consists of "episodes of acute anxiety, occurring mainly in schoolchildren", with no prior tension, spreading fast "by visual contact"; treatment "consists of separating the participants and the prognosis is good". "Mass motor hysteria" consists of "abnormalities in motor behaviour", occurs in any age group, follows prior tension, spreads gradually and "may be prolonged" ([Wessely, Psychological Medicine 17(1), February 1987, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=3575566)). Bartholomew and Wessely later argued that before 1900 most reports were of the motor kind, in convents, factories and strict schools, while in the twentieth century anxiety outbreaks triggered by "an innocuous odour or food poisoning rumours" dominated, and that from the 1980s fears of chemical and biological terrorism became a recurrent theme ([Bartholomew and Wessely, 2002](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/protean-nature-of-mass-sociogenic-illness/2BDC2262E104B8A33F3DD49773DA0D8B)).

### The features investigators look for

Bartholomew and Wessely listed eight features whose combination, in their view, allows a preliminary diagnosis before air, food and water tests come back ([Bartholomew and Wessely, 2002](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/protean-nature-of-mass-sociogenic-illness/2BDC2262E104B8A33F3DD49773DA0D8B)):

| Feature | What it looks like in an outbreak |
| --- | --- |
| No plausible organic basis | tests of blood, urine, air and water find nothing that explains the symptoms |
| Transient and benign symptoms | headache, dizziness, nausea, fainting, breathlessness that pass |
| Rapid onset and recovery | cases appear within minutes or hours and resolve when people are separated |
| A segregated group | a school, a factory, a ward, a convent |
| Extraordinary anxiety | a rumour, an odour, a war, an exam season |
| Spread by sight, sound or word of mouth | people fall ill after seeing others fall ill |
| Spread down the age scale | older or higher-status members first |
| A preponderance of women and girls | most attack rates are higher in girls |

Timothy Jones, then an officer of the Epidemic Intelligence Service of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and colleagues measured several of these features in 1998 (below). A study by Lisa Page, Simon Wessely and colleagues found that, in a random sample of 280 chemical incidents, 19 were probable MPI, "16% of incidents for which people reported symptoms and 7% of all incidents"; "Odor was a robust predictor", and the episodes were "especially likely to occur in schools or healthcare facilities" ([Page and colleagues, Epidemiology 21(5), September 2010, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=20592616)). A meta-analysis of 28 studies of children and adolescents published between 2010 and 2020 put the pooled attack rate at 9.8%, found the rate in girls 2.43 times that in boys, the highest rate in primary schools (15.4%) and higher rates in rural than urban areas (11.1% against 5.6%) ([Zhao, Cheng, Dong and Xie, Journal of International Medical Research 49(12), December 2021, full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8829737/fullTextXML)).

### Real illness, not faking

The symptoms are not invented. The United States (US) National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke (NINDS) describes functional neurological disorder (FND), the clinical category under which these symptoms now fall, as caused by "changes in how brain networks work", with symptoms that "are genuine" and "not intentionally produced", and warns that "Physicians may wrongly consider symptoms of FND as feigning or faking, which can leave patients feeling stigmatized" ([NINDS, Functional Neurologic Disorder, last reviewed 13 March 2026](https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/disorders/functional-neurologic-disorder)). Commenting on the Tennessee outbreak, Wessely said the affected students experienced authentic pain and suffering, and that it is difficult to explain that without "blaming or demeaning the victims" ([Science News, 15 January 2000](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/mass-illness-tied-contagious-fear)). Jones told Education Week that the diagnosis was not a criticism of those affected: "This is an example of the powerful impact environment can have on you" ([Education Week, 2 February 2000](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/health-update/2000/02)). In Brazil, the psychiatrist Renato Marchetti of the University of São Paulo (USP) said of the adolescents his team examined in Acre that, if the vaccine was not the cause, neither had the patients faked the illness ([Exame, 1 December 2019](https://exame.com/brasil/apos-reacao-adversa-governo-nega-problemas-com-a-vacina-contra-o-hpv/)).

The diagnostic logic has also changed. The neurologists who treated the Le Roy cohort write that MPI was "previously regarded as a diagnosis of exclusion" and "is now considered a 'rule-in' diagnosis" under the fifth edition, text revision, of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5-TR), whose criteria for functional neurologic symptom disorder require "evidence of incompatibility between the symptom and recognized neurological or medical conditions" ([McVige and colleagues, 2026](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC13018255/fullTextXML)).

### When the label was wrong

A diagnosis that needs only the absence of a toxin can hide a toxin. Bartholomew and Wessely themselves listed cases: a "cursory environmental probe" that diagnosed MPI among garment workers in Puerto Rico "was later traced to toxic fumes that had caused respiratory and degenerative diseases, and some deaths"; a 1930s "epidemic hysteria" at a rayon plant attributed to carbon disulphide; and a 1990 outbreak at a London school with "classic features" (a high female attack rate, rapid onset and recovery, over-breathing, line-of-sight spread) in which "Tests later revealed cucumber pesticide contamination" ([Bartholomew and Wessely, 2002](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/protean-nature-of-mass-sociogenic-illness/2BDC2262E104B8A33F3DD49773DA0D8B)). They also recorded that critics had argued, in letters about the Tennessee case, that rapidly dissipating volatile organic compounds could have produced short-lived symptoms "erroneously attributed to mass sociogenic illness" (same). The World Health Organization's (WHO) regional office warned in 2012, about Afghanistan, that "genuine complaints may be missed out and the consequences may be catastrophic" ([WHO Eastern Mediterranean Regional Office, Weekly Epidemiological Monitor 5(22), 27 May 2012](https://applications.emro.who.int/dsaf/epi/2012/Epi_Monitor_2012_5_22.pdf)). And in 1994 Bartholomew questioned the category itself, arguing that diagnoses rest on "subjective, ambiguous categories that reflect stereotypes of female normality" and that some episodes "may involve normal, rational people" whose conduct codes differ from the investigators' ([Bartholomew, Psychological Medicine 24(2), May 1994, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=8084927)).

### Tanganyika, 1962: the laughing epidemic and its later numbers

The outbreak began on 30 January 1962 at a mission-run girls' middle school at Kashasha, 25 miles from Bukoba; by 18 March, when the school closed, "95 of the 159 pupils had been affected", and 57 more were affected after it reopened on 21 May ([Rankin and Philip, Central African Journal of Medicine 9, May 1963, transcription posted by the WNYC producer Ellen Horne on 31 May 2007](https://rltz.blogspot.com/2007/05)); the paper itself is indexed but not online ([PubMed record](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=13973013)). The two doctors described attacks of laughing and crying "lasting for a few minutes to a few hours", no fever, no fatal cases, symptoms of up to 16 days; spread to the village complex of Nshamba, where "217 people out of a total 10,000 were attacked", to Ramashenye girls' school (48 of 154) and to the village of Kanyangereka, where two boys' schools closed (same). Lumbar punctures in 17 cases, virus studies in 15 and checks of maize flour found nothing; in Bukoba some believed that "the atmosphere has been poisoned" by atom bomb explosions, others that "someone has poisoned the maize flour"; the authors concluded that "mass hysteria seems the most likely explanation" and called it "probably a culturally determined disease" (same). Their paper also asserted that "No literate and relatively sophisticated members of society have been attacked", a framing that the historian Yolana Pringle places in a psychiatry still struggling "to deal with the legacy of colonial racism", in which such epidemics seemed to confirm colonial warnings "that westernization would eventually result in mass mental instability" ([Pringle, Journal of the History of Medicine and Allied Sciences 70(1), January 2015, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=24191308)).

The numbers grew in the retelling. A Brazilian newspaper wrote in August 2026 that the outbreak affected "cerca de mil pessoas ao longo de dezoito meses" (about a thousand people over eighteen months) and paralysed "mais de catorze escolas" (more than fourteen schools), citing the original study ([Correio Braziliense, 24 August 2026](https://www.correiobraziliense.com.br/cbradar/a-histeria-coletiva-bizarra-que-comecou-em-uma-escola-contagiou-milhares-de-pessoas-e-causou-uma-epidemia-incontrolavel-de-risos-na-africa/)). The original, written while the outbreak was still spreading, gives the counts above and no total; Pringle's later review of the East African reports says each epidemic "reportedly affected between three hundred and six hundred people" ([Pringle, 2015](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=24191308)). <Claim status="disputed">The total of about a thousand people and fourteen schools</Claim> rests on later accounts, not on the doctors' report read here.

### Tennessee, 1998: a smell and a hundred patients

In November 1998 a teacher at a Tennessee high school noticed a "gasoline-like" smell and soon had headache, nausea, shortness of breath and dizziness; the school was evacuated, "80 students and 19 staff members went to the emergency room", 38 were kept overnight, and five days after reopening "another 71 persons went to the emergency room" ([Jones, Craig, Hoy and colleagues, New England Journal of Medicine 342(2), 13 January 2000, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=10631279)). Blood and urine showed no carbon monoxide, volatile organic compounds, pesticides, polychlorinated biphenyls, paraquat or mercury, and the environment held no toxic compounds; a questionnaire a month later linked symptoms to female sex, "seeing another ill person, knowing that a classmate was ill, and reporting an unusual odor" (same). The school was Warren County High School in McMinnville, with 1,800 students ([Education Week, 2 February 2000](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/health-update/2000/02)); the ill came from 36 classrooms "that weren't along any specific air-distribution route" ([Science News, 15 January 2000](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/mass-illness-tied-contagious-fear)). The authors wrote that relieving the anxiety "requires prompt recognition and a detailed investigation" ([Jones and colleagues, 2000](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=10631279)). The letters that disputed the diagnosis are summarized above.

### Le Roy, New York, 2011 and 2012: tics, cameras and a chemical spill

The state's interim report of 31 January 2012 counted tic symptoms in 12 female students aged 13 to 19 at Le Roy Junior/Senior High School, onsets from May to December 2011; three had earlier tic-related conditions, one with a previous Tourette's diagnosis; eight were diagnosed with conversion disorder by a paediatric neurologist; "Significant life stressors were identified in 11 of the cases" ([NYSDOH, 31 January 2012](https://www.health.ny.gov/press/releases/2012/leroy/docs/investigation_summary.pdf)). It found no common exposure, water that met all standards, and no route from the 1970 Lehigh Valley Railroad derailment site, "approximately 3.5 miles east of the school", or from two other sites; seven girls had received the human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine, six of them more than a year before onset; and the doctors consulted "considered and rejected" pediatric autoimmune neuropsychiatric disorders associated with streptococcal infections (PANDAS) (same). Its conclusion: "this cluster of cases to be the result of conversion disorder/mass psychogenic illness" (same).

The families were not all persuaded. At a meeting on 11 January a state health official refused to disclose the diagnosis, citing privacy and a wish not to see the girls "labeled"; a father told reporters he did not care about the privacy law and wanted the cause found ([The Batavian, 12 January 2012](https://thebatavian.com/node/29933)). On 28 January an investigator sent by the environmental campaigner Erin Brockovich, Robert Bowcock, tried to take samples on school grounds and was stopped; he said that in his opinion the district's environmental reports were not "even close to science", and the superintendent called the visit unprofessional ([The Batavian, 28 January 2012](https://thebatavian.com/node/30187)); the superintendent's letter to families then said the visit "was clearly staged as a publicity stunt" ([WBFO via Buffalo Toronto Public Media, 3 February 2012](https://www.btpm.org/2012-02-03/epa-re-examines-site-near-leroy-high-school)). A neurologist from New Jersey proposed PANDAS, and some parents accepted it ([Good Morning America, 7 February 2012](https://www.goodmorningamerica.com/news/story/parents-demand-environmental-investigation-tic-illness-affecting-ny-15529671)). Bowcock's own well samples indicated that the trichloroethene plume had not moved towards the school ([The Batavian, 10 February 2012](https://thebatavian.com/node/30404)); the federal Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) began removing 235 drums of soil left at the derailment site for four decades and found no tested contaminants in 203 of them, and Brockovich, while saying her team had found no link to the tics, accused the agency of having "dropped the ball" on the site ([ABC News, 23 February 2012](https://abcnews.go.com/blogs/headlines/2012/02/erin-brockovich-slams-epa-over-toxic-waste-superfund-site-in-le-roy)). In June a commissioned firm reported "no adverse health impacts" from air, soil or water at the school ([The Batavian, 13 June 2012](https://thebatavian.com/node/32362)).

## Vaccines and psychogenic reactions

### What health authorities call it

Fainting and stress reactions around injections are recognized adverse events following immunization. A joint note of the Brazilian Society of Pediatrics (Sociedade Brasileira de Pediatria, SBP) and the Brazilian Society of Immunizations (Sociedade Brasileira de Imunizações, SBIm) explains that "reações de estresse vacinal" (stress reactions to vaccination) have no biological causal link to the vaccine but a psychological one, that the name was adopted officially by the WHO, and that such reactions can turn into a cluster; it also records that the National Health Surveillance Agency (Agência Nacional de Vigilância Sanitária, Anvisa) added a warning on psychogenic reactions to the HPV vaccine's leaflet in the Diário Oficial da União of 14 April 2023 ([SBP and SBIm, joint note 63, 2 May 2023](https://sbim.org.br/images/24035c-NEspConj_-_ReacoesPsicogenicas_e_vacinas.pdf_2025-07-28.pdf)). The same note lists group reactions since 1992 with cholera vaccine in Vietnam, H1N1 in China, tetanus-diphtheria in Jordan, Iran and Canada, hepatitis B in Italy and Spain, and HPV in Colombia, Japan and Denmark (same).

### El Carmen de Bolívar, 2014: an official finding and its critics

Colombia introduced school-based HPV vaccination in August 2012 and reached "over 90% of the target population in the first year" ([Simas, Munoz, Arregoces and Larson, Human Vaccines & Immunotherapeutics 15(1), 2019, full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6363158/fullTextXML)). Between 29 May and 2 June 2014, 15 girls from one school in El Carmen de Bolívar were admitted with fast heartbeat, breathlessness and numb limbs; videos spread on national television and YouTube, and "over 600 cases" followed across Colombia; the peaks of admissions "coincided with peaks in media coverage and visits of Ministry of Health officials and politicians", and "No cases were reported during the weekends and holiday periods" (same). The National Institute of Health (Instituto Nacional de Salud, INS) investigated 517 girls with two or more symptoms and "No organic association between adverse reactions and the HPV vaccination was found" (same); a 2022 review of 37 texts says the INS report, presented in January 2015, analysed 629 cases, set the outbreak between 28 May and 15 October 2014 and, unable to determine the cause, called it "un evento de etiología desconocida" (an event of unknown etiology) while proposing mass psychogenic illness as the hypothesis ([Baltar-Moreno, Puello-Sarabia and Carrazco-Delgado, Revista Gerencia y Políticas de Salud 21, 30 October 2022](https://revistas.javeriana.edu.co/index.php/gerepolsal/article/download/34614/28546)). President Juan Manuel Santos said publicly that the phenomenon was probably a collective suggestion ([Baltar-Moreno and colleagues, 2022](https://revistas.javeriana.edu.co/index.php/gerepolsal/article/download/34614/28546)); the diagnosis was not given to parents directly by officials, but broadcast in the news, which Simas and colleagues say "contributed to outrage" ([Simas and colleagues, 2019](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6363158/fullTextXML)).

Coverage collapsed: "By 2016, HPV vaccine uptake among eligible girls declined to 14% for the first dose and 5% for the complete course, down from 98% and 88%, respectively, in 2012" ([Simas and colleagues, 2019](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6363158/fullTextXML)). The dispute that followed is on the record:

- Andrés Idrovo and colleagues wrote that Simas and colleagues had left out a government case-control study (137 cases and 437 controls) in which having received the HPV vaccine was associated with the event, with an adjusted odds ratio of 10.8 (95% confidence interval 1.4 to 80.2), under the title "more uncertainty is required" ([Idrovo, Pinilla-Monsalve and Manrique-Hernández, Human Vaccines & Immunotherapeutics 15(5), 2019, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=30735450)).
- Simas and colleagues replied that the paper had said the reaction was "provoked by anxiety and perceptions of risk following the HPV vaccination", that the WHO safety committee had met seven times since 2007 and "considers HPV vaccines to be extremely safe", and that emphasizing uncertainty would "undermine the robust evidence" ([Simas and colleagues, reply letter, 4 April 2019](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6605877/fullTextXML)). Two of the authors disclosed a collaborative research grant from the vaccine maker GlaxoSmithKline on maternal vaccine acceptance (same).
- A study by the University of Cartagena commissioned by the department of Bolívar, with 283 affected girls, again found no cause-effect relation with the vaccine but said that, four years on, many were still clinically ill and needed care; social-science studies criticized the authorities' handling and the hierarchy between Bogotá investigators and young rural women ([Baltar-Moreno and colleagues, 2022](https://revistas.javeriana.edu.co/index.php/gerepolsal/article/download/34614/28546)).

In a separate case from Cali, the Constitutional Court decided on 2 June 2017 that the vaccine would not be suspended because "no fue posible demostrar -con grado de certeza científica-" (it was not possible to show with scientific certainty) that it caused the girl's illnesses, ordered her health insurer to give her full diagnosis and care, and warned the Ministry of Health that the vaccine "no puede imponerse contra la voluntad" (cannot be imposed against people's will) and needs informed consent ([Corte Constitucional de Colombia, Sentencia T-365 de 2017](https://www.corteconstitucional.gov.co/relatoria/2017/T-365-17.htm)). The review of 2022 concludes that the available explanations "solamente puedan ser tomadas como provisionales" (can only be taken as provisional) ([Baltar-Moreno and colleagues, 2022](https://revistas.javeriana.edu.co/index.php/gerepolsal/article/download/34614/28546)).

### Brazil: Bertioga in 2014 and Acre from 2018

In the first week of September 2014, during the second stage of the national HPV campaign, 11 girls at a state school in Bertioga, on the São Paulo coast, had headache, dizziness and weakness about two hours after vaccination; eight went home the same day and three were moved to a hospital in Santos ([Agência Brasil via Uai, 8 September 2014](https://www.uai.com.br/app/noticia/saude/2014/09/08/noticias-saude,191645/amp.html)). A Folha de S.Paulo report reproduced locally said the girls had lost sensation in their legs and that the city would open an inquiry ([Correio do Estado, 7 September 2014](https://correiodoestado.com.br/cidades/menores-perdem-sensibilidade-das-pernas-e-sao-internadas-apos-vacina-c/226651/)). The head of the state immunization department, Helena Sato, said: "Trata-se, muito provavelmente, de uma reação de ansiedade após a vacinação" (it is very probably an anxiety reaction after vaccination), adding that there was no risk of the paraplegia that had been reported; the federal health surveillance secretary, Jarbas Barbosa, called it a post-vaccination stress syndrome and said the same batch had been used elsewhere without similar reports ([Agência Brasil via Uai, 8 September 2014](https://www.uai.com.br/app/noticia/saude/2014/09/08/noticias-saude,191645/amp.html)).

In Rio Branco, Acre, 72 notifications of adverse events after the quadrivalent HPV vaccine were recorded between 30 May 2018 and 31 July 2019, 16 of them serious; at the Ministry of Health's request, a team of the Institute of Psychiatry of the University of São Paulo's Hospital das Clínicas evaluated 12 patients between 3 June and 7 September 2019 with about 14 days of video electroencephalography each ([Marchetti, Gallucci Neto and Proença, presentation to the Chamber of Deputies' social security and family committee, presented on 20 November 2019 according to Aos Fatos](https://www2.camara.leg.br/atividade-legislativa/comissoes/comissoes-permanentes/cssf/arquivos/GALLUCCIRENATOEINAHCNEPHPVparaCmaraDeputados.pdf)). Ten had psychogenic non-epileptic seizures, which the team called a "Doença psicogênica pós-vacinal de curso crônico" (a chronic post-vaccination psychogenic illness), and two siblings had a genetic generalized epilepsy; the factors listed included material deprivation, anti-vaccination beliefs, poor conditions at vaccination, delayed diagnosis, "Hostilização por agentes de saúde" (hostility from health workers) and "Reforço e contágio pela rede social" (reinforcement and contagion through social media) (same). The Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) reported the conclusions to the state government on 28 November 2019: the seizures were psychogenic crises "causadas pelo estresse do ato de vacinar" (caused by the stress of being vaccinated), not by the vaccine ([PAHO, 29 November 2019](https://www.paho.org/pt/noticias/29-11-2019-estudo-conclui-que-sintomas-neurologicos-pos-vacinacao-contra-hpv-no-acre)). The report also described a Facebook network of relatives spreading anti-vaccine content and health workers who treated the adolescents as "pacientes que não têm nada" (patients with nothing wrong) ([Aos Fatos, 2 December 2019](https://www.aosfatos.org/noticias/relatorio-da-usp-cita-desinformacao-entre-causas-de-rejeicao-vacina-anti-hpv-no-acre/)). In 2026 the state's immunization coordinator said notifications had risen from 14 to 127 in six months out of fear, that fewer than 10% of adolescents came for the vaccine in 2018 and 2019, and that in 2025 the state's coverage was 59% for girls and 50% for boys against national averages of 86% and 74.5% ([Jornal de Brasília, 2 June 2026](https://jornaldebrasilia.com.br/noticias/saude/acre-tenta-recuperar-confianca-e-ampliar-vacinacao-contra-hpv/)). That report dates the episode to 2017, while the USP team's notification window starts in May 2018 (same; [Chamber presentation](https://www2.camara.leg.br/atividade-legislativa/comissoes/comissoes-permanentes/cssf/arquivos/GALLUCCIRENATOEINAHCNEPHPVparaCmaraDeputados.pdf)). The longer story of vaccine scares is in [Health scares: from the Revolta da Vacina to the autism myth](/learn/health-scares-from-the-revolta-da-vacina-to-the-autism-myth).

## Tics on screens, 2019 to 2022

From 2019, Tourette clinics in several countries saw young people with sudden, complex, tic-like movements and words. In Germany, Kirsten Müller-Vahl and colleagues traced their first cases, in May 2019, to a popular YouTube channel launched in February of that year, whose behaviours their patients reproduced, sometimes word for word; they proposed a new name, "mass social media-induced illness" (MSMI), for an illness spread "solely via social media" ([Müller-Vahl, Pisarenko, Jakubovski and Fremer, Brain 145(2), published 23 August 2021, full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9014744/fullTextXML)). In Calgary, all 20 patients with rapid-onset functional tic-like behaviours (FTLBs) in a registry had onset after March 2020 and "all endorsed exposure to influencers on social media (mainly TikTok)"; 19 of the 20 were female, against 21% among patients with primary tic disorders ([Pringsheim, Ganos, McGuire and colleagues, Movement Disorders 36(12), December 2021, full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8441698/fullTextXML)). A London team reported the rise in children's tics and tic-like attacks during the pandemic ([Heyman, Liang and Hedderly, Archives of Disease in Childhood 106(5), 2021, PubMed record](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=33677431)).

Clinicians agree that these are not Tourette tics, and differ on what to call them. A German comparison of 13 patients with 13 matched Tourette patients found features that "discriminated perfectly", such as abrupt onset and "symptom deterioration in the presence of others" ([Paulus and colleagues, Movement Disorders 36(12), December 2021, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=34558735)). An international registry of 294 patients from 10 centres found 87% female, peak severity within a month in 70%, spontaneous remission in 20%, and "Almost 60% explicitly reported exposure to tic-related social media content" ([Martino and colleagues, European Journal of Neurology 30(2), February 2023, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=36282623)). The European Society for the Study of Tourette Syndrome agreed diagnostic criteria for FTLBs in 2022, noting that they "lack prospective testing of their sensitivity and specificity" ([Pringsheim and colleagues, European Journal of Neurology 30(4), April 2023, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=36587367)). Müller-Vahl's group rejects treating its patients simply as a functional movement disorder rather than as "affected persons of an MSMI outbreak" ([Müller-Vahl and colleagues, 2021](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9014744/fullTextXML)); its German household survey of 9,253 people put the prevalence of probable cases at 0.36% and of highly likely cases at 0.17%, most of them male ([Hartung and colleagues, Journal of Psychiatric Research 177, September 2024, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=39033669)). <Claim status="disputed">Whether these cases are a new kind of mass psychogenic illness or individual functional disorders shaped by social modelling</Claim> remains a question of classification more than of facts. An Italian follow-up of 11 patients found tic severity and anxiety improved over 12 months while depressive symptoms did not ([Prato and colleagues, Frontiers in Pediatrics 10, January 2023, full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9869029/fullTextXML)). The platform side of this story is in [Social media and the contagion of symptoms and beliefs](/learn/social-media-and-the-contagion-of-symptoms-and-beliefs).

## Contested outbreaks: when poisoning is alleged

### Afghanistan, 2009 to 2012 and 2023

The WHO's regional office counted 5 episodes with 297 cases in 2009, 8 with 370 in 2010, 5 with 119 in 2011 and 6 with 848 in 2012, "over 1634 cases from 22 schools" and no deaths; it noted that outbreaks "were believed to be the work of political elements in the country who oppose girls education", that "investigations into the causes of these outbreaks have yielded no such evidence so far", and that doctors believed it was MPI ([WHO Eastern Mediterranean Regional Office, 27 May 2012](https://applications.emro.who.int/dsaf/epi/2012/Epi_Monitor_2012_5_22.pdf)). An interior ministry spokesman told Agence France-Presse (AFP) that "So far no evidence or any traces of any kind of poison or gas have been found", and a spokesman for the North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) force said that initial tests of samples from Khost "were negative for any organic compounds like poisons or other toxic material" ([AFP via Morocco World News, 28 May 2012](https://www.moroccoworldnews.com/2012/05/152103/doubt-cast-over-attacks-on-afghan-schoolgirls/)). The Taliban denied any role (same).

Then the National Directorate of Security (NDS), the intelligence service, announced on 6 June 2012 the arrest of 15 people, 12 of them Taliban, among them two schoolgirls, and made their confessions public; the health minister said an unknown powder had been used ([Pajhwok Afghan News, 6 June 2012](https://pajhwok.com/2012/06/06/15-held-poisoning-schoolgirls-mashal/); [Al Jazeera, 7 June 2012](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2012/6/7/taliban-arrested-for-afghan-school-poisonings)). <Claim status="alleged" by="by the NDS in June 2012">The Taliban had bribed school staff and a pupil to poison the schools</Claim>, a claim the Taliban spokesman rejected as an attempt "to defame the rebels" ([Pajhwok, 6 June 2012](https://pajhwok.com/2012/06/06/15-held-poisoning-schoolgirls-mashal/)). The United Nations Assistance Mission in Afghanistan (UNAMA) said it was concerned that the confessions had been obtained by torture and that publicizing them violated the presumption of innocence; Human Rights Watch's researcher Heather Barr said "you've gotten a bunch of people to confess to a poisoning where no actual poison seems to have been involved", and saw the government "becoming more aggressive in terms of their own psychological operations"; the interior ministry denied the torture claims ([Al Jazeera, 12 July 2012](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2012/7/12/torture-alleged-in-afghan-poisoning-arrests)). How states use fear and confessions is the subject of [State influence campaigns on the record](/learn/state-influence-campaigns-on-the-record) and of [How manipulators harness collective panic](/learn/how-manipulators-harness-collective-panic). In June 2023, under Taliban rule, nearly 80 girls at two primary schools in Sar-e Pul province were hospitalized; the provincial education head said "someone with a grudge paid a third party to carry out the attacks" and gave no detail of the substance ([NBC News, from the Associated Press, 5 June 2023](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/world/schoolgirls-poisoned-hospitalized-afghanistan-taliban-rcna87703); [Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty's Gandhara, 4 June 2023](https://gandhara.rferl.org/a/afghanistan-girls-poisoned/32444237.html)).

### Iran, 2022 and 2023

The Iranian episodes began on 30 November 2022 at a girls' school in Qom, during the "Woman, Life, Freedom" protests, and continued at hundreds of schools until at least May 2023 ([Ulbricht and Weiner, School Poisonings in Iran: An Open-Source Investigation, Stanford Law School, October 2025](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Final-Iran-Report-v4-.pdf)). The report, by two Stanford lawyers with an open-source archive, compiles the official statements, which changed over time (all as the report translates them from Iranian state media):

| Date | Official or body | Statement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 26 February 2023 | Deputy health minister Younes Panahi | "some people wanted all schools, especially girls' schools, to be closed"; "what has been proven is a chemical compound"; retracted a day later |
| 1 March 2023 | Interior minister Ahmad Vahidi | more than 90% of students were not poisoned but stressed |
| 6 March 2023 | Supreme Leader, as reported by the Iranian Students' News Agency (ISNA) | "a major and unforgiveable crime", perpetrators to be "severely punished" |
| 6 March 2023 | Deputy health minister Saeed Karimi | "less than 10 percent of affected children actually encountered a [toxin]" |
| 7 March 2023 | Deputy interior minister Majid Mirahmadi | "no toxic substances or poisoning caused by toxic substances so far"; 95% unwell from stress |
| 13 March 2023 | Tasnim news agency | 13,000 students discharged and treated |
| 14 April 2023 | Health ministry | "in over 90% of cases, no poisoning or toxicity was observed" |
| 28 April 2023 | Intelligence ministry | five causes, from stink bombs and pepper spray to mass hysteria; no toxic substance |
| 11 June 2023 | Parliamentary fact-finding group | final report to the Speaker, never published |

Source for every row: [Ulbricht and Weiner, October 2025](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Final-Iran-Report-v4-.pdf). The intelligence ministry's statement said that "Toxic substances have not been spread in any of the country's schools, but non-toxic agents that have caused panic have been used inadvertently or intentionally in some reported settings", and that groups inside and outside the country had spread rumours to close schools and blame the state ([Iran Times, 12 May 2023](https://iran-times.com/intel-ministry-says-there-has-not-been-even-a-single-school-poisoning-in-iran/?amp=1)). Officials blamed foreign Persian-language media for stirring panic, a Basij commander said enemies were creating "an atmosphere of fear and psychological warfare", and by August 2023 the United Nations fact-finding mission recorded 48 court cases against people accused of spreading rumours or fear about the poisonings ([Ulbricht and Weiner, 2025](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Final-Iran-Report-v4-.pdf)).

The two readings, each with its evidence:

- <Claim status="attributed" by="by the Iranian intelligence and health ministries">Most incidents were anxiety and mass hysteria, the rest stink bombs, pepper spray or mischief</Claim>, based, by their account, on field and laboratory tests and short hospital stays ([Ulbricht and Weiner, 2025](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Final-Iran-Report-v4-.pdf)). The sociologist Robert Bartholomew told the Islamic Republic News Agency (IRNA) in March 2023 that "mass mental illness is most likely the main cause", given that no one had been arrested and no chemical detected (same).
- <Claim status="disputed" by="by Ulbricht and Weiner">The symptoms were likely caused by something physical rather than psychological</Claim>: their 13 verified case files include leg numbness and inability to walk lasting days, affected teachers and longer hospital stays; officials had at times said toxins were found; toxicology results were never published; and they conclude they are "doubtful that the vast majority of episodes were the result of anxiety or mass hysteria" ([Ulbricht and Weiner, 2025](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Final-Iran-Report-v4-.pdf)). They add that the authors were not able to work directly with those affected and that their methods "are not foolproof" (same).

No public toxicology report from the Iranian investigations exists in the sources read here, and no trial of a perpetrator for the poisonings is reported in them ([Ulbricht and Weiner, 2025](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Final-Iran-Report-v4-.pdf)). Contested outbreaks of other kinds, from the gasser of Mattoon to Havana syndrome, are in [Phantom attackers and contested outbreaks](/learn/phantom-attackers-and-contested-outbreaks).

## Brazil beyond vaccines

On 8 April 2022, the last day of an exam week, 26 students from different classes at a state secondary school in Recife had anxiety crises with sweating, low oxygen saturation and fast heartbeat; 16 professionals in six ambulances and two motorcycle units of the emergency service, the Serviço de Atendimento Móvel de Urgência (SAMU), attended them at the school and none went to hospital ([TNH1, 9 April 2022](https://www.tnh1.com.br/noticia/nid/recife-26-alunos-de-escola-passam-mal-e-samu-destaca-crise-de-ansiedade/)). The psychiatrist Raphael Boechat, of the University of Brasília, noted that international psychiatric manuals have no diagnosis of collective anxiety and said: "Possivelmente havia fator de estresse, isso gerou ansiedade em alguns e pode ter contaminado outros" (there was possibly a stress factor, it caused anxiety in some and may have spread to others) ([Folha de Pernambuco, 12 April 2022](https://www.folhape.com.br/noticias/crise-de-ansiedade-que-atingiu-alunos-em-recife-e-cada-vez-mais-comum/223268/)). On 19 May two more Recife schools called the emergency services, one after a police chase near the school, the other for just over twenty students with crying and anxiety ([Folha de Pernambuco, 19 May 2022](https://www.folhape.com.br/noticias/duas-escolas-no-recife-registram-casos-de-crise-de-ansiedade-em-alunos/227483/)).

Fear can also follow a real hazard. After the caesium-137 accident in Goiânia in September 1987, "some 112 000 persons were monitored, of whom 249 were contaminated"; rumours spread overnight, many people went to the Olympic stadium "for reassurance, straining the limited monitoring resources", and an inquiry by the national nuclear energy commission (Comissão Nacional de Energia Nuclear, CNEN) found that "74% had spontaneously gone to be monitored for fear of radiation" ([International Atomic Energy Agency, The Radiological Accident in Goiânia, 1988](https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/publications/PDF/Pub815_web.pdf)). Bartholomew and Wessely cite a different figure, about 10,000 people or 10% of the population seeking medical examination ([Bartholomew and Wessely, 2002](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/protean-nature-of-mass-sociogenic-illness/2BDC2262E104B8A33F3DD49773DA0D8B)). Four people died and 249 were contaminated; the fear was a response to a real danger, which is what separates Goiânia from mass psychogenic illness ([International Atomic Energy Agency, 1988](https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/publications/PDF/Pub815_web.pdf)).

## Who steered, and who did not

In most of these cases nobody steered: the trigger was a smell, a fainting classmate, a video, an exam. Others shaped the course afterwards. In Afghanistan the security service presented confessions that UNAMA feared were extracted by torture, and a human rights researcher described it as the government's own psychological operations ([Al Jazeera, 12 July 2012](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2012/7/12/torture-alleged-in-afghan-poisoning-arrests)). In Iran officials accused foreign media of psychological warfare while critics accused officials of using the word hysteria to dismiss the girls ([Ulbricht and Weiner, 2025](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Final-Iran-Report-v4-.pdf)). In Colombia a later commentator blamed the media, a premature presidential statement, lawyers who promised compensation and politicians seeking votes ([Baltar-Moreno and colleagues, 2022](https://revistas.javeriana.edu.co/index.php/gerepolsal/article/download/34614/28546)); in Acre the immunization society's director said the anti-vaccine movement used the case to spread fear ([Jornal de Brasília, 2 June 2026](https://jornaldebrasilia.com.br/noticias/saude/acre-tenta-recuperar-confianca-e-ampliar-vacinacao-contra-hpv/)). The repetition that makes such claims feel true is described in [The human attack surface: why repetition works](/learn/the-human-attack-surface-why-repetition-works), and why corrections leave them standing in [Why misbeliefs persist](/learn/why-misbeliefs-persist).

What helped is recorded too: separating the group ([Wessely, 1987](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=3575566)); telling the families the diagnosis before the press ([Simas and colleagues, 2019](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6363158/fullTextXML)); taking the symptoms seriously, since statements suggesting patients were feigning "were noted to increase exacerbations" ([McVige and colleagues, 2026](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC13018255/fullTextXML)); and, in Goiânia, explaining in plain language, a 24-hour telephone line and 250,000 leaflets ([International Atomic Energy Agency, 1988](https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/publications/PDF/Pub815_web.pdf)). The evidence on each is weighed in [What ends an outbreak, and what helps](/learn/what-ends-an-outbreak-and-what-helps).

## What to remember

- Mass psychogenic illness is real, involuntary illness shared by a group, spread by sight, sound and belief; FND is the clinical category, and the old name "mass hysteria" is avoided because of its history.
- Investigators look for a combination: no organic cause, benign transient symptoms, rapid onset and recovery, a closed group, anxiety, spread by sight, spread down the age scale, more women and girls; odours and schools are common settings.
- The label can be wrong: pesticides, carbon disulphide and industrial fumes were found after outbreaks were called hysteria, which is why thorough testing comes first.
- In Tanganyika, the original report counts a few hundred cases; the round figure of a thousand people and fourteen schools comes from later retellings.
- Le Roy's count ran from 12 to 19 depending on who counted; the state and the treating clinic found no toxin or infection, and every patient recovered as coverage faded.
- After HPV vaccination, Colombia's coverage fell from 98% to 14% for the first dose by 2016; the official study found no organic link, a case-control analysis was cited against it, and the courts required informed consent.
- In Acre, ten of twelve adolescents examined had psychogenic seizures and two had genetic epilepsy; in Bertioga, health officials called it an anxiety reaction.
- In Afghanistan and Iran, officials alternated between poisoning, enemies and hysteria; no toxicology was published, confessions were disputed, and the question of what happened is still open.
