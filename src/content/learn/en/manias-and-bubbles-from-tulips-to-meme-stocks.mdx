A speculative mania is collective belief with a price attached. Someone buys because the price has been rising and will, they expect, keep rising; others see the gains and join; a story spreads that explains why this time the rise is justified; and the crowd grows until the buyers run out, the story fails or a shock arrives. The mechanisms behind that sequence (social proof, information cascades, contagion of emotion) are set out in [the part on conformity, cascades and contagion](/learn/conformity-cascades-and-contagion-the-mechanisms), and the series as a whole is introduced in [the opener on mass hysteria](/learn/mass-hysteria-how-groups-come-to-believe-anything). This part follows the financial cases. It starts with a book that shaped how all of them are told, Charles Mackay's *Extraordinary Popular Delusions* of 1841, and with the historians who have since tested its stories against the archives. It then runs from Amsterdam in 1637 to Paris and London in 1720, to Rio de Janeiro in 1890 and 1971, to New York in 1929, 2000 and 2008, to the crypto collapses of 2022 and the GameStop squeeze of January 2021. It ends with the question that won two of the economists quoted here a shared Nobel prize without settling it: can anyone tell a bubble from a boom before it ends? After reading, you can separate what the sources show from what is retold, name the cases that had a manipulator and those that had none, and weigh a claim that something "is a bubble".

## The words and the book that told the stories

### Mania, bubble, crash

Economists have no single definition of a bubble, and the disagreement is part of the subject. A widely quoted one, from Joseph Stiglitz in 1990, is given by Charles Himmelberg, Christopher Mayer and Todd Sinai as: "[I]f the reason that the price is high today is only because investors believe that the selling price is high tomorrow -- when 'fundamental' factors do not seem to justify such a price -- then a bubble exists" ([Himmelberg, Mayer and Sinai, National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) working paper 11643, September 2005](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w11643/w11643.pdf)). Robert Shiller, in his Nobel lecture, proposed a definition centred on behaviour rather than on prices: "A situation in which news of price increases spurs investor enthusiasm which spreads by psychological contagion from person to person, in the process amplifying stories that might justify the price increase and bringing in a larger and larger class of investors, who, despite doubts about the real value of the investment, are drawn to it partly through envy of others' successes and partly through a gambler's excitement" ([Shiller, Nobel lecture, 8 December 2013, published 2014](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/shiller-lecture.pdf)). He added that a bubble is "not a wild orgy of delusions but a natural consequence of the principles of social psychology coupled with imperfect news media and information channels". Eugene Fama, who shared that prize, called "bubble" "a treacherous term" ([Fama, Nobel lecture, 8 December 2013, published 2014](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/fama-lecture.pdf)). Their dispute is the last section of this part.

### Mackay, 1841 and 1852

Most popular accounts of early manias descend from one book. Charles Mackay published *Memoirs of Extraordinary Popular Delusions* in London with Richard Bentley in 1841, opening with three chapters on the Mississippi scheme, the South Sea Bubble and the "Tulipomania" ([Mackay, 1841 edition, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/636/pg636.txt)); the 1852 edition, issued by the Office of the National Illustrated Library, added *and the Madness of Crowds* to the title ([Mackay, 1852 edition, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/24518/pg24518.txt)). Its preface supplied the sentence still quoted in finance books: "Men, it has been well said, think in herds; it will be seen that they go mad in herds, while they only recover their senses slowly, and one by one" ([Mackay, 1841](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/636/pg636.txt)).

Mackay was a compiler, and said so. For the tulips he named his sources: Johann Beckmann's *History of Inventions*, "Munting, an industrious author of that day, who wrote a folio volume of one thousand pages upon the tulipomania", and "Blainville's Travels" for the story of the sailor who ate a bulb ([Mackay, 1841](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/636/pg636.txt)). For the most quoted South Sea anecdote, a "company for carrying on an undertaking of great advantage, but nobody to know what it is", he wrote that "Were not the fact stated by scores of credible witnesses, it would be impossible to believe that any person could have been duped by such a project", without naming any of the witnesses ([Mackay, 1841](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/636/pg636.txt)). The stories below are therefore told twice: as Mackay and his successors told them, and as the archives that historians and economists later read allow them to be told.

## Tulips, 1636 and 1637: the story and the archives

### What Mackay wrote

Mackay's tulip chapter describes a whole nation swept up: "Nobles, citizens, farmers, mechanics, seamen, footmen, maidservants, even chimney-sweeps and old clotheswomen, dabbled in tulips." It lists the goods (wheat, rye, oxen, swine, sheep, wine, beer, butter, cheese, a bed, clothes and a silver cup) said to have been given for a single bulb of the variety called Viceroy, valued at 2,500 florins; it tells of a sailor jailed for eating a Semper Augustus he mistook for an onion; and it ends in ruin: "Substantial merchants were reduced almost to beggary, and many a representative of a noble line saw the fortunes of his house ruined beyond redemption" ([Mackay, 1841](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/636/pg636.txt)). According to Mackay, the courts "refused to take cognizance of gambling transactions", and "the commerce of the country suffered a severe shock, from which it was many years ere it recovered".

### What the archives show

Anne Goldgar, professor of early modern history at King's College London, spent years in Dutch archives for her book *Tulipmania: Money, Honor and Knowledge in the Dutch Golden Age* (University of Chicago Press, 2007), and summarized her findings in 2018 ([Goldgar, The Conversation, 12 February 2018](https://theconversation.com/tulip-mania-the-classic-story-of-a-dutch-financial-bubble-is-mostly-wrong-91413); [University of Chicago Press blog, 29 May 2008](https://pressblog.uchicago.edu/2008/05/29/financial-speculation-in-the-dutch-golden-age.html)). <Claim status="attributed" by="by Anne Goldgar, from her archival research">The trade was small, socially narrow and mostly calm</Claim>: "I was able to identify only 37 people who spent more than 300 guilders on bulbs, around the yearly wage of a master craftsman", and "Far from bulbs being traded hundreds of times, I never found a chain of buyers longer than five". The buyers came "mainly from the merchant and skilled artisan class", and "Sellers mainly sold to people they knew". The crash of February 1637 was, in her account, mostly notional, because bulbs were in the ground and money was due only at delivery in May or June: "Anyone who had both bought and sold a tulip on paper since the summer of 1636 had lost nothing." She found no bankrupt ruined by tulips and wrote that "No one drowned themselves in canals" and "The Dutch economy was left completely unaffected" ([Goldgar, 2018](https://theconversation.com/tulip-mania-the-classic-story-of-a-dutch-financial-bubble-is-mostly-wrong-91413)).

Her explanation for the legend is a chain of copying: satirical songs and pamphlets of 1637 were picked up by later writers, then by "a late 18th-century German writer of a history of inventions" (Beckmann), whose book "was in turn plundered by Charles Mackay"; "Much of what Mackay says about tulip mania comes straight from the satirical songs of 1637" ([Goldgar, 2018](https://theconversation.com/tulip-mania-the-classic-story-of-a-dutch-financial-bubble-is-mostly-wrong-91413)). The moralizing was the point of those songs, which voiced, in her words, "the fears of 17th-century people about a 17th-century situation".

Two economists reached related conclusions from prices. Peter Garber compiled time series of bulb prices and concluded that "Most of the "tulipmania" was not obvious madness", since high and rapidly falling prices for rare new bulbs are "a typical pattern in the flower-bulb industry"; "Only the last month of the speculation, during which common-bulb prices increased rapidly and crashed, remains as a potential bubble" ([Garber, Journal of Political Economy 97(3), June 1989, abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jpolec/v97y1989i3p535-60.html)). Earl Thompson argued that the famous prices were an artefact of contracts converted after the fact from futures into options, so that "There was thus nothing maniacal about prices in this period" ([Thompson, Public Choice 130(1), January 2007, abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/pubcho/v130y2007i1p99-114.html)). <Claim status="disputed">Whether the tulip episode was a bubble at all</Claim> is therefore contested; that it was not the nationwide ruin Mackay described is the finding of the archival work cited here.

Nobody steered the tulip trade in the record cited here; what was steered, by satirists and moralists after the crash, was its story.

## John Law and the Mississippi Company, 1716 to 1720

The French episode was designed by one man with the backing of the state. François Velde of the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago describes John Law's System as "a radical restructuring of French public finances, carried out from 1716 to 1720", combining "a conversion of the existing French public debt into something like government equity" with "the replacement of commodity money with fiat money" ([Velde, Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago working paper 2003-31, 30 November 2003](https://www.chicagofed.org/-/media/publications/working-papers/2003/wp2003-31-pdf.pdf)). Law founded the General Bank in May 1716; his trading company took over tax collection, the mints, overseas trade and part of France's colonies, and offered government creditors its shares in exchange for their bonds; Law himself became minister of finance on 5 January 1720 ([Velde, 2003](https://www.chicagofed.org/-/media/publications/working-papers/2003/wp2003-31-pdf.pdf)).

The share price told the story contemporaries remembered. In 1718 a share of the Company of the West "stood around 250L"; it "peaked at 10,000L in January 1720"; after the Company's restructuring in 1722 it was back to about the same level as in 1718, adjusted for splits ([Velde, 2003](https://www.chicagofed.org/-/media/publications/working-papers/2003/wp2003-31-pdf.pdf)). Law supported the price by buying shares with newly printed notes: on 5 March 1720 he opened an office buying and selling shares at a fixed 9,000 livres, and from March to late May the company spent another 1,213.5 million livres buying 27% of its own stock. On 21 May 1720 an arrêt drafted by Law devalued both shares and notes in stages; its preamble blamed "ill-disposed individuals" for attempts to undermine the System; "within days public outrage against the measures was growing", and on 27 May the devaluation was rescinded. Law left France in early December 1720 ([Velde, 2003](https://www.chicagofed.org/-/media/publications/working-papers/2003/wp2003-31-pdf.pdf)).

Was it a bubble? Velde's estimate is precise and divided: <Claim status="attributed" by="by François Velde">"the share prices were overvalued at their peak by a factor of 2 to 5, but I attribute this to Law's systematic policy of price support"</Claim>, and the public debt, restored by Law's successors "at roughly its earlier level", means the System "was far from a default" ([Velde, 2003](https://www.chicagofed.org/-/media/publications/working-papers/2003/wp2003-31-pdf.pdf)). Mackay's version adds colour that cannot be checked from the sources he gives, among them a hunchbacked man in the rue Quincampoix who earned money "by lending his hump as a writing-desk"; Mackay introduces it with "The story goes" ([Mackay, 1841](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/636/pg636.txt)). Here there was a steersman: the price was the policy of the state's finance minister, and when it failed the official text blamed unnamed enemies.

## The South Sea Bubble, 1720

### The scheme

The South Sea Company was founded in 1711 to absorb unpaid government bills; creditors received its stock and the company passed on the government's interest payments, while holding a monopoly of British trade with Spanish America that included the slave trade ([Odlyzko, Physics Today, 1 July 2020](https://physicstoday.aip.org/features/isaac-newton-and-the-perils-of-the-financial-south-sea)). In 1720 Parliament accepted its plan to take over most of the national debt; the South Sea bill passed on 7 April; the price soared through the summer and collapsed in September, and "By October, the stock was worth less than a quarter of its peak price". The company was not destroyed: it survived to the middle of the nineteenth century as an agent paying out government funds. Andrew Odlyzko, who reconstructed the period from archival records, describes the company's 1720 operations as <Claim status="attributed" by="by Andrew Odlyzko">"a Ponzi scheme that rewarded early investors with money from new investors"</Claim>, and an anonymous article in the *Flying-Post* of 9 April 1720, apparently arranged by the company's managers, as a promise of near-infinite returns that he calls "mathiness" ([Odlyzko, 2020](https://physicstoday.aip.org/features/isaac-newton-and-the-perils-of-the-financial-south-sea)). A London banker who had long doubted the scheme wrote in June 1720 that "when the rest of the world is mad we must imitate them in some measure" (quoted by [Odlyzko, 2020](https://physicstoday.aip.org/features/isaac-newton-and-the-perils-of-the-financial-south-sea)).

### Not only the South Sea

Rik Frehen, William Goetzmann and Geert Rouwenhorst collected share prices for British and Dutch companies from the *Leydse Courant* and *The Course of the Exchange* and found that 1720 was a linked event in Paris, London and the Netherlands, with "evidence against indiscriminate irrational exuberance and evidence in favor of speculation about two factors: the Atlantic trade and the incorporation of insurance companies" ([Frehen, Goetzmann and Rouwenhorst, NBER working paper 15332, November 2009](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w15332/w15332.pdf)). The two marine insurers, Royal Exchange Assurance and London Assurance, rose to higher multiples than the South Sea Company; the Bubble Act of 9 June 1720 chartered exactly those two insurers and "explicitly limited the chartering of competitors". Their conclusion fits Shiller's definition: "financial bubbles require a plausible story to justify investor optimism".

### The inquiry

When the price fell, the House of Commons appointed a Committee of Secrecy of thirteen members chosen by ballot, in January 1721 (1720 in the Old Style calendar then in use), to inquire into the execution of the South Sea Act ([The Several Reports of the Committee of Secrecy, London, 1721](https://archive.org/download/bim_eighteenth-century_the-several-reports-of-t_great-britain-parliamen_1721/bim_eighteenth-century_the-several-reports-of-t_great-britain-parliamen_1721_djvu.txt)). Its first report found that "in some of the Books produced before them, false and fictitious Entries were made", others with blanks, erasures and torn-out leaves, and described what it called "a Scene of Iniquity and Corruption": stock of £574,500, booked as sold for £1,259,325 between 4 February and 12 April 1720 with no buyer named, which witnesses linked to ministers and their friends (spelling modernized from the long s of the 1721 printing; [Committee of Secrecy, 1721](https://archive.org/download/bim_eighteenth-century_the-several-reports-of-t_great-britain-parliamen_1721/bim_eighteenth-century_the-several-reports-of-t_great-britain-parliamen_1721.pdf)). Frehen and colleagues write that John Aislabie, Chancellor of the Exchequer, "took a bribe of 20,000 pounds of South Sea stock in return for his political support of the firm" ([Frehen, Goetzmann and Rouwenhorst, 2009](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w15332/w15332.pdf)). Here, too, there were steersmen, and Parliament named them.

### Newton and the "madness of the people"

The best-known South Sea story is about Isaac Newton, then Master of the Mint. The saying attributed to him, that he could calculate the motions of the heavenly bodies but not the madness of people, has a thin paper trail. Its earliest printed source is a note in Joseph Spence's "Second Memorandum Book, 1756", published in 1820, which reports, from Lord Radnor, that Newton, asked "about the continuance of the rising of South Sea stock", answered "that he could not calculate the madness of the people" ([Spence, Anecdotes, Observations, and Characters, of Books and Men, ed. Singer, London, 1820, p. 368](https://archive.org/download/b29302547/b29302547_djvu.txt)). <Claim status="reported" by="by Joseph Spence, from Lord Radnor, 36 years after the event">The words are second-hand, written down decades later, and refer to a question asked while the price was still rising</Claim>, not to a lament after the crash; the "heavenly bodies" half appears in later variants ([WIST quotations, 14 January 2021](https://wist.info/newton-isaac/45097/)).

Newton's losses, by contrast, are documented. Odlyzko worked from newly found records, many in the Bank of England's archives; his analysis "strongly suggests" that Newton sold his whole South Sea stake on 19 and 23 April 1720, bracketing a shareholders' meeting on 21 April, and that in mid-June he moved "essentially all his financial assets" back into the stock near the top ([Odlyzko, 2020](https://physicstoday.aip.org/features/isaac-newton-and-the-perils-of-the-financial-south-sea)). "Newton's net worth shortly before the South Sea Bubble started was just over £30 000"; "By mid 1721, his net worth was down to about £20 000". In a letter of 1724 Newton wrote: "I lost very much by the South Sea company which makes my pockets empty, & my mind averse from dealing in these matters." Odlyzko's verdict: "Unlike many other anecdotes about famous figures, the colorful story of Newton and the South Sea Bubble was largely correct. In some ways it even understated the extent of his mistakes" ([Odlyzko, 2020](https://physicstoday.aip.org/features/isaac-newton-and-the-perils-of-the-financial-south-sea)). He also warns that a letter placing "a Mr. Newton" in Exchange Alley probably refers to another Newton. The longer study behind that article, in *Notes and Records of the Royal Society* (2019), could not be read for this part.

## Brazil's Encilhamento, 1889 to 1891

### Credit after abolition and the Republic

Brazil's first great bubble followed two upheavals: the abolition of slavery in 1888 and the proclamation of the Republic on 15 November 1889. On 17 January 1890 the Provisional Government issued two decrees together: number 164 on joint-stock companies and number 165, which allowed banks founded with government authorization to issue notes backed by public bonds, dividing the country into three regions with banks capitalized at up to 150,000, 200,000 and 100,000 contos de réis ([Decreto nº 165, de 17 de janeiro de 1890, Senado Federal](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/386185/publicacao/15722552); [Revista Eletrônica da OAB-RJ, Os impactos da crise do Encilhamento](https://revistaeletronicaoabrj.emnuvens.com.br/revista/article/download/512/498/943)). The finance minister was Rui Barbosa. Money issued, in the original intention, to give liquidity to planters who now had to pay wages flowed, in the words of the OAB-RJ study, progressively "aos negócios na Praça do Comércio", into trading on the exchange ([OAB-RJ](https://revistaeletronicaoabrj.emnuvens.com.br/revista/article/download/512/498/943)).

### The frenzy, in numbers and in words

The historian Ney Carvalho counts "mais de 400 companhias abertas", more than 400 listed companies founded in the capital in the three years of the "delírio", and dates the foundation of the Bolsa Livre de São Paulo, ancestor of today's exchange, to August 1890 ([Ney Carvalho, Capital Aberto, 1 February 2011](https://capitalaberto.com.br/empresas/2011/02/furando-a-bolha-do-encilhamento)). The chronicler Noronha Santos gives the capital mobilized by joint-stock companies founded in Rio from November 1889 to October 1890 as "10.169.380:000$000", and passes on Pandiá Calógeras's line that "O delírio [...] tinha chegado ao ponto de já não se achar nome para dar às novas empresas": the delirium had reached the point where no names could be found for the new companies ([Noronha Santos, O Encilhamento, reprinted in Notícia Bibliográfica e Histórica, PUC-Campinas](https://periodicos.puc-campinas.edu.br/noticiabibliohist/article/download/18149/14154/67923)). The name itself, Carvalho explains, came from the racecourse, where horses were saddled (*encilhados*) before a race, and alluded to the pavement market where unwary investors were themselves "encilhados como qualquer cavalgadura", saddled like any mount ([Carvalho, 2011](https://capitalaberto.com.br/empresas/2011/02/furando-a-bolha-do-encilhamento)).

The minister responsible saw the problem from inside. In his report to the President, presented in January 1891, Rui Barbosa described "emprezas destituidas de vitalidade real, incapazes de vingar, engenhadas unicamente para utilizar, em detrimento dos inexpertos, a beneficio dos sagazes" the abundant credit of the period (companies without real vitality, unable to thrive, devised solely to use, to the detriment of the inexperienced and the benefit of the shrewd, the abundant credit), and a "superfetação de especulações estereis, damninhas e perfidas", a superabundance of sterile, harmful and perfidious speculations ([Relatório do Ministro da Fazenda Ruy Barbosa, January 1891, p. 53](https://archive.org/download/rmfazenda18891890/rmfazenda18891890.pdf)). His remedy, a decree of 13 October 1890, required a company's capital to be fully subscribed and 30% deposited in a bank before it counted as constituted, and barred trading in its shares before 40% had been paid in ([Relatório, p. 53](https://archive.org/download/rmfazenda18891890/rmfazenda18891890_djvu.txt)).

### The crash and its explanations

Rui Barbosa left the ministry in January 1891. His successor, Tristão de Alencar Araripe, issued on Saturday 14 February 1891 a decree taxing forward operations at 3% of the nominal value of each share; brokers at the Rio exchange went on strike for a week, prices collapsed on the pavement market, the measure was revoked, and "os preços continuaram a cair, dando fim ao Encilhamento" ([Carvalho, 2011](https://capitalaberto.com.br/empresas/2011/02/furando-a-bolha-do-encilhamento)). The OAB-RJ study identifies the decree as number 1,362 of 1891 and adds the political turmoil of the year's end, with Deodoro da Fonseca's resignation and Floriano Peixoto's rise ([OAB-RJ](https://revistaeletronicaoabrj.emnuvens.com.br/revista/article/download/512/498/943)).

Who was to blame was disputed from the start. Marcus Croce's study of the bankruptcy of the Banco Territorial e Mercantil de Minas, from its court file, summarizes the camps: the Visconde de Taunay's novel *O Encilhamento* (1893) "ataca as medidas do governo republicano, representado pelo Ministro da Fazenda Rui Barbosa", Antônio Carlos Ribeiro de Andrada blamed the monarchy, and the historians Maria Bárbara Levy and Stanley Stein pointed to "a especulação e oportunismo de agentes financeiros" ([Croce, Revista de História Econômica & Economia Regional Aplicada 6(11), 2011](https://periodicos.ufjf.br/index.php/heera/article/view/26334/18146)). Taunay's novel first appeared as a serial in the *Gazeta de Noticias* on 26 February 1893 under the pen name Heitor Malheiros, and was published as a book in 1894 with the subtitle "scenas contemporaneas da Bolsa em 1890, 1891 e 1892" ([Heitor Malheiros, O Encilhamento, Rio de Janeiro, 1894](https://archive.org/download/oencilhamentosce01escruoft/oencilhamentosce01escruoft_djvu.txt)). Carvalho's own reading is that <Claim status="attributed" by="by Ney Carvalho">"A bolha teria sido perfurada naturalmente, como todas as demais", but the minister's clumsy decree made the government responsible for the cataclysm</Claim> ([Carvalho, 2011](https://capitalaberto.com.br/empresas/2011/02/furando-a-bolha-do-encilhamento)).

Not everything vanished. The OAB-RJ study lists the Companhia Antártica Paulista (1891), the Bolsa Livre (1890) and Melhoramentos (1890) among the survivors, and cites Gustavo Franco's count that in 1905, 30.2% of the textile companies then existing had been founded between 1889 and 1895 ([OAB-RJ](https://revistaeletronicaoabrj.emnuvens.com.br/revista/article/download/512/498/943)). The law changed for decades: Decree 434 of 1891 on joint-stock companies lasted until 1940, Decree 354 of 1895 on the securities market until Law 4,728 of 1965, and the bankruptcy decree of 1890 until 1902 ([OAB-RJ](https://revistaeletronicaoabrj.emnuvens.com.br/revista/article/download/512/498/943)). The Encilhamento had steersmen of two kinds: a state that opened the credit taps and promoters who, in its own minister's words, built companies to exploit the inexperienced.

## Brazil again: the boom and crash of 1971

Eighty years later a second Brazilian mania was encouraged by the state itself. Under the military government, Decree-Law 157 of 1967 let taxpayers put part of their income tax into share funds, the "Fundo 157", which banks advertised with television comedians; the Rio exchange's trading volume rose 484% in 1969, and in June 1971 the magazine *Manchete* ran the headline "A Bolsa está na moda", the exchange is in fashion, according to a 2026 report by Agência Pública based on the newspapers of the period ([Sérgio Barbo, Agência Pública, 1 April 2026](https://apublica.org/2026/04/como-a-ditadura-ajudou-a-impulsionar-o-mercado-financeiro-no-brasil/)). The report describes the media tone as "explicitamente propagandístico" and part of the developmental policy of the "milagre econômico". <Claim status="reported" by="by Agência Pública">The Rio exchange index fell 54.79% in July 1971</Claim> after a Banco do Brasil share bonus announced by the finance minister, Delfim Netto, disappointed expectations, and small investors left the market for years; the Banco Central and the Bovespa did not answer the reporter's questions ([Agência Pública, 2026](https://apublica.org/2026/04/como-a-ditadura-ajudou-a-impulsionar-o-mercado-financeiro-no-brasil/)). Roberto Teixeira da Costa, later the first president of the Comissão de Valores Mobiliários (CVM), Brazil's securities regulator, recalled that "Como não havia uma base institucional, a crise foi profunda no momento em que o mercado deixou de ser comprador para ser vendedor": with no institutional base, the crisis was deep once the market turned from buyer to seller ([Capital Aberto, July 2006](https://capitalaberto.com.br/regulacao/2006/07/um-pais-sem-limites)). The response was Law 6,385 of 1976, which created the CVM, and the Corporations Law, 6,404 of the same year ([Agência Pública, 2026](https://apublica.org/2026/04/como-a-ditadura-ajudou-a-impulsionar-o-mercado-financeiro-no-brasil/); [CVM, institutional page](https://www.gov.br/cvm/pt-br/acesso-a-informacao-cvm/institucional/sobre-a-cvm)).

## 1929: the plateau that was not

The Federal Reserve's own history of the crash gives the arc: the Dow Jones Industrial Average rose about six-fold, from 63 in August 1921 to 381.17 on 3 September 1929; ordinary investors bought on margin, typically putting down 10% and borrowing the rest; on 28 October the Dow fell nearly 13% and on 29 October nearly 12% more; on 8 July 1932 it closed at 41.22, 89% below its peak, and it did not regain its 1929 high until November 1954 ([Federal Reserve History, Stock Market Crash of 1929](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/stock-market-crash-of-1929)). The economist Irving Fisher had said after the peak, at a dinner reported by *The New York Times* on 16 October 1929, that "stock prices have reached 'what looks like a permanently high plateau'" ([Federal Reserve History](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/stock-market-crash-of-1929)).

The Senate's inquiry, run from 1933 by its chief counsel Ferdinand Pecora, found much to reproach in the bankers who had sold the boom. National City Bank was chosen first, the committee's former chairman Peter Norbeck explained, because of "its recognized leadership in the orgy of speculation which led to the business collapse"; its chairman, Charles Mitchell, admitted selling stock to a relative at a loss to avoid income tax and resigned soon after; an investor testified that brokers had shamed him into holding a collapsing portfolio ([United States Senate, The Pecora Investigation](https://www.senate.gov/about/powers-procedures/investigations/pecora.htm)).

Whether 1929 was a bubble is still argued. Ellen McGrattan and Edward Prescott estimated the fundamental value of corporate equity from growth theory and concluded that <Claim status="disputed">"Even at the 1929 peak, stocks were undervalued relative to the prediction of theory"</Claim> ([McGrattan and Prescott, Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis staff report 294, December 2003](https://www.minneapolisfed.org/research/sr/sr294.pdf)). The Federal Reserve's own lesson is about policy rather than diagnosis: "Detecting and deflating financial bubbles is difficult", and its 1929 attempt to restrain speculation through "direct pressure" and a 6% discount rate had "unintended consequences" abroad ([Federal Reserve History](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/stock-market-crash-of-1929)). Ben Bernanke called the Board members of 1928 and 1929 "a coterie of aggressive bubble-poppers" ([Bernanke, 15 October 2002](https://www.federalreserve.gov/boarddocs/speeches/2002/20021015/default.htm)).

## Irrational exuberance and the dot-com years

On 5 December 1996, at the American Enterprise Institute, the chairman of the Federal Reserve, Alan Greenspan, asked a question that became a label: "But how do we know when irrational exuberance has unduly escalated asset values, which then become subject to unexpected and prolonged contractions as they have in Japan over the past decade?" ([Greenspan, 5 December 1996](https://www.federalreserve.gov/boarddocs/speeches/1996/19961205.htm)). Shiller later used the phrase as the title of his book, and Fama recounts that, according to Shiller's own website, Shiller had warned Greenspan two days earlier that prices were irrationally high ([Fama, 2013](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/fama-lecture.pdf)).

The Nasdaq Composite closed at its all-time high of 5,048.62 on 10 March 2000; the same index stood at 1,114 in August 1996 and again in October 2002 ([Pástor and Veronesi, NBER working paper 10581, 2004](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w10581/w10581.pdf)). Two readings of the same numbers compete. Eli Ofek and Matthew Richardson explained internet stock prices from January 1998 to November 2000 by optimistic investors who could not be offset because short selling was constrained, and tied "the eventual fall, or bubble bursting, of internet stocks" to "the increase in the number of sellers to the market via expiration of lockup agreements" ([Ofek and Richardson, NBER working paper 8630, December 2001](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w8630/w8630.pdf)). Ľuboš Pástor and Pietro Veronesi answered the question in their title, "Was there a Nasdaq bubble in the late 1990s?", with <Claim status="disputed">"Not necessarily"</Claim>: in their model the value of a firm rises with uncertainty about its future profitability, and "the uncertainty needed to match the observed Nasdaq valuations at their peak is high but plausible" ([Pástor and Veronesi, 2004](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w10581/w10581.pdf)). Bernanke, speaking in 2002, said only that the decline since March 2000 "has not only vaporized trillions of dollars in wealth, but also likely played a role in worsening the recession" ([Bernanke, 2002](https://www.federalreserve.gov/boarddocs/speeches/2002/20021015/default.htm)).

## 2008: the housing bubble and three explanations

The Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission (FCIC), set up by Congress, reported in January 2011. Its majority wrote that it was the collapse of the housing bubble that "was the spark that ignited a string of events" leading to the crisis of the autumn of 2008, and concluded: "We conclude this financial crisis was avoidable"; "Theirs was a big miss, not a stumble" ([FCIC, Final Report, January 2011](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-FCIC/pdf/GPO-FCIC.pdf)). Three members, Keith Hennessey, Douglas Holtz-Eakin and Bill Thomas, dissented with "ten causes that are essential to explaining the crisis", starting with a global credit bubble and a "large and sustained housing bubble in the United States" ([FCIC, dissenting statement](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-FCIC/pdf/GPO-FCIC.pdf)). Peter Wallison dissented separately: <Claim status="attributed" by="by Peter Wallison, FCIC member">"I believe that the sine qua non of the financial crisis was U.S. government housing policy"</Claim>, which in his account produced 27 million subprime and other risky loans ([FCIC, dissent of Peter J. Wallison](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-FCIC/pdf/GPO-FCIC.pdf)). The Federal Reserve dates the resulting recession from December 2007 to June 2009 ([Federal Reserve History, The Great Recession](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/great-recession-of-200709)).

The bubble was not obvious to everyone at the time. In 2005, Himmelberg, Mayer and Sinai concluded that "As of the end of 2004, our analysis reveals little evidence of a housing bubble" ([Himmelberg, Mayer and Sinai, 2005](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w11643/w11643.pdf)). Fama later took July 2003 as the date of the first published warning, by Karl Case and Shiller, and noted that the S&P/Case-Shiller 20-City index stood at 142.99 then, peaked at 206.52 in July 2006 and fell to 134.07 in March 2012, only 6.7% below the warning level ([Fama, 2013](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/fama-lecture.pdf)).

## Crypto and non-fungible tokens

### The promise and the price

Bitcoin began with a nine-page paper dated 2008 that proposed "A purely peer-to-peer version of electronic cash" allowing payments "without going through a financial institution" ([Satoshi Nakamoto, Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System, 2008](https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf)). In 2018 the Bank for International Settlements (BIS), the central banks' bank, concluded that in a decentralized system "Trust can evaporate at any time because of the fragility of the decentralised consensus through which transactions are recorded", and noted that many coin offerings had "turned out to be fraudulent Ponzi schemes" ([BIS, Annual Economic Report 2018, chapter V, Cryptocurrencies: looking beyond the hype](https://www.bis.org/publications/cryptocurrencies-looking-beyond-hype.pdf)).

The BIS later measured who gained and who lost. Using app downloads on crypto trading platforms from August 2015 to December 2022, its economists found that "almost threequarters of the users downloaded a crypto platform app when the price of bitcoin was above $20,000", that "In nearly all economies in our sample, a majority of investors probably lost money on their bitcoin investment", and that "The median investor would have lost $431 by December 2022, corresponding to almost half of their total $900 in funds invested"; the share of losers was "even higher in several emerging market economies like Brazil, India, Pakistan, Thailand and Turkey", and larger investors "tended to sell their coins right before steep price declines, while smaller investors were still buying" ([Cornelli, Doerr, Frost and Gambacorta, BIS Bulletin 69, 20 February 2023](https://www.bis.org/publications/bulletin-69-crypto-shocks-and-retail-losses.pdf)).

On 11 March 2021, at the height of the market for non-fungible tokens (NFTs), Christie's sold a purely digital collage by the artist Beeple, *Everydays: The First 5000 Days*, for US$69,346,250 including the buyer's premium; bidding had opened at $100, and "of the 33 active bidders, 91 percent were new to Christie's", the auction house said ([Christie's, sale 20447 results](https://onlineonly.christies.com.cn/s/beeple-first-5000-days/overview/2020?sc_lang=en); [Al Jazeera, 11 March 2021](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2021/3/11/power-to-the-beeple-digital-art-fetches-69-3m-at-auction)).

### Where courts found fraud

Two of the 2022 collapses ended in criminal judgments. The TerraUSD and LUNA tokens crashed in May 2022 with "over $40 billion worth in investor losses"; their creator, Do Kwon, <Claim status="established" by="by his guilty plea and sentence in a US federal court">pleaded guilty in August 2025 to wire fraud and conspiracy and was sentenced on 11 December 2025 to 15 years in prison</Claim>; prosecutors alleged that a 2021 loss of the dollar peg, which he publicly said the protocol had repaired, was in fact restored only after a trading firm bought millions of dollars of the token ([United States Attorney's Office, Southern District of New York, 11 December 2025](https://www.justice.gov/usao-sdny/pr/crypto-enabled-fraudster-sentenced-orchestrating-40-billion-fraud)). Samuel Bankman-Fried, founder of the FTX exchange, <Claim status="established" by="by a jury verdict and sentence in a US federal court">was convicted of wire fraud and conspiracy after a one-month trial and sentenced on 28 March 2024 to 25 years</Claim>, with "over $11 billion in forfeiture"; the United States Attorney said he had orchestrated one of the largest financial frauds in history, "stealing over $8 billion of his customers' money" ([United States Attorney's Office, Southern District of New York, 28 March 2024](https://www.justice.gov/usao-sdny/pr/samuel-bankman-fried-sentenced-25-years-prison)).

In Brazil, the regulator moved against a scheme that used bitcoin as its story. In August 2023 the CVM fined G.A.S. Consultoria e Tecnologia, its founder Glaidson Acácio dos Santos and a partner R$34 million each, R$102 million in all, for an unregistered public offer of securities and barred them for eight and a half years; the CVM's report counted at least 8,976 investors, and the business "prometia retorno médio de 10% ao mês por 12 meses", promised an average return of 10% a month ([Suno, 30 August 2023](https://www.suno.com.br/noticias/cvm-multa-farao-dos-bitcoins-fraude-gpj/amp/)). He was arrested in August 2021 and charged by the federal prosecution service; <Claim status="denied" by="by Glaidson Acácio dos Santos">he denied that his company was a front for such a scheme and said that "A empresa G.A.S. não deixou de pagar os seus clientes. Ela foi violada pela PF e paralisou as suas operações"</Claim>, blaming the Federal Police for the halt in payments ([Suno, 2023](https://www.suno.com.br/noticias/cvm-multa-farao-dos-bitcoins-fraude-gpj/amp/)). Schemes of this kind, and the economics that make them pay, are covered in [the part on scams at scale](/learn/scams-at-scale-why-a-tiny-success-rate-pays) and, for fake crypto investments sold through online relationships, in [the part on scam compounds and "pig butchering"](/learn/scam-compounds-and-pig-butchering).

## GameStop, January 2021: a squeeze, a crowd, a hearing

### What happened in the market

The meme-stock episode is the best-documented crowd event in market history. The staff of the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) reported on 14 October 2021 that on 22 January 2021 the price of GameStop (GME) "rose from $43 to $72 (a 71% increase) in approximately three hours", that 197.2 million shares traded that day, that "By January 27, GME closed at a high of $347.51 per share", and that the next day the shares "jumped further to an intraday high of $483.00" ([SEC staff, Staff Report on Equity and Options Market Structure Conditions in Early 2021, 14 October 2021](https://www.sec.gov/files/staff-report-equity-options-market-struction-conditions-early-2021.pdf)). Online forums, "including YouTube channels and the subreddit WallStreetBets", carried posts that "heavily touted the prospects for GME".

The popular account was a short squeeze, with short sellers forced to buy. The SEC staff found that covering "likely contributed to increases in GME's price" at certain times, but that "such buying was a small fraction of overall buy volume", and concluded that <Claim status="established" by="by the SEC staff's analysis of trading data">"it was the positive sentiment, not the buying-to-cover, that sustained the weeks-long price appreciation of GameStop stock"</Claim>; "a short squeeze did not appear to be the main driver of events" ([SEC staff, 2021](https://www.sec.gov/files/staff-report-equity-options-market-struction-conditions-early-2021.pdf)). It left the deeper causes open: "The underlying causes of the meme stock phenomenon that are unrelated to market structure are a subject of speculation that is beyond the scope of this report."

On 28 January the clearinghouse, the National Securities Clearing Corporation (NSCC), "made intraday margin calls from 36 clearing members totaling $6.9 billion", bringing the total required to $25.5 billion, and several brokers restricted purchases; the restrictions were, in the staff's words, "a decision made by the broker-dealers and not directed by NSCC" ([SEC staff, 2021](https://www.sec.gov/files/staff-report-equity-options-market-struction-conditions-early-2021.pdf)). The staff named four areas for further study: the forces that may lead a broker to restrict trading, including the settlement cycle; digital engagement practices and payment for order flow (PFOF), which "can create a conflict of interest for the retail broker-dealer"; trading in dark pools and through wholesalers, which "face fewer requirements concerning their operational transparency and resiliency as compared to exchanges or ATSs"; and short-sale reporting. How wholesalers and high-speed market makers operate, and the dispute over their effect, is in [the part on high-frequency trading](/learn/high-frequency-trading-what-it-is-and-the-dispute); the European Union's later ban on payment for order flow is in [the part on the rules that rewired markets](/learn/the-rules-that-rewired-markets-reg-ats-reg-nms-mifid-and-brazil).

### What the participants said

At a House Financial Services Committee hearing on 18 February 2021, the investor Keith Gill, whose posts as "Roaring Kitty" had been followed on the forum, testified: "I am not a cat. I am not an institutional investor. Nor am I a hedge fund", and "The idea that I used social media to promote GameStop stock to unwitting investors and influence the market is preposterous. My posts did not cause the movement of billions of dollars into GameStop shares"; he ended "As for me, I like the stock" ([House Committee on Financial Services, hearing of 18 February 2021, Serial No. 117-3](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-117hhrg43966/html/CHRG-117hhrg43966.htm)). Robinhood's chief executive officer (CEO), Vladimir Tenev, said that on 28 January "our daily deposit requirement was 10 times more than on January 25th", that the restrictions were imposed "to meet increased regulatory deposit requirements, not to help hedge funds", and apologized to customers. Gabriel Plotkin of Melvin Capital, a fund that had bet against GameStop, said "Melvin Capital played absolutely no role in those trading platform" decisions and that none of its short positions were "part of any effort to artificially depress or manipulate downward the price of a stock" ([hearing of 18 February 2021](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-117hhrg43966/html/CHRG-117hhrg43966.htm)).

The committee's majority staff reported in June 2022, in a report the committee did not formally adopt, that "Robinhood exhibited troubling business practices, inadequate risk management, and a culture that prioritized growth above stability", and that the Depository Trust & Clearing Corporation (DTCC) "waived $9.7 billion of collateral deposit requirements on January 28, 2021" ([House Financial Services Committee majority staff, Game Stopped, June 2022](https://democrats-financialservices.house.gov/uploadedfiles/6.22_hfsc_gs.report_hmsmeetbp.irm.nlrf.pdf)). The Republican staff answered that the findings "tend to be at odds with documents and testimony obtained by the Committee that show the core infrastructure of the securities market remained resilient", and that "there is no evidence of any collusion between market makers and broker-dealers, as the Chairwoman's own report makes clear" ([House Financial Services Committee Republican staff, memorandum, 24 June 2022](https://financialservices.house.gov/uploadedfiles/memorandum_for_fsc-rs_re_meme_stock_event_final.pdf)). On the record cited here, nobody was found to have steered the crowd; the dispute is about the plumbing it exposed.

## Can a bubble be recognized before it bursts?

### The prize that split the question

In 2013 the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences gave the economics prize to Eugene Fama, Lars Peter Hansen and Robert Shiller, observing that "There is no way to predict the price of stocks and bonds over the next few days or weeks. But it is quite possible to foresee the broad course of these prices over longer periods, such as the next three to five years" ([Nobel Prize, press release, 14 October 2013](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2013/press-release/)). The press release set the two interpretations side by side: rational investors demanding compensation in "unusually risky times", or behavioural finance, in which frictions such as borrowing limits "prevent smart investors from trading against any mispricing".

Fama's case in his lecture is that "the available research provides no reliable evidence that stock market price declines are ever predictable", so that "confident statements about "bubbles" and what should be done about them are based on beliefs, not reliable evidence" ([Fama, 2013](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/fama-lecture.pdf)). He notes that the five largest US price declines since 1925 coincided with recessions, asks "which leg of a "bubble" is irrational, the up or the down?", and tests the two famous warnings: the stock index stood at 1518 when Shiller warned Greenspan on 3 December 1996, more than doubled to 3191 by September 2000, and at its low in March 2003, at 1739, was still about 15% above the warning level. Shiller's answer, in his own lecture, is that the split between efficient-market and behavioural economists is partly illusory, "from the problem that there is not a widely accepted definition of the term "bubble"", and that a sudden, irrevocable burst is "not essential" to the idea ([Shiller, 2013](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/shiller-lecture.pdf)).

### Tests since

Robin Greenwood, Andrei Shleifer and Yang You put Fama's claim to a direct test with US industry returns from 1926 to 2014 and international sectors from 1985 to 2014. They found that "Fama is correct in that a sharp price increase of an industry portfolio does not, on average, predict unusually low returns going forward", but that "such sharp price increases predict a substantially heightened probability of a crash", and that volatility, turnover, share issuance and the shape of the run-up help forecast which run-ups end badly ([Greenwood, Shleifer and You, NBER working paper 23191, February 2017](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w23191/w23191.pdf)). Defining a crash as a 40% drawdown, they report that going from a 50% to a 100% two-year industry return net of the market raises the probability of a crash from 20% to 53%.

## What to remember

- Mackay's *Extraordinary Popular Delusions* (1841, retitled in 1852) is a compilation; its tulip chapter comes largely from satirical literature of 1637, and archival research found a small trade, no traced ruin and an unaffected economy.
- John Law's System (1716 to 1720) was a state design: shares rose about 40-fold and fell back, with overvaluation at the peak estimated at 2 to 5 times and attributed to price support.
- The South Sea Bubble of 1720 involved documented corruption; the Newton saying rests on a single second-hand note printed in 1820, while his losses are documented and real.
- Brazil's Encilhamento (1889 to 1891) followed easy note issue after abolition and the Republic; its own minister denounced companies built to exploit the inexperienced, and its laws lasted for decades. The 1971 boom, promoted with tax incentives, led to the CVM in 1976.
- For 1929, the dot-com years and 2008, economists still dispute whether prices were bubbles; inquiries found sales abuses in 1929 and an avoidable crisis in 2008, with dissents.
- Crypto's collapses ended in fraud convictions in the cases cited; the BIS estimates that most retail buyers probably lost money, with Brazil among the worst affected.
- In the GameStop episode the SEC staff found sentiment, not short covering, drove the rise, and no manipulation was established.
- Whether bubbles can be identified in advance remains open: Fama finds no reliable predictability of declines; later tests find higher crash risk after sharp run-ups but not lower average returns.
