On 30 September 1937 Brazil's government read on its official radio programme the supposed instructions of the Communist International for an uprising, a document known as the Plano Cohen; Congress approved a state of war, decreed two days later, and on 10 November the Estado Novo dictatorship began; in 1945 the general who had headed the army general staff in 1937 said, as the dictionary reports his interview, that the "documento evidentemente apócrifo" (evidently apocryphal document) had been brought to the staff by an integralist officer, and its author later said he had written it as a "hipótese de trabalho", a working hypothesis ([Fundação Getulio Vargas, Dicionário Histórico-Biográfico Brasileiro, Plano Cohen](https://www18.fgv.br/CPDOC/acervo/dicionarios/verbete-tematico/plano-cohen)). On 4 December 2016 a man drove three loaded guns from North Carolina to a pizzeria in Washington and fired a rifle into a locked door, motivated in part, prosecutors said, by "unfounded rumors concerning a child sex-trafficking ring" there ([United States Attorney's Office for the District of Columbia, 24 March 2017](https://justice.gov/usao-dc/pr/north-carolina-man-pleads-guilty-charges-armed-assault-northwest-washington-pizza)). On 31 March 2023 a Delaware judge wrote that the evidence made it "CRYSTAL clear that none of the Statements relating to Dominion about the 2020 election are true" ([Delaware Superior Court, US Dominion v. Fox News Network, 31 March 2023](https://courts.delaware.gov/Opinions/Download.aspx?id=345820)). And on 17 August 2006 a federal judge found that the largest tobacco companies had "conspired together" and that "over the course of more than 50 years, Defendants lied, misrepresented, and deceived the American public" about smoking ([United States District Court for the District of Columbia, United States v. Philip Morris USA, 17 August 2006](https://www.publichealthlawcenter.org/sites/default/files/resources/doj-final-opinion.pdf)).

Three of those were invented conspiracies; the last was real. This part of the series opened by [the map of mass hysteria](/learn/mass-hysteria-how-groups-come-to-believe-anything) asks how conspiracy theories spread to millions, what psychologists and political scientists have found about who believes them and how they measure it, how the most destructive forgery of the twentieth century was exposed, how Brazilian and foreign courts weighed conspiracy claims, and why the line between a proven conspiracy and an invented one runs through evidence rather than through how plausible a story feels. After reading, you can say what the research does and does not show, place each case on its court record, and judge a new claim by the questions that separated the real cases from the false ones.

No person is described here as guilty of anything a court did not find, and every defendant's or accused party's answer is given where the record holds one. English quotations are given as published; Portuguese-language sources are quoted verbatim with an English gloss.

## What a conspiracy theory is, and what it is not

### Definitions

Karen Douglas, Robbie Sutton and Aleksandra Cichocka, of the University of Kent, define conspiracy theories as "explanations for important events that involve secret plots by powerful and malevolent groups" ([Douglas, Sutton and Cichocka, Current Directions in Psychological Science, December 2017, full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC5724570/fullTextXML)). Joseph Uscinski and colleagues, of the University of Miami, add a test of evidence: a conspiracy theory cites "a small group of powerful people working in secret, for their own benefit, against the common good", and such theories "have not been judged as likely accurate by the appropriate epistemological bodies using publicly available evidence" ([Uscinski et al., PLOS ONE, 20 July 2022, full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9299316/fullTextXML)).

The Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) drew the same line in an intelligence bulletin of its Phoenix field office: "While a conspiracy theory refers to an allegation that may or may not be true, a conspiracy is a true causal chain of events", and "Real conspiracies involving illegal, antidemocratic, or harmful activities by high-level government officials and political elites have been exposed in the past" ([FBI Phoenix Field Office, intelligence bulletin, 2019](https://www.justsecurity.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/FBI-Intelligence-Bulletin-on-Conspiracy-Theories-and-Domestic-Violent-Extremism.pdf)). The physicist David Robert Grimes, modelling how long secrets keep, used a definition that "does not a priori dismiss all such theories as inherently false" ([Grimes, PLOS ONE, 26 January 2016, full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC4728076/fullTextXML)). In each of these definitions, the label alone does not settle whether a claim is true; the evidence does.

### What sets them apart

Douglas and her colleagues list three attributes that set conspiracy theories apart from other explanations: they are "speculative in that they posit actions that are hidden from public scrutiny, complex in that they postulate the coordination of multiple actors, and resistant to falsification in that they postulate that conspirators use stealth and disinformation to cover up their actions", which implies that "people who try to debunk conspiracy theories may, themselves, be part of the conspiracy" ([Douglas et al., 2017](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC5724570/fullTextXML)). The FBI bulletin summarizes the reasoning it found in its cases: theorists rely "on the premises that nothing happens by accident, nothing is as it seems, and everything is connected", and they "dismiss disconfirming evidence as 'fabricated' by the conspirators" ([FBI Phoenix, 2019](https://www.justsecurity.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/FBI-Intelligence-Bulletin-on-Conspiracy-Theories-and-Domestic-Violent-Extremism.pdf)).

Belief in one conspiracy theory goes with belief in others. Robert Brotherton, Christopher French and Alan Pickering cite findings that people who endorse one theory "tend to endorse others, including unrelated theories", even theories invented by researchers and "mutually contradictory theories", a trait the literature calls "conspiracist ideation" ([Brotherton, French and Pickering, Frontiers in Psychology, 21 May 2013, full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC3659314/fullTextXML)). The contradiction was tested directly: Michael Wood, Douglas and Sutton found that the more participants believed Princess Diana had faked her death, the more they believed she was murdered, and that both beliefs rested on the broader view that "the authorities are engaged in a cover-up" ([Wood, Douglas and Sutton, Social Psychological and Personality Science, 25 January 2012, abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/1948550611434786)).

## Who believes, and why: what the research says

### Three motives

Douglas and her colleagues sort the drivers of belief into three groups ([Douglas et al., 2017](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC5724570/fullTextXML)):

| Motive | What the person seeks | Findings the authors cite |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Epistemic | understanding, accuracy, certainty | stronger belief when the motivation to find patterns is raised, among believers in the paranormal, for large events left with "mundane, small-scale explanations", with a need for cognitive closure, and with lower analytic thinking |
| Existential | safety and control | belief rises when people are anxious or feel powerless and falls when their sense of control is affirmed |
| Social | a positive image of self and group | stronger among people on the losing side of politics, among low-status groups and with collective narcissism |

Their review found something less expected: the theories may not deliver what draws people to them. Experimental exposure to conspiracy theories "appears to immediately suppress people's sense of autonomy and control", makes people less inclined to engage in "voting and party politics", and "decreases trust in governmental institutions, even if the conspiracy theories are unrelated to those institutions"; "for many people, conspiracy belief may be more appealing than satisfying" ([Douglas et al., 2017](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC5724570/fullTextXML)). They add a caveat that matters for the rest of this part: most experiments sampled students and survey panellists who were not disadvantaged, and "history has repeatedly shown that corporate and political elites do conspire against public interests. Conspiracy theories play an important role in bringing their misdeeds into the light" (same).

### The correlates, in a meta-analysis

Shauna Bowes, Thomas Costello and Arber Tasimi combined 170 studies, 257 samples, 1,429 effect sizes and 158,473 participants; the strongest correlates of conspiratorial ideation were "(a) perceiving danger and threat, (b) relying on intuition and having odd beliefs and experiences, and (c) being antagonistic and acting superior", with "Considerable heterogeneity" within these relations ([Bowes, Costello and Tasimi, Psychological Bulletin, 2023, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=37358543)). Correlates are not causes, and the authors call for "a unified account" still to be built (same).

### Left, right, and the extremes

Whether one political side believes more is disputed. Jan-Willem van Prooijen, André Krouwel and Thomas Pollet found in four studies in the United States (US) and the Netherlands "a quadratic relationship between strength of political ideology and conspiracy beliefs", higher at both extremes ([van Prooijen, Krouwel and Pollet, Social Psychological and Personality Science, 12 January 2015, abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/1948550614567356)). Roland Imhoff and 41 co-authors, with 104,253 respondents in 26 countries, found "both linear and quadratic relations, albeit small and heterogeneous across countries", stronger among voters of opposition parties, and concluded that conspiracy mentality is associated "with extreme left- and especially extreme right-wing beliefs" ([Imhoff et al., Nature Human Behaviour, March 2022, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=35039654)).

Adam Enders, Uscinski and colleagues, using 20 US surveys from 2012 to 2021 (37,776 respondents) and surveys in 20 more countries (26,416), reported: "In no instance do we observe systematic evidence of a political asymmetry"; the direction of the relationship depended on "the characteristics of the specific conspiracy beliefs employed by researchers and the socio-political context" ([Enders et al., Political Behavior, 22 July 2022, full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9307120/fullTextXML)). They note that theories endorsed by a party's elites find more support in that party, and that "very little is known about the basic nature and scope of conspiracy theorizing outside of North America and Europe" (same). <Claim status="disputed" by="by Imhoff et al. (2022) against Enders et al. (2022)">Whether conspiracy belief leans to one side of politics</Claim> depends, so far, on which theories are asked about, where and when.

## Measuring it: the disputes

### How the question is asked

Steven Clifford, Yongkwang Kim and Brian Sullivan argue that the usual agree-disagree items "inflate estimates of conspiracy belief among the mass public"; a format that offers "an explicit choice between a conspiratorial and a conventional explanation" reduced both no-opinion answers and estimated belief, "particularly among those low in political knowledge or cognitive reflection" ([Clifford, Kim and Sullivan, Public Opinion Quarterly, 2019, abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1093/poq/nfz049)).

The effect of wording shows in QAnon. Uscinski's team asked "Are you a believer in QANON?" and found 5% saying yes in August 2019 and 6% in May 2021 ([Uscinski et al., 2022](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9299316/fullTextXML)). The Public Religion Research Institute (PRRI), surveying 5,625 adults in March 2021, found that 15% agreed that "the government, media, and financial worlds in the U.S. are controlled by a group of Satan-worshipping pedophiles who run a global child sex trafficking operation" ([PRRI, Understanding QAnon's Connection to American Politics, Religion, and Media Consumption, 2021](https://www.prri.org/research/qanon-conspiracy-american-politics-report/)). In the same surveys by Uscinski's team, 34% in May 2021 agreed that "Elites, from government and Hollywood, are engaged in a massive child sex trafficking racket", a statement close to QAnon's core without its name ([Uscinski et al., 2022](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9299316/fullTextXML)). One question asks about an identity, the others about agreement with statements of different breadth; the spread from 6% to 34% is largely a spread in measurement.

### Has belief increased?

According to the surveys Uscinski and colleagues cite, 73% of Americans believe conspiracy theories are "out of control" and about 77% blame social media and the Internet, while journalists speak of a "golden age" of conspiracy theorizing. The team treated the rise as a hypothesis to test: they re-asked the exact wording of 46 conspiracy theories and four items of misinformation polled in the US between 1966 and 2020, compared six theories in six European countries in 2016 and 2018, and tracked a four-item measure of conspiracy thinking from 2012 to 2021 ([Uscinski et al., 2022](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9299316/fullTextXML)). What they found:

| Test | What was compared | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 37 US conspiracy theories polled since 1966 | same wording, then and in 2020 or 2021 | 6 rose (4 to 10 points), 16 did not change, 15 fell (3 to 31 points); average change, a fall of 3.84 points |
| Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) theories, 2020 to 2021 | "purposely created and released" | 31% in March 2020, 27% in June 2020, 29% in May 2021 |
| QAnon feeling thermometer | rating of the movement | 21 in July 2019, 16 in May 2021 |
| Six European countries | 35 comparisons, 2016 and 2018 | 1 rose (Holocaust denial in Sweden, 1% to 3%), 7 fell, 27 unchanged |
| American Conspiracy Thinking Scale | eight surveys, 2012 to 2021 | small rise from 2012 to 2016, otherwise no rise |

The authors acknowledge a limit: if conspiracy-minded people increasingly refused to take polls, their estimates would be "downwardly biased", a possibility they "cannot fully rule out" (same). <Claim status="disputed" by="by Uscinski et al. (2022), against the view of a rise held by most Americans polled and by journalists they cite">That conspiracy belief has grown with social media</Claim> was not supported by the repeated polls; [the part on social media and contagion](/learn/social-media-and-the-contagion-of-symptoms-and-beliefs) and [the psyops part on reach](/learn/from-the-lodge-to-the-network-how-reach-changed) examine what platforms did change.

### A dispute about a famous correlation

In 2013 Stephan Lewandowsky, Klaus Oberauer and Gilles Gignac reported, from a survey of visitors to climate blogs, that endorsing "a cluster of conspiracy theories" predicted rejection of climate science and of findings such as that the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) causes acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS) ([Lewandowsky, Oberauer and Gignac, Psychological Science, May 2013, abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=23531484)). Ruth Dixon and Jonathan Jones, of the University of Oxford, reanalysed that survey and a second, panel survey of US citizens by the same team, and concluded that the claim was "not supported by the data": in the blog survey of 1,145 responses, conspiracist ideation explained "less than 2% of the variance", the sample was "too skewed on the variable of interest", and in the 1,001-person panel the relationship was bell-shaped, with "the highest conspiracist ideation" among respondents whose views on climate science were neutral ([Dixon and Jones, Psychological Science, May 2015, full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC4426137/fullTextXML)). Lewandowsky and colleagues replied in the same issue under the title "The robust relationship between conspiracism and denial of (climate) science" ([Lewandowsky, Gignac and Oberauer, Psychological Science, May 2015, record](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=25814501)).

A follow-up paper by Lewandowsky's group on bloggers' reactions to the first study, "Recursive fury", was retracted in March 2014 after complaints; the journal said its investigation "did not identify any issues with the academic and ethical aspects of the study" but found "the legal context is insufficiently clear", while the authors "stand by their article" ([Frontiers in Psychology, Retraction, 27 March 2014, full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC3967708/fullTextXML)). The research on conspiracy belief has its own disputes about data, analysis and publication, which is a reason to read each finding with its sample and its critics.

## The Protocols of the Elders of Zion: anatomy of a forgery

### The text and its spread

The Protocols first appeared in the autumn of 1903, serialized in Znamia, a St Petersburg newspaper owned by Pavel Krushevan, whose papers had helped incite the Kishinev pogrom that April; in 1905 the text appeared as an appendix to a book by the Russian mystic Sergei Nilus ([United States Holocaust Memorial Museum (USHMM), Holocaust Encyclopedia, An Antisemitic Conspiracy: The Protocols of the Elders of Zion, last edited 23 April 2026](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/protocols-of-the-elders-of-zion)). It claims to record secret meetings of Jewish leaders plotting to control world politics, finance, the press and education; "These meetings never took place and the purported leaders, the so-called Elders of Zion, never existed" (same). It spread after the Russian Revolution of 1917: a German edition in 1919, then translations in Paris, London, New York and Boston, Japanese in 1920 and Arabic in Syria by 1925; in the US, Henry Ford's newspaper serialized The International Jew, which sold more than 500,000 copies and was translated into at least 16 languages (same). Ford issued a public apology in 1927 and ordered remaining copies withdrawn, but overseas publishers ignored him ([USHMM, Protocols of the Elders of Zion: Key Dates](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/protocols-of-the-elders-of-zion-key-dates)).

### The exposure

In 1920 the British journalist Lucien Wolf showed that a chapter of a German novel, Hermann Goedsche's Biarritz of 1868, supplied ideas for the text ([USHMM, 2026](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/protocols-of-the-elders-of-zion)). On 16, 17 and 18 August 1921 The Times of London published three articles by its Constantinople correspondent, later identified as Philip Graves, that presented, in the paper's words, "conclusive proof that the document is in the main a clumsy plagiarism" of a French satire of Napoleon III, Maurice Joly's Dialogue aux Enfers entre Machiavel et Montesquieu ([The Times, The Truth About "The Protocols": A Literary Forgery, reprint of 16 to 18 August 1921](https://archive.org/download/truthaboutthepro00londiala/truthaboutthepro00londiala.pdf)). The articles set passages side by side. Joly's Machiavelli says: "Like the God Vishnu, my press will have a hundred arms, and these arms will give their hands to all the different shades of opinion throughout the country"; the Protocols say: "These newspapers, like the Indian god Vishnu, will be possessed of hundreds of hands, each of which will be feeling the pulse of varying public opinion" (same). The correspondent counted "Fully 50 paragraphs" that were "simply paraphrases of passages in the Dialogues", and wrote that the plagiarist "has given a Jewish colour" to the schemes Joly had put in Machiavelli's mouth to satirize the French emperor (same). "There is no mention of Jews in Dialogue in Hell", the USHMM notes ([USHMM, Key Dates](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/protocols-of-the-elders-of-zion-key-dates)).

The exposure itself had a sourcing gap, which the newspaper stated. The book reached the correspondent through "Mr. X.", a Russian landowner and refugee "who does not wish his real name to be known", who had bought it among old books from a former officer of the Okhrana, the tsarist political police, who had fled to Constantinople; the paper's own suggestion of how Joly's book reached Russia, through Corsicans in the tsar's secret service, was, it wrote, "only a theory" ([The Times, 1921](https://archive.org/download/truthaboutthepro00londiala/truthaboutthepro00londiala.pdf)). The comparison did not rest on the informant: the paper identified the book from a complete copy in the British Museum, published anonymously at Brussels in 1865, according to its preface to the reprint, while the copy Mr. X. had bought carried a preface dated Geneva, 15 October 1864 (same); the USHMM dates the satire to 1864 ([USHMM, 2026](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/protocols-of-the-elders-of-zion)). Who wrote the Protocols remains debated: the Times suggested "some quite unimportant precis writer employed by the Court or by the Okhrana" (same), the USHMM notes that "Some scholars are convinced that Krushevan was the original author" ([USHMM, 2026](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/protocols-of-the-elders-of-zion)), and a review of Michael Hagemeister's study records that the Italian Slavist Cesare De Michelis first showed that several different versions of the text existed ([Barinov, review of Hagemeister, 2019](https://www.chronos-verlag.ch/sites/default/files/press_comments_files/barinov-rezension.pdf)).

### In court: Grahamstown, Bern, Moscow

Courts examined the text three times in the twentieth century. In 1934 a division of the South African Supreme Court at Grahamstown found that a document resembling the Protocols circulated by the Greyshirts was "a defamatory document"; in 1935 a Swiss court in Bern ruled against members of the Swiss National Front who had distributed a German edition, in a case filed in 1933 by two Jewish organizations, and the trial judge called the Protocols "ridiculous nonsense"; in 1993 a Russian court ruled them a forgery and their publication an antisemitic act ([USHMM, 2026](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/protocols-of-the-elders-of-zion); [USHMM, Key Dates](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/protocols-of-the-elders-of-zion-key-dates)). Germany's historical museum dates the Swiss suit to 1934 and says it was through this case that the text's "zweifelhafte historische Aussagekraft" (dubious historical value) was established in court ([Deutsches Historisches Museum, LeMO, Die "Protokolle der Weisen von Zion"](https://www.dhm.de/lemo/kapitel/weimarer-republik/antisemitismus/protokolle-der-weisen-von-zion.html)). Hagemeister, who worked through 30 archives in 10 countries, titles his study of the case "Der Berner Prozess 1933–1937" and judges its results ambiguous: the court recognized the fabricated character of the Protocols, but the case became a political campaign against Hitler, and both sides accused each other of manipulating sources and calling disputed witnesses ([Barinov, 2019](https://www.chronos-verlag.ch/sites/default/files/press_comments_files/barinov-rezension.pdf)). The appeal stage of 1937 could not be read in the sources open to this part.

### Known to be false, used anyway

Exposure did not stop use. Joseph Goebbels wrote in his diary in 1924 that he believed the Protocols were a forgery, but believed "in the inner, but not the factual, truth" of them, in one translation the USHMM gives, or "in the intrinsic but not the factual truth", in another on its own timeline ([USHMM, 2026](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/protocols-of-the-elders-of-zion); [USHMM, Key Dates](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/protocols-of-the-elders-of-zion-key-dates)). The Nazi party's publishing house issued 22 editions between 1919 and 1938, and German authorities printed editions in Russian, Ukrainian and Belarusian in 1943 ([USHMM, 2026](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/protocols-of-the-elders-of-zion)). A US Senate subcommittee called the text "a vicious hoax" in 1964; article 32 of the Hamas charter of 1988 cites it; and a 2024 report of the US Department of State found that it "retains significant influence in Russia" (same; [USHMM, Key Dates](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/protocols-of-the-elders-of-zion-key-dates)). How propagandists used it is part of [the short history of propaganda](/learn/a-short-history-of-propaganda-and-psychological-warfare) and of [the part on manipulators](/learn/how-manipulators-harness-collective-panic).

### In Brazil: the Ellwanger case

Brazil's Supreme Court, the Supremo Tribunal Federal (STF), met the Protocols in a criminal case. The publisher Siegfried Ellwanger had been convicted by the Rio Grande do Sul court of justice for publishing and selling books including the "Os Protocolos dos Sábios de Sião", in a full text "apostilado por Gustavo Barroso" (annotated by Gustavo Barroso), Barroso's "A História Secreta do Brasil" and Ford's "O Judeu Internacional" ([STF, DJ publica acórdão de Habeas Corpus de editor condenado por racismo contra judeus, 19 March 2004](https://noticias.stf.jus.br/?p=5404)). His habeas corpus argued that the crime had lapsed: racism, which the Constitution makes imprescriptible, concerned skin colour, and Jews, the petition said, are an ethnic group rather than a race ([STF, Ellwanger Case: Freedom of Speech v. Anti-Semitism or Racism, case summary](https://www.stf.jus.br/arquivo/cms/publicacaoBOInternet/anexo/link_download/casos_relevantes/en/HC_82424.pdf)). On 17 September 2003 the court denied it by seven votes to three, holding that racism includes discrimination against Jews and that "hate speech is not protected under the freedom of speech" ([STF, Ellwanger Case: Freedom of Speech v. Anti-Semitism or Racism, case summary](https://www.stf.jus.br/arquivo/cms/publicacaoBOInternet/anexo/link_download/casos_relevantes/en/HC_82424.pdf)). The dissent is on the record: the rapporteur, Justice Moreira Alves, held that the crime "não se pode qualificar ... como delito de racismo" (could not be classed as racism) and had lapsed; Justice Marco Aurélio defended freedom of expression and the view that the editor sought a historical revision; Justice Carlos Ayres Britto acquitted for lack of evidence ([STF, 19 March 2004](https://noticias.stf.jus.br/?p=5404)).

## Brazil, 1937: the Plano Cohen

### A document read on the radio

The Plano Cohen shows a state using a forged conspiracy. According to the Dicionário Histórico-Biográfico Brasileiro, compiled by the Centro de Pesquisa e Documentação de História Contemporânea do Brasil (CPDOC) of the Fundação Getulio Vargas (FGV), the document was released on 30 September 1937 as instructions of the Communist International (Komintern) for its agents in Brazil; the head of the president's military staff announced its discovery, and the text was broadcast on the official programme Hora do Brasil ([CPDOC/FGV, Plano Cohen](https://www18.fgv.br/CPDOC/acervo/dicionarios/verbete-tematico/plano-cohen)). The atmosphere had been prepared: at a ceremony on 22 September General Newton Cavalcanti, "notório integralista" (a notorious integralist, of the fascist-inspired Ação Integralista Brasileira), warned that "Não consentiremos nunca que o judeu moscovita faça deste Brasil invejável o mercado sórdido e infame de nosso caráter" (we will never allow the Muscovite Jew to make this enviable Brazil the sordid and infamous market of our character); on 27 September the war minister, Eurico Dutra, told a meeting of generals that "Não é fantasia do governo" (it is not the government's fantasy), and, in the dictionary's words, "Ninguém colocou em dúvida a autenticidade do Plano Cohen" (no one questioned the Plano Cohen's authenticity) (same).

On 1 October President Getúlio Vargas asked Congress for a 90-day state of war. Opposition deputies asked to see the original document; the majority leader, Carlos Luz, answered that it could not be contested because it bore the "autenticidade do selo oficial" (the authenticity of the official seal); the measure passed by 138 votes to 58 in the Chamber and 23 to 5 in the Senate, the state of war was decreed on 2 October, and on 10 November 1937 Vargas dissolved Congress and imposed the constitution of the Estado Novo ([CPDOC/FGV, Plano Cohen](https://www18.fgv.br/CPDOC/acervo/dicionarios/verbete-tematico/plano-cohen)).

## Pizzagate: from a leaked inbox to a rifle

### How the story was assembled

Marc Tuters, Emilija Jokubauskaitė and Daniel Bach traced Pizzagate to a 25-hour period, from 22:17 on 2 November 2016 to 23:24 on 3 November, in which users of the 4chan forum /pol/ combed emails of Hillary Clinton's campaign chairman, John Podesta, which had been "obtained by Russian hackers" and leaked by WikiLeaks, and built a story of a child-abuse ring linked to a Washington pizzeria; the phrase "cheese pizza" in the emails was read as a code word, and an invitation from the performance artist Marina Abramović mentioning her work "Spirit Cooking" was read as evidence of Satanic ritual ([Tuters, Jokubauskaitė and Bach, M/C Journal, 2018](https://journal.media-culture.org.au/index.php/mcjournal/article/view/1422)). Users produced maps and diagrams linking businesses near the restaurant, which served to "identify connections", to escape the forum's purging of threads and to carry the story to a broader audience (same). The article dates the hashtag's trending to "4 November 2017", a slip for 2016 given its own timeline (same). The return of Satanic-cult stories, decades after the ritual-abuse scare, links this case to [the part on the Satanic panic](/learn/satanic-panic-false-memories-and-the-escola-base-case).

### The shooting and the court record

On 4 December 2016 Edgar Maddison Welch, then 28, drove from Salisbury, North Carolina, with an AR-15 rifle loaded with about 29 rounds, a .38-calibre revolver and a shotgun; at about 3 p.m. he entered the restaurant while employees and customers, including children, fled, tried to force a locked room with a butter knife, fired the rifle several times into the door, turned it towards an employee, and after more than 20 minutes walked out unarmed and was arrested ([US Attorney's Office for the District of Columbia, 24 March 2017](https://justice.gov/usao-dc/pr/north-carolina-man-pleads-guilty-charges-armed-assault-northwest-washington-pizza)). He pleaded guilty on 24 March 2017 to interstate transportation of a firearm and to assault with a dangerous weapon; on 22 June 2017 Judge Ketanji Brown Jackson sentenced him to four years in prison and $5,744 in restitution, saying, as the prosecutors' release reports, that "the extent of the recklessness in this case is breathtaking" ([US Attorney's Office for the District of Columbia, 22 June 2017](https://www.justice.gov/usao-dc/pr/north-carolina-man-sentenced-four-year-prison-term-armed-assault-northwest-washington)). The public broadcaster NPR (formerly National Public Radio) reported that he said he had come to "self-investigate"; no one was hurt ([NPR, 26 March 2017](https://www.npr.org/sections/thetwo-way/2017/03/26/521545788/conspiracy-theorist-alex-jones-apologizes-for-promoting-pizzagate)).

The broadcaster Alex Jones, whose show had promoted the story, apologized in March 2017, saying that to his knowledge neither the restaurant nor its owner "were involved in any human trafficking as was part of the theories about Pizzagate that were being written about in many media outlets and which we commented upon"; NPR, citing The Washington Post, noted that under Texas law he had to retract within a month of the owner's letter to avoid exposure to punitive damages in a libel suit, while Jones said he apologized because "we think it is the right thing to do" ([NPR, 2017](https://www.npr.org/sections/thetwo-way/2017/03/26/521545788/conspiracy-theorist-alex-jones-apologizes-for-promoting-pizzagate)). The FBI's bulletin later listed the case among examples of theorists who, "claiming to act as 'researchers' or 'investigators'", single out targets that then face harassment and threats, and recorded that in December 2018 a California man found with bomb-making materials said he meant to blow up a monument at the Illinois Capitol to "make Americans aware of 'Pizzagate' and the New World Order (NWO), who were dismantling society" ([FBI Phoenix, 2019](https://www.justsecurity.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/FBI-Intelligence-Bulletin-on-Conspiracy-Theories-and-Domestic-Violent-Extremism.pdf)).

## QAnon: a theory that predicts, fails and adapts

### The FBI's first assessment

The Phoenix bulletin, circulated on 30 May 2019 according to NBC News, was, in its own words, "the first FBI product examining the threat from conspiracy theory-driven domestic extremists" ([NBC News, 1 August 2019](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/ncna1038441); [FBI Phoenix, 2019](https://www.justsecurity.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/FBI-Intelligence-Bulletin-on-Conspiracy-Theories-and-Domestic-Violent-Extremism.pdf)). It described QAnon as the belief that "An anonymous government official known as 'Q' posts classified information online to reveal a covert effort, led by President Trump, to dismantle a conspiracy involving 'deep state' actors and global elites allegedly engaged in an international child sex trafficking ring", and assessed "with high confidence" that such theories "very likely motivate some domestic extremists, wholly or in part, to commit criminal and sometimes violent activity" (same). Its examples included a man who blocked the Hoover Dam bypass bridge with an armoured truck on 15 June 2018 and, according to a source the bulletin cites, referenced QAnon directly, and the leader of a group in Tucson who in 2018, after police discredited his claim of a child-trafficking camp, alleged a cover-up, referenced QAnon and searched with armed members for more camps, while the group accused residents, businesses and local officials (same). The bulletin also named a cause outside the theorists: "the uncovering of real conspiracies or cover-ups involving illegal, harmful, or unconstitutional activities by government officials or leading political figures" (same). The FBI declined to comment to NBC News on the leaked document ([NBC News, 2019](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/ncna1038441)).

### After 6 January 2021

A joint assessment by the FBI and the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), dated 4 June 2021, states that QAnon's "foundational principle holds that a corrupt cabal of 'global elites' and 'deep state' actors run a Satan-worshiping international child sex trafficking ring", that its narratives expand to include "alleged election fraud, the COVID-19 pandemic, and the dangers of 5G technology", and that the FBI had arrested "more than 20 self-identified QAnon adherents" who took part in the violent entry of the Capitol on 6 January ([FBI and DHS, Adherence to QAnon Conspiracy Theory by Some Domestic Violent Extremists, 4 June 2021](https://www.heinrich.senate.gov/download/qanon-fbi-response21)). Its outlook foresaw two paths: some adherents "will begin to believe they can no longer 'trust the plan'" and turn to violence, while others "will disengage from the movement" after the change of administration, the removal of QAnon content from mainstream platforms and "the failure of long-promised QAnon-linked events to materialize" (same). The document adds that interacting with QAnon content "without engaging in violence or other illegal activity is legal and protected by the First Amendment" (same). What happens to a movement when its predictions fail is the subject of [the part on failed prophecies and the closed group](/learn/cults-failed-prophecies-and-the-closed-group).

On 20 January 2025 President Donald Trump granted "a full, complete and unconditional pardon to all other individuals convicted of offenses related to events that occurred at or near the United States Capitol on January 6, 2021", commuted 14 sentences to time served and directed the Attorney General to pursue dismissal of all pending indictments; the proclamation states that it "ends a grave national injustice that has been perpetrated upon the American people over the last four years" ([Proclamation 10887, Federal Register, 29 January 2025](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-01-29/pdf/2025-01950.pdf)). The convictions remain in the court records; their penalties were lifted.

## When courts weighed conspiracy claims: Sandy Hook and the 2020 election

### The Sandy Hook hoax claims

Alex Jones told his audience for years that the 2012 shooting at Sandy Hook Elementary School in Newtown, Connecticut, in which 20 first graders and six educators were killed, was staged by "crisis actors" ([Associated Press via Insurance Journal, 9 December 2024](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/east/2024/12/09/804000.htm)). The FBI bulletin records that relatives of victims were harassed and threatened by people who believed the shooting a hoax, and that a Florida woman was sentenced in 2017 for sending death threats to a victim's father ([FBI Phoenix, 2019](https://www.justsecurity.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/FBI-Intelligence-Bulletin-on-Conspiracy-Theories-and-Domestic-Violent-Extremism.pdf)). A Connecticut jury awarded $965 million in compensatory damages in 2022; on 6 December 2024 the Appellate Court unanimously upheld that sum, citing the "traumatic threats and harassment" stemming from "the lies, as propagated by the defendants, that the Sandy Hook massacre was a hoax", and vacated $150 million awarded under the state's unfair trade practices law ([Associated Press via Insurance Journal, 2024](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/east/2024/12/09/804000.htm)). Jones's lawyer said the jury had been "sold a bill of goods" and that the sorrow of the plaintiffs was not Jones's fault (same). The Connecticut Supreme Court declined to hear his appeal in April 2025, and Jones has since acknowledged that the shooting was "100% real" ([Associated Press via WJAR, 9 April 2025](https://turnto10.com/news/local/connecticut-supreme-court-declines-alex-jones-appeal-1-billion-verdict-sandy-hook-elementary-school-conspiracy-theory-hoax-newtown-april-9-2025)); the US Supreme Court denied review on 14 October 2025 ([Bloomberg Government, 14 October 2025](https://news.bgov.com/bankruptcy-law/alex-jones-1-billion-defamation-case-rejected-by-supreme-court)).

### Dominion and the 2020 election

Dominion Voting Systems sued Fox News over segments in which, as NPR described them, "wild and spurious conspiracies held it had switched votes" from Donald Trump to Joe Biden ([NPR, 18 April 2023](https://www.npr.org/2023/04/18/1170339114/fox-news-dominion-settlement)). Fox argued that it had accurately reported what newsworthy figures, including a sitting president, were saying, and invoked the neutral report, fair report and opinion privileges ([Delaware Superior Court, 31 March 2023](https://courts.delaware.gov/Opinions/Download.aspx?id=345820); [NPR, 2023](https://www.npr.org/2023/04/18/1170339114/fox-news-dominion-settlement)). Judge Eric Davis granted Dominion summary judgment on falsity, holding that "none of the Statements relating to Dominion about the 2020 election are true" ([Delaware Superior Court, 2023](https://courts.delaware.gov/Opinions/Download.aspx?id=345820)). On 18 April 2023 the parties settled for $787.5 million; Fox said: "We acknowledge the Court's rulings finding certain claims about Dominion to be false" and that the settlement "reflects FOX's continued commitment to the highest journalistic standards" ([NPR, 2023](https://www.npr.org/2023/04/18/1170339114/fox-news-dominion-settlement)).

## Brazil: voting machines and a coup plot in the courts

### The ambassadors meeting

On 30 June 2023 Brazil's electoral court, the Tribunal Superior Eleitoral (TSE), declared former president Jair Bolsonaro ineligible for eight years, by five votes to two, for abuse of political power and misuse of the media at a meeting with foreign ambassadors at the Alvorada palace on 18 July 2022 at which he attacked the electronic voting system ([Agência Brasil, 30 June 2023](https://agenciabrasil.ebc.com.br/justica/noticia/2023-06/por-5-votos-2-tse-torna-bolsonaro-inelegivel-por-oito-anos)). The court's president, Justice Alexandre de Moraes, held that Bolsonaro used public resources to attack the judiciary and to spread disinformation to discredit the voting system, including the insinuation that votes could not be audited; Justices Raul Araújo and Nunes Marques dissented, finding the meeting not grave enough to warrant ineligibility; the vice-presidential candidate, Walter Braga Netto, was acquitted unanimously (same). The defence, led by Tarcísio Vieira de Carvalho, argued that the meeting had no electoral purpose, served as a "contraponto institucional" (institutional counterpoint) to propose changes to the system, and took place before the candidacy was official, so that at most a fine applied (same). The earlier electoral cases over mass messaging on WhatsApp are in [the psyops part on Brazil's courts](/learn/brazil-mass-messaging-the-courts-and-the-cpmi).

## Conspiracies that were real, and how they came to light

### The tobacco industry

United States v. Philip Morris was a civil racketeering case under the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act (RICO). After a trial of about nine months with 84 witnesses, Judge Gladys Kessler found that the companies "have conspired together to violate the substantive provisions of RICO", that they had known the harms of smoking "for at least 50 years or more" and denied them "consistently, repeatedly, and with enormous skill and sophistication", and that lawyers "played an absolutely central role" in a scheme that included directing research, vetting papers and "document destruction policies" ([United States v. Philip Morris USA, 17 August 2006](https://www.publichealthlawcenter.org/sites/default/files/resources/doj-final-opinion.pdf)). She ordered the companies to stop using descriptors such as "low tar" and "light" and to publish corrective statements (same). The proof came from "millions of documents" exchanged in litigation and from testimony in open court (same).

### Tuskegee

From 1932 the US Public Health Service, with the Tuskegee Institute, studied 600 Black men, 399 with syphilis and 201 without, without collecting informed consent; the men were told they were being treated for "bad blood"; penicillin became the treatment of choice by 1943 but was not offered to them; after an Associated Press story in 1972, an advisory panel found the study "ethically unjustified" and it was ended; a class action produced a $10 million settlement in 1974, and President Bill Clinton apologized on 16 May 1997 ([Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), Timeline of the Untreated Syphilis Study at Tuskegee](https://www.cdc.gov/tuskegee/about/timeline.html)). Grimes notes that it was exposed in 1972 by the researcher Peter Buxtun, after ethical questions had been raised in the 1960s ([Grimes, 2016](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC4728076/fullTextXML)). The study is one source of the distrust taken up in [the part on health scares](/learn/health-scares-from-the-revolta-da-vacina-to-the-autism-myth).

### How long secrets keep

Grimes estimated, from three exposed affairs (the National Security Agency's surveillance programme PRISM, Tuskegee and the FBI forensic scandal in which "at least 26 of the 28 dedicated hair analysts gave misleading testimony"), a best-case leak rate per conspirator per year, and applied it to popular theories ([Grimes, 2016](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC4728076/fullTextXML)):

| Claimed conspiracy | People assumed involved | Years to imminent exposure (failure probability above 95%) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Faked moon landings | staff of the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) at the 1965 peak, 411,000 | 3.68 |
| Climate-change fraud, scientists only | active climate researchers | 26.77 |
| Climate-change fraud, with scientific bodies | adds the bodies endorsing the consensus | 3.70 |
| Vaccine danger hidden, CDC and the World Health Organization (WHO) only | public health bodies | 34.78 |
| Vaccine danger hidden, with drug companies | adds industry staff | 3.15 |
| A suppressed cure for cancer | industry staff | 3.17 |

Grimes concluded that "large conspiracies (≥1000 agents) quickly become untenable and prone to failure" ([Grimes, 2016](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC4728076/fullTextXML)). The paper needed a correction five weeks later for an equation that did not hold when the number of conspirators changes over time; the correction states that the conclusions stand and replaces one figure, the 650 participants at which a single event's odds of exposure exceed 5% within ten years, with 1,328 ([Grimes, Correction, PLOS ONE, 1 March 2016](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC4773114/fullTextXML)). The original text also gives 2,521 conspirators as the five-year limit where its own table gives 2,531 ([Grimes, 2016](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC4728076/fullTextXML)). The model rests on three cases, whose staff figures the author took at their upper bound so as to keep the estimated leak rate generous, and on his estimates of how many people each theory would need; it measures plausibility, not truth.

In the table, the three cases that courts or a panel found real were exposed by internal documents, insiders, recordings and testimony, and examined by institutions: a federal court, a federal panel, the STF. The invented ones rested on readings of other material (a satire, leaked emails, an official seal) and, in the record, on promoters who treated counter-evidence as part of the plot, the trait Douglas and her colleagues call resistance to falsification ([Douglas et al., 2017](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC5724570/fullTextXML); [FBI Phoenix, 2019](https://www.justsecurity.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/FBI-Intelligence-Bulletin-on-Conspiracy-Theories-and-Domestic-Violent-Extremism.pdf)). And the bodies that exposed the real conspiracies are the same kinds of institutions that conspiracy theories ask people to distrust, which is one reason why the FBI's analysts listed the uncovering of real cover-ups among the drivers of conspiracy theorizing ([FBI Phoenix, 2019](https://www.justsecurity.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/FBI-Intelligence-Bulletin-on-Conspiracy-Theories-and-Domestic-Violent-Extremism.pdf)).

## What to remember

- A conspiracy theory explains an event as a secret plot by a powerful, malevolent group; a conspiracy is a real chain of events. The label settles nothing; evidence does.
- Research links belief to epistemic, existential and social motives, and finds that the theories may frustrate those motives; the strongest correlates are perceived threat, intuition and antagonism.
- Measurement shapes the numbers: agree-disagree items inflate estimates, QAnon support reads 6% or 15% depending on the question, and repeated polls since 1966 show no general rise.
- The Protocols were shown in 1921 to be plagiarized from a satire that never mentions Jews, were ruled false by courts and remained in use; in Brazil the STF upheld the conviction of a publisher of the Protocols for racism in 2003.
- The Plano Cohen of 1937 was a fabricated communist plan used to justify a state of war and a dictatorship; its author and the army's chief of staff later gave conflicting accounts of how it was used.
- Courts found Pizzagate's rumours unfounded, the Sandy Hook hoax claims defamatory and the statements about Dominion false; Brazil's courts found the 2022 attacks on voting machines an abuse of power and convicted the core of a coup plot, a judgment the defence contests.
- Real conspiracies, such as the tobacco industry's deception and the Tuskegee study, were exposed by documents, insiders and institutions; large invented conspiracies would require secrecy among thousands for decades.

<ReaderQuestions>

- If repeated polls show no general rise in conspiracy belief, why does so much of the public feel that such beliefs are spreading, and what would count as evidence either way?
- The same institutions that exposed tobacco's deception and the Tuskegee study are the ones conspiracy theories ask people to distrust. How should an institution earn trust from people who have reasons to doubt it?
- A court can find a statement false or a defendant liable, but believers may read the judgment as proof of the plot. What, if anything, can a court ruling do for public belief?
- The Times exposed the Protocols through an anonymous informant and a comparison anyone could check. When is an anonymous source acceptable, and what makes a claim checkable?
- Grimes's model says large conspiracies leak quickly, yet the coup plot judged in Brazil involved dozens of people over two years before it was exposed. What does the size of a group tell you, and what does it not?

</ReaderQuestions>
