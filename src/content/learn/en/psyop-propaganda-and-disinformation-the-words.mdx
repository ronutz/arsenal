A North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) manual, a Council of Europe report, a European Union (EU) threat report and a Meta enforcement notice can name one activity differently, each term written for its owner's purpose: some require a false message, others a hidden author, a foreign state or a soldier. This part of the [series](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign) compares each term, in its owner's words, on intent, truth, actor, content and behaviour. After reading, you can identify the definition a report relies on and what it does and does not claim.

## Psychological operations and MISO

NATO's strategic communications doctrine, Allied Joint Publication (AJP) 10 of March 2023, defines a psychological operation (PsyOps there, PSYOPS in older NATO doctrine, PSYOP in United States (US) usage) as "Planned activities using methods of communication and other means directed at approved audiences in order to influence perceptions, attitudes and behaviours, affecting the achievement of political and military objectives" ([AJP-10, lexicon](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6525459d244f8e00138e7343/AJP_10_Strat_Comm_Change_1_web.pdf)); NATO's glossary, Allied Administrative Publication (AAP) 06, dates the wording to 31 January 2013 ([AAP-06](https://www.coemed.org/files/stanags/05_AAP/AAP-06_2019_EF.pdf)). The earlier doctrine, AJP-3.10.1 of September 2014 (its United Kingdom (UK) copy now marked withdrawn), added that "PSYOPS must be based on true information" and that they are generally attributable to NATO or a concurring partner ([AJP-3.10.1, paragraphs 0114 and 0115](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a80ce48e5274a2e87dbbecb/20150223-AJP_3_10_1_PSYOPS_with_UK_Green_pages.pdf)).

In US joint doctrine, Joint Publication (JP) 3-13.2, as amended on 20 December 2011, defines military information support operations (MISO) as "Planned operations to convey selected information and indicators to foreign audiences to influence their emotions, motives, objective reasoning, and ultimately the behavior of foreign governments, organizations, groups, and individuals in a manner favorable to the originator's objectives" ([JP 3-13.2, glossary](https://www.bits.de/NRANEU/others/jp-doctrine/JP3-13.2C1%2811%29.pdf)), and adds that "US MISO will not target US citizens at any time, in any location globally, or under any circumstances" (first chapter).

### The renaming, step by step

In July 2010 the US Special Operations Command spokesman, Ken McGraw, told the Associated Press (AP) that Defense Secretary Robert Gates and Admiral Eric Olson, the Special Operations commander, had approved the new name in mid-June, partly over foreign and domestic sensitivities to the term that often led to the mission being misunderstood ([AP, 2010](https://www.foxnews.com/us/army-drops-psychological-operations-as-name-of-branch-that-tries-to-influence-enemy-forces.amp)). Rosa Brooks, then senior adviser to the Under Secretary of Defense for Policy, wrote in an internal e-mail: "This is just a terminological change, not a substantive change" ([Army Times, 2017](https://www.armytimes.com/news/your-army/2017/11/06/the-armys-psychological-operations-community-is-getting-its-name-back/)). Gates's reasoning survives only secondhand: Army majors Chaveso Cook and David Cowen quote him: <Claim status="reported">"the term PSYOP tends to connote propaganda, brainwashing, manipulation, and deceit"</Claim>, citing a 2010 article by Alfred H. Paddock Jr. ([Cook and Cowen, 2018](https://www.psywarrior.com/PSYOPvsMiso.htm)).

JP 3-13.2's Change 1 replaced psychological operations with MISO throughout, citing a Secretary of Defense memo of 3 December 2010; the amended edition had been titled Psychological Operations, and the change, it states, does not affect use of the older terms outside the US ([JP 3-13.2, Change 1](https://www.bits.de/NRANEU/others/jp-doctrine/JP3-13.2C1%2811%29.pdf)). An Army message of 19 April 2011 limited the change to the function, not unit names, and the units returned to PSYOP under US Army Special Operations Command (USASOC) guidance from 27 October 2017 ([Cook and Cowen](https://www.psywarrior.com/PSYOPvsMiso.htm)). The USASOC spokesman, Lt. Col. Robert Bockholt, told Army Times that psychological operations names the units, MISO the function their soldiers perform ([Army Times](https://www.armytimes.com/news/your-army/2017/11/06/the-armys-psychological-operations-community-is-getting-its-name-back/)).

A memorandum stamped 2 December 2025, headed Secretary of War, tells the Department of War (DoW) to use PSYOP instead of MISO "Effective immediately", with JP 3-13.2's definition of MISO ending instead "in a manner favorable toward DoW objectives" ([memorandum, 2025](https://media.defense.gov/2025/Dec/08/2003839440/-1/-1/0/CHANGING-THE-TERM-MILITARY-INFORMATION-SUPPORT-OPERATIONS-BACK-TO-PSYCHOLOGICAL-OPERATIONS.PDF)). After fifteen years, it says, PSYOP better aligns functions with branding and "eliminates confusion". It orders Department of Defense Instruction 3607.02 of 2016 revised and full implementation by the end of fiscal 2026. DefenseScoop reported it on 9 December 2025, naming the signer as Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth ([DefenseScoop](https://defensescoop.com/2025/12/09/hegseth-memo-psyop-miso-psychological-operations/)).

Army training publication (ATP) 3-53 of 20 April 2026, replacing the 2013 Field Manual 3-53, is briefer: "Psychological operations are operations to influence the behavior of targeted foreign audiences in support of a commander's objectives" ([ATP 3-53, paragraph 1-9](https://armypubs.army.mil/epubs/DR_pubs/DR_a/ARN46327-ATP_3-53-000-WEB-1.pdf)). Its history appendix says the 2010 change sought "to capture the more nuanced set of the military's information and influence activities", and the memorandum of "XX December 2025" sought "to more accurately reflect the psychological effects these operations hinge upon" (appendix A). Whether that instruction or JP 3-13.2 has since been revised is not shown in the sources cited here.

Christopher Paul of the RAND Corporation welcomed the 2010 change but wrote that "current PSYOP doctrine does allow for the possibility of products and messages that are not wholly true, or are made to appear as if they were written or published by someone else", and asked that doctrine ban them ([Paul, 2010](https://archive.smallwarsjournal.com/index.php/node/9441)). Cook and Cowen found in 2018 no evidence that the change had altered attitudes in the State Department ([Military Review](https://www.psywarrior.com/PSYOPvsMiso.htm)).

## Information operations

For NATO, information operations is a staff role, not a message: "A staff function to analyze, plan, assess and integrate information activities to create desired effects on the will, understanding and capability of adversaries, potential adversaries and audiences in support of mission objectives" ([AJP-10, lexicon](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6525459d244f8e00138e7343/AJP_10_Strat_Comm_Change_1_web.pdf)). JP 3-13.2 refers readers to JP 3-13 for the US term. At Twitter the same words named a platform policy, which Camille François and evelyn douek list beside Meta's and Google's equivalents ([François and douek, 2021](https://tsjournal.org/index.php/jots/article/download/17/8/15)).

## Propaganda

George Creel, who ran the US Committee on Public Information in the First World War, wrote in 1920: "We did not call it propaganda, for that word, in German hands, had come to be associated with deceit and corruption" ([Creel, 1920](https://archive.org/download/howweadvertameri00creerich/howweadvertameri00creerich_djvu.txt)). Edward Bernays answered in 1928 that "whether, in any instance, propaganda is good or bad depends upon the merit of the cause urged, and the correctness of the information published" ([Bernays, 1928, chapter II](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/78634/pg78634.txt)).

NATO's definition of propaganda names no actor: "Information, especially of a biased or misleading nature, used to promote a political cause or point of view" ([AJP-10, lexicon](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6525459d244f8e00138e7343/AJP_10_Strat_Comm_Change_1_web.pdf)). The US definition reserves the word for the other side: "Any form of adversary communication, especially of a biased or misleading nature, designed to influence the opinions, emotions, attitudes, or behavior of any group in order to benefit the sponsor, either directly or indirectly" ([JP 3-13.2, glossary](https://www.bits.de/NRANEU/others/jp-doctrine/JP3-13.2C1%2811%29.pdf)).

## Active measures

The US State Department wrote in 1987 that the term "is a literal translation from Russian, aktivnyye meropriyatiya, which denotes covert or deceptive operations conducted in support of Soviet foreign policy", distinct from espionage and from ordinary diplomacy, and that <Claim status="attributed">"Deception is the essence of active measures"</Claim> ([State Department, 1987](https://sspconline.org/sites/default/files/2026-01/soviet-influence-activities-1987_0.pdf)). This is one Cold War side describing the other; no Soviet definition is among the sources here. For the best-documented case, the 1980s story that the virus causing acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS) was a US weapon, Thomas Boghardt records that Moscow never publicly acknowledged authorship, and that in 1992 Yevgeny Primakov, head of Russian foreign intelligence, confirmed the participation of the Soviet Committee for State Security (KGB) ([Boghardt, 2009](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/1-Operation_INFEKTION_Soviet_Bloc_AIDS_Disinformation_Campaign.pdf)).

## Disinformation, misinformation and malinformation

Claire Wardle and Hossein Derakhshan set out the trio in a report published by the Council of Europe on 27 September 2017, which states that its opinions are the authors' ([Wardle and Derakhshan, 2017](https://firstdraftnews.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/PREMS-162317-GBR-2018-Report-de%CC%81sinformation-1.pdf)). They set aside "fake news" and sorted the field "Using the dimensions of harm and falseness" (executive summary):

- Dis-information: "Information that is false and deliberately created to harm a person, social group, organization or country" (part 1).
- Mis-information: "Information that is false, but not created with the intention of causing harm" (part 1).
- Mal-information: "Information that is based on reality, used to inflict harm on a person, organization or country" (part 1); their example is the leak of Emmanuel Macron's e-mails before the French run-off vote of 7 May 2017.

The EU wrote its own versions in the European Democracy Action Plan (EDAP) of 3 December 2020. Misinformation is "false or misleading content shared without harmful intent though the effects can still be harmful"; disinformation is "false or misleading content that is spread with an intention to deceive or secure economic or political gain and which may cause public harm" ([EDAP, section 4](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-13678-2020-INIT/en/pdf)). The plan has no malinformation category and called its wording provisional, promising refined common definitions (section 4). NATO's AJP-10 lexicon cites the Concise Oxford English Dictionary: "Information which is intended to mislead" ([AJP-10](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6525459d244f8e00138e7343/AJP_10_Strat_Comm_Change_1_web.pdf)). James Pamment, in a paper commissioned by the European External Action Service (EEAS) and published by the Carnegie Endowment in September 2020, added that misinformation should not be called a campaign, because without intent its spread is uncoordinated ([Pamment, 2020](https://carnegieendowment.org/files/Pamment_-_Crafting_Disinformation_1.pdf)).

## Foreign information manipulation and interference

The EEAS defines foreign information manipulation and interference (FIMI) as conduct, not content: it "describes a mostly non-illegal pattern of behaviour that threatens or has the potential to negatively impact values, procedures and political processes", carried out intentionally and in coordination by state or non-state actors, including proxies inside and outside their territory ([EEAS, 2026, glossary](https://euvsdisinfo.eu/uploads/2026/03/EEAS-4th-Threat-Report_web_compressed.pdf)); its first report, of February 2023, used nearly the same words ([EEAS, 2023, glossary](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/rr9q9n-yuiux/2023-02-00-EU-First-EEAS-Report-Foreign-Manipulation-Interference-Threats-via-website.pdf)). The EDAP's narrower "foreign interference" requires a state: "coercive and deceptive efforts to disrupt the free formation and expression of individuals' political will by a foreign state actor or its agents" ([EDAP](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-13678-2020-INIT/en/pdf)).

The 2026 report <Claim status="attributed" by="by the EEAS">attributes 29% of 540 incidents in 2025 to Russia and connects 6% to China</Claim>, leaving 65% unattributed ([EEAS, 2026](https://euvsdisinfo.eu/uploads/2026/03/EEAS-4th-Threat-Report_web_compressed.pdf)). Neither state's reply to these reports is among the sources here. China's Mission to the EU said of an EEAS report on pandemic disinformation in 2021 that a report with no factual basis "is in itself an example of disinformation" ([Chinese Mission, 2021](https://eu.china-mission.gov.cn/eng/fyrjh/202104/t20210429_9099561.htm)). Russia's representative Dmitry Polyanskiy said in June 2026 that "under the euphemistic label of 'fighting foreign information manipulation' (FIMI), an instrument for suppressing dissent is being created" ([Polyanskiy, 2026](https://france.mid.ru/fr/presse/dmitry_polyanskiy_on_the_address_by_the_secretary_general_of_the_council_of_europe_11_june_2026/)).

## Coordinated inauthentic behaviour

Meta defines its policy term, coordinated inauthentic behaviour (CIB), as "coordinated efforts to manipulate public debate for a strategic goal, in which fake accounts are central to the operation", and says it focuses on behaviour rather than content when removing such operations ([Meta, 2023](https://about.fb.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Meta-Quarterly-Adversarial-Threat-Report-Q42022.pdf)). The next clause changed: the May 2024 report still says "no matter who's behind them, what they post or whether they're foreign or domestic" ([Meta, 2024](https://transparency.meta.com/sr/Q1-2024-Adversarial-threat-report)); the February 2025 report omits "who's behind them" ([Meta, 2025](https://transparency.meta.com/sr/Q4-2024-Adversarial-threat-report)). evelyn douek argued in July 2020 that "Coordination and authenticity are not binary states but matters of degree, and this ambiguity will be exploited by actors of all stripes" ([douek, 2020](https://cyber.harvard.edu/story/2020-07/what-does-coordinated-inauthentic-behavior-actually-mean)).

In a Brazilian domestic case, on 8 July 2020 Meta removed Facebook and Instagram accounts, saying <Claim status="attributed" by="by Meta">its investigation "found links to individuals associated with the Social Liberal Party and some of the employees of the offices of Anderson Moraes, Alana Passos, Eduardo Bolsonaro, Flavio Bolsonaro and Jair Bolsonaro"</Claim>, and that such domestic campaigns blur the line between healthy public debate and manipulation ([Meta, 2020](https://about.fb.com/news/2020/07/removing-political-coordinated-inauthentic-behavior/)). Meta's Nathaniel Gleicher told reporters it saw office employees involved but no evidence about the politicians themselves. The Social Liberal Party (PSL) spokesperson <Claim status="denied">"denied any responsibility over the removed accounts"</Claim>; Senator Flávio Bolsonaro said it was impossible to evaluate what had been banned or whether the platform had crossed the limits of censorship; Eduardo Bolsonaro did not comment ([AP, 2020](https://kstp.com/kstp-news/international/facebook-removes-false-accounts-linked-to-brazils-bolsonaro/)). State deputies Alana Passos and Anderson Moraes replied that she never directed fake profiles and that his removed aide was a real person ([Congresso em Foco, 2020](https://www.congressoemfoco.com.br/noticia/31287/facebook-remove-contas-ligadas-ao-psl-e-a-familia-bolsonaro)). President Jair Bolsonaro said the next day that opponents "não apresentam uma prova sequer", offer not a single proof ([ABCdoABC, 2020](https://www.abcdoabc.com.br/apos-acao-do-facebook-bolsonaro-desafia-imprensa-e-adversarios/)).

## Influence operations

The EDAP defines an information influence operation as "coordinated efforts by either domestic or foreign actors to influence a target audience using a range of deceptive means, including suppressing independent information sources in combination with disinformation" ([EDAP, section 4](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-13678-2020-INIT/en/pdf)). Pamment's proposal, three months earlier, required them to serve an adversary's objectives and noted that true information can too, in an opportunistically timed hack or leak or in words taken out of context ([Pamment](https://carnegieendowment.org/files/Pamment_-_Crafting_Disinformation_1.pdf)). OpenAI, in May 2024, defined covert influence operations as "deceptive attempts to manipulate public opinion or influence political outcomes without revealing the true identity or intentions of the actors behind them" ([OpenAI, 2024](https://www.rivista.ai/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/Threat_Intel_Report.pdf)). Ben Nimmo and Eric Hutchins, then at Meta, noted that these terms may have distinct meanings but are often used interchangeably ([Nimmo and Hutchins, 2023](https://assets.carnegieendowment.org/static/files/202303-Nimmo_Hutchins_Online_Ops.pdf)).

## Where the definitions disagree

**Intent.** Disinformation requires intent to harm (Council of Europe), to deceive or gain (EU), to mislead (NATO). In the Council of Europe's summary, a false item shared with no harm meant is mis-information; knowingly shared to cause harm, dis-information.

**Truth.** Disinformation and misinformation require falsity (the EU adds misleading content); malinformation is true. FIMI, CIB and influence operations need no false statement, and Pamment includes timed leaks of true material. NATO's 2014 principles require true PSYOPS; Paul wrote that US doctrine allowed products "not wholly true".

**Actor and audience.** Foreign interference needs a foreign state; FIMI admits non-state actors but must be foreign; CIB and the EDAP's influence operation may be domestic. US propaganda is the adversary's. US MISO and PSYOP target foreign audiences, NATO's approved ones.

## Content or behaviour

Camille François's ABC (actors, behaviours, content) framework of September 2019 separates "Manipulative actors, deceptive behaviors, harmful content", and notes that industry has concentrated on regulating behaviour and governments on content ([François, 2019](https://www.ivir.nl/publicaties/download/ABC_Framework_2019_Sept_2019.pdf)). On that line, disinformation, misinformation, malinformation and propaganda are content terms (kinds of information); CIB, FIMI, influence operations, active measures, PSYOP and MISO are behaviour terms (efforts, operations or activities). A behaviour term can fit when every message is true; a content term, one person sharing one post.

## Why this series says "psychological operations"

In doctrine, psychological operations is narrow: planned military activity for approved or foreign audiences, in NATO's 2014 principles truthful and generally attributable. No owner's term covers this series' range, from a repeated rumour or a scam call to a state campaign: disinformation needs a falsehood, CIB fake accounts, FIMI a foreign actor, MISO a soldier. All share the doctrinal object, to "influence perceptions, attitudes and behaviours", so the series uses psychological operations, written out, for the whole field, and each owner's term, PSYOP and MISO included, when reporting what that owner found or decided.

## What to remember

- NATO aims psychological operations at approved audiences; US doctrine, as MISO from 2010 and as PSYOP again, at foreign ones.
- The US renaming took three steps: MISO by a memo of 3 December 2010, Army units back to PSYOP from 27 October 2017, the function back by a memo stamped 2 December 2025.
- The Council of Europe defines disinformation, misinformation and malinformation by falseness and intent to harm; the EU's 2020 definitions turn on intent to deceive or gain.
- FIMI (EEAS) and CIB (Meta) describe behaviour, not content: neither requires a false statement.
- NATO's propaganda names no actor; the US definition reserves the word for adversaries.
- Before comparing two reports, check each one's definition: one campaign can be disinformation in one, FIMI in another, CIB in a third.
