<MethodNote />

Most frauds have to invent a reason to pay. Business email compromise (BEC) does not: it waits for a payment that was going to happen anyway, an invoice, a payroll run, the closing of a house sale, and changes one detail, usually the account number. The person asking appears to be someone the payer already trusts, an executive, a supplier, a lawyer or a title company, and the request arrives in the channel where such requests normally arrive. After [Social engineering](/learn/social-engineering-pretexting-vishing-and-baiting), [Scams at scale](/learn/scams-at-scale-why-a-tiny-success-rate-pays) and [scam compounds](/learn/scam-compounds-and-pig-butchering), this part of the series opened by [the map from the small lie to the campaign](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign) turns from the mass lure to the targeted one. After reading, you can name the variants, explain the impersonation techniques, read the official figures within their limits, recognize the Brazilian forms, and say which defences work at which step.

## What the authorities mean by it

The Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) of the United States (US) Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) defines BEC as "a scam targeting businesses or individuals working with suppliers and/or businesses regularly performing wire transfer payments", carried out "by compromising email accounts and other forms of communication such as phone numbers and virtual meeting applications, through social engineering or computer intrusion techniques" ([IC3, 2025 Annual Report, Appendix B, April 2026](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf)). The word email in the name has become a historical label: the same report counts telephone numbers and meeting applications among the channels.

The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) of the US Treasury widened its own definitions in July 2019. It now speaks of email compromise fraud in two forms: business email compromise, which targets the accounts of operational entities, "including commercial, non-profit, non-governmental, or government entities", and email account compromise (EAC), which "Targets personal email accounts belonging to an individual" ([FinCEN, FIN-2019-A005, 16 July 2019](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/advisory/2019-07-16/Updated%20BEC%20Advisory%20FINAL%20508.pdf)). The money does not always move by wire: FinCEN reported BEC schemes inducing payments in convertible virtual currency, automated clearing house (ACH) transfers and gift cards. The US Department of Justice (DOJ) described BEC in 2019 as a scam "also called 'cyber-enabled financial fraud'", which may involve fraudulent check requests, the theft of employee tax records, or no actual account compromise at all ([DOJ, 10 September 2019, read through WebFetch](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/281-arrested-worldwide-coordinated-international-enforcement-operation-targeting-hundreds)).

In MITRE's ATT&CK knowledge base the technique is impersonation: adversaries pose as "a known sender such as an executive, colleague, or third-party vendor", typically after reconnaissance of the victim's identity and organization and after acquiring email domains "to substantiate their false identity" ([MITRE ATT&CK, T1684.001](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1684/001/)).

## The variants

In May 2017 the IC3 described "five main scenarios by which this scam is perpetrated" ([IC3, I-050417-PSA, 4 May 2017](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2017/PSA170504)). Later warnings added payroll diversion and gave real estate a section of its own. Read together, they are a catalogue of the payments a business makes routinely.

### Invoice and vendor fraud

A business with "a longstanding relationship with a supplier is requested to wire funds for an invoice payment to an alternate, fraudulent account", by telephone, fax or a spoofed email; the IC3 lists the nicknames "Bogus Invoice Scheme," "Supplier Swindle," and "Invoice Modification Scheme" ([IC3, 2017](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2017/PSA170504)). FinCEN found that fraudulent vendor and client invoices are "generally affiliated with larger BEC transaction amounts than even the CEO fraud scheme", likely because the amounts expected for goods and services are already high ([FinCEN, 2019](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/advisory/2019-07-16/Updated%20BEC%20Advisory%20FINAL%20508.pdf)). In 2016 the IC3 saw some fraudsters go further and redirect not one remittance but "all incoming invoice payments" ([IC3, 2017](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2017/PSA170504)).

### CEO fraud

In the second scenario the email account of a chief executive officer (CEO), a chief financial officer (CFO) or another senior executive is spoofed or hacked, and a request for a wire goes to the employee who usually processes them, sometimes to "urgently send funds to bank 'X' for reason 'Y.'" ([IC3, 2017](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2017/PSA170504)). FinCEN found that about half of the BEC fraud targeting financial institutions came through emails impersonating the CEO or president ([FinCEN, 2019, note 10](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/advisory/2019-07-16/Updated%20BEC%20Advisory%20FINAL%20508.pdf)). A peer-reviewed study by Asaf Cidon and colleagues at Barracuda Networks, a security vendor, and Columbia University sampled attacks at 50 companies and found the CEO or founder impersonated in about 43% of cases, while the targets were spread across roles: "simply protecting employees in sensitive departments in not sufficient to protect against BEC" ([Cidon et al., USENIX Security 2019](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec19-cidon.pdf); "in not" as printed).

### A compromised employee writing to the vendors

In the third scenario an employee's personal email, used for business too, is hacked and requests for invoice payments go to "multiple vendors identified from this employee's contact list"; the business may learn of it only when a vendor asks about an unpaid invoice ([IC3, 2017](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2017/PSA170504)).

### Attorney impersonation

Callers or writers present themselves "as lawyers or representatives of law firms and claim to be handling confidential or time-sensitive matters"; victims "may be pressured by the fraudster to act quickly or secretly", and the request may come at the end of the business day or week, timed to the close of international banks ([IC3, 2017](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2017/PSA170504)). The European Union Agency for Law Enforcement Cooperation (Europol) described this form in a case it helped investigate: in late December 2021 a Paris real estate developer was contacted by people who "impersonated lawyers, saying they worked for a well-known French accounting company", asked for "a large, urgent and confidential transfer" and obtained almost EUR 38 million in a few days, moved through other European countries, then China and Israel ([Europol, 17 February 2023](https://www.europol.europa.eu/media-press/newsroom/news/franco-israeli-gang-behind-eur-38-million-ceo-fraud-busted)). Europol reported eight arrests in France and Israel, among them the main organizer, and seizures of about EUR 5.5 million. No court finding on the suspects appears in the sources read here.

### Data theft: W-2 forms and personal data

The fifth scenario asks for data rather than money: a request sent from an executive's compromised email to human resources (HR), bookkeeping or auditing for employees' Wage and Tax Statement (W-2) forms or other personally identifiable information (PII); the IC3 says it "first appeared just prior to the 2016 tax season" and that some victims fell for it "even if they were able to successfully identify and avoid the traditional BEC scam" ([IC3, 2017](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2017/PSA170504)). The Internal Revenue Service (IRS) warned in February 2017 that the scam had spread from companies to school districts, tribal organizations and nonprofits, and that the scammers were coupling the W-2 request with an older wire-transfer scheme, a combination "victimizing some organizations twice"; its commissioner called it "one of the most dangerous email phishing scams we've seen in a long time" ([IRS, IR-2017-20, 2 February 2017](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/dangerous-w-2-phishing-scam-evolving-targeting-schools-restaurants-hospitals-tribal-groups-and-others)).

### Payroll diversion

In September 2018 the IC3 described phishing aimed at the online payroll accounts of employees in education, healthcare and commercial airway transportation: with stolen credentials the criminal changes the employee's bank details, adds rules that prevent the employee "from receiving alerts regarding direct deposit changes", and redirects the salary to an account "which is often a prepaid card" ([IC3, I-091818-PSA, 18 September 2018](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2018/PSA180918)).

### Real estate and conveyancing

A house purchase gathers large sums, several parties and many emails in a few weeks. The IC3 says BEC "targets all participants in real estate transactions", and that once inside a participant's mailbox the criminals "monitor the real estate proceeding" and time a request to change payment from check to wire or from one account to another; from 2020 to 2022 victim reports with a real estate nexus rose 27% and their losses 72% ([IC3, I-060923-PSA, 9 June 2023](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2023/PSA230609)). FinCEN's review of 2,013 such incidents in 2020 and 2021 found that "Individual homebuyers suffer disproportionately", that 37% involved impersonation of title and closing entities, and that nearly 88% sent the money first to accounts at US depository institutions ([FinCEN, Financial Trend Analysis, March 2023](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/shared/Financial_Trend_Analysis_BEC_FINAL.pdf)).

### Public bodies

FinCEN counted "Dozens of government organizations" among the targets, including pension, payroll and contracted-services accounts, often through vendor impersonation ([FinCEN, 2019](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/advisory/2019-07-16/Updated%20BEC%20Advisory%20FINAL%20508.pdf)). In 2025 someone posing as an employee of a contractor of the City of Portland, Oregon, changed the contractor's bank details in the city's payment system; the FBI seized $6,748,680 left in the receiving account, and the civil forfeiture complaint's accusations are, the US Attorney's Office notes, only allegations ([DOJ, District of Oregon, 23 May 2025, read through WebFetch](https://www.justice.gov/usao-or/pr/united-states-files-forfeiture-action-recover-67-million-stolen-funds)).

## How the impersonation works

The sender's identity in an email has several layers, and each variant attacks a different one. The defences later in this part map onto the same layers.

### Lookalike domains

The criminal registers a domain that differs from a trusted one by a character or a suffix. In the Apache Corporation case decided by the US Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, the vendor's real domain was "petrofac.com" and "the criminals created 'petrofacltd.com' to send the fraudulent email" ([Apache Corp. v. Great American Insurance Co., No. 15-20499, 5th Cir., 18 October 2016](https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/Opinions/unpub/15/15-20499.0.pdf)). An FBI affidavit filed in the Eastern District of Virginia in 2019 described a domain using "an 'L' instead of an 'I' because the two lowercase characters look similar", and the agent wrote that subjects "hope the misspellings will be overlooked by email recipients" ([affidavit in support of criminal complaint, United States v. Okeke, 4:19-mj-00116, E.D. Va., filed 2 August 2019](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.vaed.450919/gov.uscourts.vaed.450919.5.0_1.pdf)). How such names are built is the subject of [Deceptive URLs](/learn/deceptive-urls).

### Display-name spoofing

Many mail programs show the sender's name and hide the address. Cidon's team noted that "since many email clients do not display the sender email address, some recipients will be deceived even if the attacker uses an unrelated email address" ([Cidon et al., 2019](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec19-cidon.pdf)). The Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) standard for domain-based message authentication says plainly that a well-known name placed in the display name with "an arbitrary address or domain name" is a display name attack, and that "Such attacks, known as display name attacks, are out of scope for DMARC" ([RFC 9989, section 11.4, May 2026](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9989.txt)).

### Spoofing the real address

A forged message can also carry the trusted organization's own domain in the From line. In one of Cidon's anonymized examples the From address is the executive's own and the Reply-To points elsewhere, so the victim's answer reaches the attacker ([Cidon et al., 2019](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec19-cidon.pdf)). In Medidata's dispute with its insurer, the US Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit described spoofed messages that "inaccurately appeared, in all respects, to come from a high-ranking member" of the company ([Medidata Solutions Inc. v. Federal Insurance Co., No. 17-2492, 2d Cir., summary order of 6 July 2018](https://www.insurancejournal.com/app/uploads/2018/07/Medidata-v-Federal-Insurance.pdf)). This is the layer that email authentication can close, when the domain owner publishes a strict policy.

### Compromised mailboxes and thread hijacking

The hardest case is the real mailbox in the wrong hands. In April 2020 the IC3 described phishing kits that "mimic the cloud-based email services", after which criminals "analyze the content of compromised email accounts for evidence of financial transactions" and impersonate the communications between businesses and their vendors or customers; the IC3 counted more than $2.1 billion in actual losses from BEC using two popular cloud services between January 2014 and October 2019 ([IC3, I-040620-PSA, 6 April 2020](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2020/PSA200406)). A message sent from a compromised account passes every authentication check, because it was sent by the account. FinCEN describes criminals who, with knowledge gathered this way, "insert themselves into communications by impersonating a critical player in a business relationship or transaction" ([FinCEN, 2019](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/advisory/2019-07-16/Updated%20BEC%20Advisory%20FINAL%20508.pdf)).

The Okeke affidavit gives a case in detail. Unatrac Holding, the United Kingdom (UK) based export sales office for Caterpillar equipment, reported that its CFO had entered his password on a page imitating the Office 365 login; logs showed the intruder in the account "at least 464 times, mostly from Internet Protocol (IP) addresses in Nigeria" between 6 and 20 April 2018, sending wire requests with invoices built from the company's own logos and templates. Knowing that invoices normally come from outside, the intruder sent them to the CFO's account from an external address and forwarded them to the finance team two minutes later. About 15 payments, nearly $11 million in total, went overseas ([United States v. Okeke, affidavit, 2019](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.vaed.450919/gov.uscourts.vaed.450919.5.0_1.pdf)). An affidavit sets out probable cause, not proof; on 16 February 2021 the US Attorney's Office for the Eastern District of Virginia announced that Obinwanne Okeke had been sentenced to ten years in prison for a scheme that, from about 2015 to 2019, caused some $11 million in known losses, nearly all of it the wire transfers taken from Unatrac ([US Attorney's Office, EDVA, 16 February 2021](https://www.justice.gov/usao-edva/pr/nigerian-national-sentenced-prison-11-million-global-fraud-scheme); [CyberScoop, 17 February 2021](https://cyberscoop.com/nigerian-email-scam-phishing-prison-sentence/)).

### Mailbox rules: hiding the replies

The same affidavit records that the intruder "created or modified email filter rules for the CFO's account on seven occasions", rules that "intercepted legitimate emails to and from employees on the financial team, marked them as read, and moved them to another folder outside the inbox", apparently to hide the replies from the real CFO ([United States v. Okeke, affidavit, 2019](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.vaed.450919/gov.uscourts.vaed.450919.5.0_1.pdf)). MITRE ATT&CK lists the move as a technique: adversaries "may use email rules to hide inbound emails in a compromised user's mailbox", and malicious rules "commonly filter out emails based on key words" such as malware, suspicious, phish and hack in message bodies and subject lines ([MITRE ATT&CK, T1564.008](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1564/008/)). A companion technique uses forwarding rules, which "can allow adversaries to maintain persistent access to victim's emails even after compromised credentials are reset by administrators" ([MITRE ATT&CK, T1114.003](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1114/003/)).

### Beyond email

In November 2026 the Polícia Civil of Rio Grande do Norte, in Brazil, carried out the second phase of an operation against a group <Claim status="alleged" by="by the police">accused of posing as executives to make employees transfer money</Claim>; one suspect allegedly used an external Microsoft Teams profile to pose as the chief executive of a company in Natal and obtained about R$ 940,000, according to the trade site Baguete, citing g1 ([Baguete, 10 November 2026](https://www.baguete.com.br/noticias/policia-deflagra-operacao-contra-golpe-do-falso-ceo)). The IC3 adds voice: "Voice cloning can also be used to request wire payment", and in 2025 businesses "reported losses over $30 million to BEC scams involving AI", artificial intelligence (AI), although "There are multiple BEC tactics, and not all are AI-enabled" ([IC3, 2025 Annual Report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf)). Voice and video impersonation is the subject of [Voice clones and deepfakes in fraud](/learn/voice-clones-and-deepfakes-in-fraud).

## The psychology: authority, urgency and routine

Three levers recur in the official descriptions, and they work together: the request comes from someone with standing, it must be done now, and it looks like the work the employee does every day.

**Authority.** FinCEN wrote that BEC schemes "continue to leverage common typologies of impersonating organization executives" in order "to discourage employees receiving the fraudulent payment instructions from challenging or confirming the order" ([FinCEN, 2019](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/advisory/2019-07-16/Updated%20BEC%20Advisory%20FINAL%20508.pdf)). The lever is the one Robert Cialdini calls authority, examined with its evidence in [The human attack surface](/learn/the-human-attack-surface-why-repetition-works); in BEC its effect is less to persuade than to make checking feel like insubordination.

**Urgency and secrecy.** MITRE notes "manipulative and persuasive language" in subject lines and message bodies, words such as payment, request or urgent, used "to push the victim to act quickly before malicious activity is detected" ([MITRE ATT&CK, T1684.001](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1684/001/)). Two of the anonymized attacks Cidon's team published open with "I need to send a wire transfer ASAP to a vendor" and "are you available for something urgent ?"; the second, an attempt to establish rapport, typically leads to a wire request once the employee replies ([Cidon et al., 2019](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec19-cidon.pdf)). Secrecy removes the colleague who might have asked a question; in the 2021 case Europol described, the accountant was asked for "an urgent and confidential transfer of EUR 300 000" by a caller impersonating his CEO ([Europol, 2023](https://www.europol.europa.eu/media-press/newsroom/news/franco-israeli-gang-behind-eur-38-million-ceo-fraud-busted)).

**Routine.** The IC3 observed that "The fraudsters will use the method most commonly associated with their victim's normal business practices" ([IC3, 2017](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2017/PSA170504)), and FinCEN that a scheme's success "often depends on the criminal's knowledge of their victim's normal business processes, as well as weaknesses in the victim's authorization and authentication protocols" ([FinCEN, 2019](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/advisory/2019-07-16/Updated%20BEC%20Advisory%20FINAL%20508.pdf)). In the Apache case the money paid "legitimate invoices", only to the wrong account ([Apache v. Great American, 2016](https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/Opinions/unpub/15/15-20499.0.pdf)). Cidon's team found that 59.9% of the attacks they sampled carried no phishing link at all: "the attack is simply a plain text email that fools the recipient to commit a wire transfer or send sensitive information" ([Cidon et al., 2019](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec19-cidon.pdf)). Nothing in such a message is technically malicious; the deception is entirely in who seems to be asking.

How much each lever contributes has not been measured on victims. A 2020 review by Cassandra Cross and Rosalie Gillett in the Journal of Financial Crime, found "a large focus on the technical aspects of BEC fraud, to the detriment of the human elements", and <Claim status="reported" by="by Cross and Gillett">"no known research which has explored the non-financial harms of BEC fraud"</Claim> ([Cross and Gillett, 2020, abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eme:jfcpps:jfc-02-2020-0026)). The levers above come from case descriptions and from a vendor's classification of attacks, not from experiments.

## The numbers, and what they can say

### The FBI's annual count

The IC3 recorded 24,768 BEC complaints in 2025 with $3,046,598,558 in reported losses, after 21,442 complaints and $2,770,151,146 in 2024 and 21,489 complaints and $2,946,830,270 in 2023 ([IC3, 2025 Annual Report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf)). The FBI names BEC second among the components of 2025 losses, after investment fraud, though it drew far fewer complaints than phishing and spoofing (191,561) or extortion (89,129): few victims, large sums. Real estate fraud is a separate IC3 crime type, with 12,368 complaints and $275,110,419 in 2025, so the BEC figure does not include all wire fraud around house sales. The report warns that "Some complainants may have filed more than once", that each complaint has only one crime type, and that the BEC count includes cases where a complainant aged 60 or over reports for a business or personally.

### The cumulative "exposed" losses

The IC3's public service announcement (PSA) of September 2024 counted 305,033 domestic and international incidents and $55,499,915,582 in "exposed dollar loss" between October 2013 and December 2023, figures "reported to the FBI IC3, law enforcement and derived from filings with financial institutions" ([IC3, I-091124-PSA, 11 September 2024](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2024/PSA240911)). Victim complaints alone gave 158,436 US victims and $20,089,561,364 in exposed losses. BEC had been reported "in all 50 states and 186 countries, with over 140 countries receiving fraudulent transfers", with banks in the UK and Hong Kong often acting as intermediary stops, followed by China, Mexico and the United Arab Emirates (UAE). The PSA does not define "exposed"; FinCEN's counts, which feed such totals, include attempted thefts: since its 2016 advisory it had received "over 32,000 reports involving almost $9 billion in attempted theft" ([FinCEN, 2019](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/advisory/2019-07-16/Updated%20BEC%20Advisory%20FINAL%20508.pdf)). The cumulative figure is therefore a measure of exposure, not of money finally lost.

### What the banks report to FinCEN

Banks' suspicious activity reports give another view. FinCEN saw BEC reports climb from just under 500 a month (about $110 million in attempted thefts a month) in 2016 to over 1,100 a month (over $300 million) in 2018, and named manufacturing and construction (25% of cases), commercial services (18%) and real estate (16%) as the most targeted sectors ([FinCEN, 2019](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/advisory/2019-07-16/Updated%20BEC%20Advisory%20FINAL%20508.pdf)). For 2020 and 2021 it counted 31,695 BEC filings reflecting roughly $10.8 billion, and noted that its real-estate data are "not a complete representation of all BEC incidents" ([FinCEN, Financial Trend Analysis, 2023](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/shared/Financial_Trend_Analysis_BEC_FINAL.pdf)).

These series overlap, measure different things (complaints, filings, attempts) and stop at different years; none of them can be added to another, and none is a census. Outside the US, no European or Brazilian count of BEC losses comparable to the IC3's was found in the official sources read for this part.

## Cases in court and in company filings

Charges, complaints and affidavits are allegations; guilty pleas, verdicts and sentences are court findings. The cases below are marked accordingly.

- **Two US internet companies, 2013 to 2015.** <Claim status="established" by="by his guilty plea">Evaldas Rimasauskas, a Lithuanian citizen, sent phishing emails impersonating an Asian computer hardware manufacturer that led two US-based internet companies to wire over $120 million to accounts he controlled in Latvia and Cyprus</Claim>; on 19 December 2019 he was sentenced to 60 months in prison and ordered to forfeit about $49.7 million and pay about $26.5 million in restitution ([DOJ, Southern District of New York, 19 December 2019, read through WebFetch](https://www.justice.gov/usao-sdny/pr/lithuanian-man-sentenced-5-years-prison-theft-over-120-million-fraudulent-business)). The DOJ does not name the companies; The Register reported that they were Facebook and Google and that the lookalike domains imitated Quanta, a contract manufacturer the two were "likely doing business with" at the time ([The Register, 20 December 2019](https://www.theregister.com/2019/12/20/facebook_google_hacker_five_years/)).
- **Ubiquiti Networks, 2015.** In a filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), the company reported a fraud involving "employee impersonation and fraudulent requests from an outside entity" targeting its finance department, which resulted in transfers of $46.7 million from a Hong Kong subsidiary; it had recovered $8.1 million and was pursuing the rest ([Ubiquiti Networks, Form 8-K, August 2015, read through WebFetch](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1511737/000157104915006288/t1501817_8k.htm)).
- **Toyota Boshoku, 2019.** The European Union's computer emergency response team, CERT-EU, recorded that a European subsidiary of the Toyota group announced a BEC attack on 14 August 2019 "that resulted in the loss of 4 billion Yen" ([CERT-EU, Memo 191001-1, 1 October 2019](https://cert.europa.eu/publications/threat-intelligence/threat-memo-191001-1/pdf)).
- **Operation reWired, 2019.** The DOJ announced 281 arrests worldwide, 167 in Nigeria and 74 in the US, with arrests also in Turkey, Ghana, France, Italy, Japan, Kenya, Malaysia and the UK, and nearly $3.7 million seized; it described BEC as frequently linked to transnational criminal groups that originated in Nigeria ([DOJ, 10 September 2019, read through WebFetch](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/281-arrested-worldwide-coordinated-international-enforcement-operation-targeting-hundreds)). A year earlier, Operation WireWire had produced 74 arrests, 42 in the US and 29 in Nigeria ([FinCEN, 2019](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/advisory/2019-07-16/Updated%20BEC%20Advisory%20FINAL%20508.pdf)). Arrest figures are not convictions.
- **A launderer for many schemes.** <Claim status="established" by="by his guilty plea">Ramon Olorunwa Abbas, known on Instagram as "Ray Hushpuppi", pleaded guilty to conspiracy to engage in money laundering, including funds from BEC schemes</Claim>, among them about $922,857 induced from a New York law firm; on 7 November 2022 he was sentenced to 135 months ([DOJ, Central District of California, 7 November 2022, read through WebFetch](https://www.justice.gov/usao-cdca/pr/nigerian-man-sentenced-over-11-years-federal-prison-conspiring-launder-tens-millions)). Prosecutors wrote that by his own admission he conspired to launder over $300 million in 18 months, while noting that much of that intended loss never materialized.
- **West African networks, 2025 to 2026.** INTERPOL's fourth Operation Jackal, run from November 2025 to June 2026 with 23 countries from six continents, led to 58 arrests and the identification of 263 suspects; INTERPOL said that <Claim status="attributed" by="by INTERPOL">groups such as the Black Axe "are responsible for a significant share of the world's cyber-enabled financial fraud, typically through romance scams, cryptocurrency and investment scams or business email compromise fraud"</Claim> ([INTERPOL, 25 August 2026](https://www.interpol.int/en/News-and-Events/News/2026/58-arrests-in-global-effort-to-dismantle-West-African-organized-crime-groups); [The Hacker News, August 2026](https://thehackernews.com/2026/08/interpol-operation-jackal-iv-arrests-58.html)). No statement from the people arrested appears in the sources.

The nationalities in these files are varied: a Lithuanian in the Google and Facebook case, a French and Israeli network in Europol's, Nigerian defendants in the DOJ's, Brazilian suspects in the cases below. The DOJ and INTERPOL attribute a large share of BEC to West African groups; no source read here measures the share of BEC losses by the offenders' origin.

## Who bears the loss: courts disagree

When a business pays a fraudster, the next question is whether its insurer, its bank or someone else must cover it, and the courts read similar facts differently.

In **Apache**, an employee in Scotland had received a call asking to change a vendor's bank details, and replied that a formal request on letterhead was needed. A week later a letter arrived by email from the lookalike domain; an employee called the number on what the court calls the "fraudulent letterhead", "instead of, for example, calling an independently-provided telephone contact for the vendor", and payments of £4.3 million (about $7 million) followed. The Fifth Circuit held that the crime policy's computer-fraud cover did not apply, because "the email was merely incidental to the occurrence of the authorized transfer of money", and observed that "had the confirmation call been properly directed" the change would not have been made ([Apache v. Great American, 2016](https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/Opinions/unpub/15/15-20499.0.pdf)). The opinion was not published and is not precedent under the court's rules.

In **Medidata**, the Second Circuit reached the opposite result on a different policy wording: although "Medidata concedes that no hacking occurred", the spoofing "represented a fraudulent entry of data into the computer system", and it affirmed an award of $5,841,787.37 in damages and interest ([Medidata v. Federal Insurance, 2018](https://www.insurancejournal.com/app/uploads/2018/07/Medidata-v-Federal-Insurance.pdf)). Both decisions turn on the policies' words, not on a general rule about BEC.

In Brazil, the Superior Tribunal de Justiça (STJ) ruled in a consumer case, reported in October 2023, that a financial company was responsible when fraudsters who knew a customer's confidential contract details sent her a false boleto, a bank payment slip, for R$ 19,200; the São Paulo court of appeal had blamed the customer for not noticing that the slip named a different bank and beneficiary. For the rapporteur, Justice Nancy Andrighi, "Dados pessoais vinculados a operações e serviços bancários são sigilosos", personal data tied to banking operations are confidential, and storing them so that third parties can learn them "configura falha na prestação do serviço", a failure in the service; when the data used are general and obtainable elsewhere, the court found no causal link ([Consultor Jurídico, 18 October 2023, on REsp 2.077.278](https://www.conjur.com.br/2023-out-18/vazamento-dados-bancarios-falha-prestacao-servico/)).

## Brazil: from the falso boleto to the falso CEO

The boleto is the Brazilian equivalent of the invoice that BEC alters. The Federação Brasileira de Bancos (FEBRABAN) ranked the golpe do falso boleto seventh among the scams most used against bank customers in 2024: criminals "falsificam boletos e colocam seus dados bancários para que recebam o crédito", forge slips with their own bank details ([FEBRABAN, 14 April 2025](https://portal.febraban.org.br/noticia/4279/pt-br/)). According to Cable News Network (CNN) Brasil, FEBRABAN reported 4.2 billion boletos in 2023, about R$ 5.8 trillion, and said that the Plataforma Centralizada de Recebíveis (PCR), the central registry of slips created in 2018, had eliminated the equivalent of R$ 450 million a year in fraud; it warned of malware that alters slips at the moment of printing and advised enrolling in the Débito Direto Autorizado (DDA), which delivers the registered electronic version of every slip issued in the payer's name ([CNN Brasil, 1 April 2024](https://www.cnnbrasil.com.br/economia/financas/nao-imprima-boletos-alerta-febraban-entenda/)). Brazil's incident response team, CERT.br, asks payers to check that the name and company registration number (Cadastro Nacional da Pessoa Jurídica, CNPJ) of the recipient "são do fornecedor que contratei", belong to the supplier actually hired, and to prefer the registered electronic boleto ([CERT.br, Golpes: Evite Fraudes, March 2026](https://cartilha.cert.br/fasciculos/golpes-evite-fraudes/fasciculo-golpes-evite-fraudes.pdf)).

Police reports show the business form. In Goiás, the Polícia Civil arrested a 27-year-old man in September 2025 in an investigation that began when <Claim status="alleged" by="by the Polícia Civil of Goiás">a company was induced to pay a falsified boleto, losing more than R$ 74,000, and one suspect had set up a business structure "destinada exclusivamente a receber valores obtidos por meio de fraudes"</Claim>, meant only to receive fraud proceeds ([Polícia Civil de Goiás, 24 September 2025](https://goias.gov.br/policiacivil/pcgo-deflagra-operacao-cobranca-verdadeira-e-prende-homem-por-aplicar-golpe-do-boleto-falso/)). The Rio Grande do Norte case above was described by investigators, according to Baguete, as Business E-mail Compromise; the fraud surfaced on 8 May 2026, when a second payment was attempted and an internal check showed the profile did not belong to the real executive ([Baguete, 2026](https://www.baguete.com.br/noticias/policia-deflagra-operacao-contra-golpe-do-falso-ceo)).

The Brazilian attorney variant has its own name, the golpe do falso advogado. In May 2026 the Polícia Civil of Bahia arrested four people in São Paulo in Operação Falsa Ordem against a group that, <Claim status="alleged" by="by the Polícia Civil of Bahia">it says, used real information from court cases to contact litigants while posing as lawyers, inducing transfers for the supposed release of court funds, and moved more than R$ 4.2 million</Claim> ([Polícia Civil da Bahia, 27 May 2026](https://www.ba.gov.br/policiacivil/noticias/2026-05/25265/operacao-falsa-ordem-prende-quatro-investigados-por-fraudes-eletronicas)). In São Paulo the same month, a task force of the Polícia Civil and the Ordem dos Advogados do Brasil (OAB-SP), the bar association, served 26 warrants against a group said to have moved about R$ 10 million in six months, in some cases using artificial intelligence "para reproduzir a voz real dos advogados", to reproduce the lawyers' real voices ([Diário do Grande ABC, 19 May 2026](https://www.dgabc.com.br/Noticia/4319978/golpe-do-falso-advogado-operacao-com-oab-sp-revela-esquema-de-reproducao-de-vozes-por-ia)). None of the suspects is named in these sources and no response from them appears. The Pix and boleto ecosystem as a whole is covered in [Brazilian cybercrime](/learn/brazilian-cybercrime-from-boleto-to-pix).

## The technical defences, and their limits

### SPF, DKIM and DMARC

Three standards let a receiver check whether mail that claims a domain really comes from it. The Sender Policy Framework (SPF) lets a domain's administrators "explicitly authorize the hosts that are allowed to use their domain names", so a receiver can check the envelope sender ([RFC 7208, April 2014](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7208.txt)). DomainKeys Identified Mail (DKIM) lets the owner of a signing domain "claim some responsibility for a message" with a cryptographic signature, and "separates the question of the identity of the Signer of the message from the purported author" ([RFC 6376, September 2011](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6376.txt)). Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting, and Conformance (DMARC) ties the two to the address a reader sees: it "permits the owner of an email's Author Domain to enable validation of the domain's use" and to state a handling preference for mail that fails; since May 2026 it is a Standards Track protocol, RFC 9989, which obsoletes RFC 7489 and RFC 9091 ([RFC 9989](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9989.txt)). How the three fit and how to reach enforcement is explained in [Email authentication](/learn/email-authentication-spf-dkim-dmarc).

Mapped onto the layers above, the limits are clear. DMARC with an enforcing policy closes exact-domain spoofing of the domain that publishes it. It does nothing against a lookalike domain, which is a different domain and can pass its own checks; RFC 9989 lists display name attacks and "Authentication of entities other than domains" among the topics out of its scope ([RFC 9989, section 2.4](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9989.txt)); and a compromised mailbox sends genuinely authenticated mail. Cidon's team, writing in 2019, added that "the vast majority of organizations do not enforce email authentication", because it is hard to implement and can block legitimate mail ([Cidon et al., 2019](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec19-cidon.pdf)).

### Protecting the mailbox

For cloud mail the IC3 recommends enabling multi-factor authentication (MFA) for all accounts, prohibiting automatic forwarding to external addresses, adding a banner to messages from outside the organization, disabling legacy protocols such as the Post Office Protocol (POP) and the Internet Message Access Protocol (IMAP) that can be used to circumvent MFA, keeping logs of mailbox login and settings changes "for at least 90 days", alerting on suspicious activity such as foreign logins, and configuring SPF, DKIM and DMARC ([IC3, 2020](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2020/PSA200406)). Phishing that relays a password and a one-time code in real time defeats ordinary MFA; credentials bound to the real site do not, as [Passkeys and phishing-resistant authentication](/learn/passkeys-and-phishing-resistant-authentication) explains. Logs of mailbox rules matter because, MITRE notes, rules can be created through a local client, a web interface or a command line, and organization-wide transport rules can act on all mail an organization receives ([MITRE ATT&CK, T1114.003](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1114/003/)).

### The second channel for payment changes

The defence the authorities name most often is procedural. The IC3's 2024 advice begins: "Use secondary channels and/or two-factor authentication to verify requests for changes in account information" ([IC3, 2024](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2024/PSA240911)); in 2020 it said "Verify all payment changes and transactions in person or via a known telephone number" ([IC3, 2020](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2020/PSA200406)); in 2017 it advised arranging that verification "early in the relationship and outside the e-mail environment to avoid interception by a hacker", being "suspicious of requests for secrecy or pressure to take action quickly", and replying by typing the correct address rather than pressing Reply ([IC3, 2017](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2017/PSA170504)). Apache shows what the word known means: the court contrasted a call to the number on the fraudulent letterhead with one to "the pre-existing contact information Apache would have used in past communications" ([Apache v. Great American, 2016](https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/Opinions/unpub/15/15-20499.0.pdf)). FinCEN frames the same idea as hardening three processes, how an organization will "1) authenticate participants in communications, 2) authorize transactions, and 3) communicate information and changes about transactions" ([FinCEN, 2019](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/advisory/2019-07-16/Updated%20BEC%20Advisory%20FINAL%20508.pdf)). Training has a place, but the randomized field studies discussed in [Social engineering](/learn/social-engineering-pretexting-vishing-and-baiting) found embedded phishing training of little or no measurable benefit; a rule that every change of bank details is confirmed through a number already on file does not depend on anyone noticing anything.

## When the money has gone: the Financial Fraud Kill Chain

Speed is the variable that matters after a fraudulent wire. The IC3's Recovery Asset Team (RAT), established in 2018, works with banks and FBI field offices to freeze funds through the Financial Fraud Kill Chain (FFKC); domestically it can follow "second hop" transfers to further accounts, and internationally it coordinates with FinCEN's Rapid Response Team, FBI legal attaché offices and foreign police ([IC3, 2025 Annual Report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf)). In 2025 it initiated 3,900 FFKC incidents involving $1,163,919,846 in attempted theft and froze $679,013,183, which the IC3 calls a 58% success rate: $507,042,623 across 3,574 domestic incidents and $171,970,560 across 326 international ones. In the past most of these incidents were BEC; in 2025 tech support and account takeover cases rose. The figures measure money frozen; the report does not say how much was finally returned to victims.

The report's own examples show the mechanics: a senior citizen in Missouri closing on a property received a compromised email from the "title company" with wire instructions for over $1.3 million, and the RAT froze the receiving account, which it found had also received money from other victims; a month later a $6 million payment by a city government office in Oregon went to the same account, and the earlier freeze let the banks stop and recall it ([IC3, 2025 Annual Report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf)). FinCEN's review of real-estate BEC gives the outcomes banks reported: full recovery in 22.21% of incidents, partial in 14.51%, none in 20.37%, payments blocked or declined in 12.47%, and no clear information in 30.45% ([FinCEN, Financial Trend Analysis, 2023](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/shared/Financial_Trend_Analysis_BEC_FINAL.pdf)).

The IC3 gives the same instructions in its 2024 warning and its 2025 report: "If you discover a fraudulent transfer, time is of the essence"; contact the bank at once to request a recall of the funds and any indemnification documents, and file a complaint at ic3.gov "Regardless of the amount lost" ([IC3, 2025 Annual Report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf)). In the Portland case the District of Oregon's asset recovery chief said the seizure was possible because the crime was reported, and advised victims of similar scams: "do not delay in contacting law enforcement" ([DOJ, District of Oregon, 2025](https://www.justice.gov/usao-or/pr/united-states-files-forfeiture-action-recover-67-million-stolen-funds)). For Pix transfers in Brazil, the refund mechanism and its results are covered in [Scams at scale](/learn/scams-at-scale-why-a-tiny-success-rate-pays).

## What to remember

- BEC redirects a payment the victim already intended to make, or extracts employee data, by impersonating an executive, supplier, lawyer or title company; the IC3 now counts phone and meeting applications among its channels.
- The variants the FBI and FinCEN describe: invoice and vendor fraud, CEO fraud, compromised employee accounts, attorney impersonation, W-2 and PII theft, payroll diversion, real-estate fraud, and attacks on public bodies.
- Lookalike domains, display names, spoofed addresses and compromised mailboxes are different layers; mailbox rules that hide replies are a documented step in court records.
- In 2025 the IC3 recorded 24,768 BEC complaints and $3.05 billion in losses; its $55.5 billion cumulative figure is an exposure measure that combines complaints and bank filings.
- DMARC stops exact-domain spoofing only when enforced, and is out of scope for display names and lookalikes; a compromised mailbox passes all three checks.
- The decisive defence is procedural: confirm every change of payment details through a channel known in advance, and after a fraudulent wire call the bank and file with IC3 at once.
- In Brazil the same pattern appears as the falso boleto, the falso fornecedor, the falso CEO and the falso advogado.
