Above the network, redundancy becomes a decision taken many times a second by software that cannot see the servers it chooses between. An application delivery controller (ADC) learns which pool members are alive by probing them; global server load balancing (GSLB) learns which sites are alive the same way and passes its verdict to clients through the Domain Name System (DNS), whose caches decide when the clients hear it. Every one of those steps can be wrong in two directions, and the operators' own reports show both: probes that removed healthy capacity and probes that passed a fault straight through. This part of the series builds on [how BIG-IP health monitors decide up or down](/learn/ltm-health-monitors), [GSLB in two tiers](/learn/gslb-two-tier-pool-then-member), [load balancing: what actually decides where a request goes](/learn/load-balancing-what-actually-decides-where-a-request-goes) and [anycast](/learn/anycast), and does not repeat them. It compares the documented defaults across implementations, turns them into times, and sets DNS-based failover against anycast with the measurements behind each. After reading, you can compute how long a member, a pool or a site takes to leave and to return to service, choose probe thresholds knowing what each costs, and decide what your design should do when every probe fails at once. The redundancy of the ADC pair itself, its state and its split brain, is [the previous part](/learn/stateful-clusters-active-standby-active-active-and-split-brain).

## Three layers of redundancy in application delivery

| Layer | What fails | What notices | What reacts | Where the time goes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Member | A server or a process on it | Active probes, passive observation of real traffic | The ADC stops sending new connections | Probe interval × failures required |
| Pool or tier | Too many members at once | A minimum-members rule or a health share | Traffic moves to a backup tier | The same, plus the rule |
| Site or region | A data centre, a cloud region, a provider | Probes from outside the site | The DNS answer changes, or an anycast route is withdrawn | Probes, then caches or routing convergence |

The [hub's timer table](/learn/redundant-architectures-how-networks-survive-failure) gives the DNS layer's time as lasting until cached answers expire; this part works out what that means in seconds.

## The probe is a binary classifier, and it errs both ways

A health check answers a yes-or-no question about a target, and like any test it has two kinds of error. A false positive marks a healthy target down and removes capacity; a false negative keeps a broken target in service. Making a check deeper reduces the second error and raises the first, a trade the [load balancing article](/learn/load-balancing-what-actually-decides-where-a-request-goes) describes as a health check that "turned a partial failure into a total one". The [health check](/glossary/health-check) glossary entry collects the vendors' definitions; the rest of this section is about the numbers.

Turning the defaults into time to leave service, roughly the interval times the failures required: a silent BIG-IP member is marked down at 16 seconds; HAProxy takes 2 × 3 = 6 seconds and comes back after 2 × 2 = 4; an Application Load Balancer target takes 30 × 2 = 60 seconds to leave and 30 × 5 = 150 seconds to return; Google Cloud takes 5 × 2 = 10 seconds each way; a Kubernetes readiness probe takes 10 × 3 = 30 seconds; Envoy's third ejection of the same host lasts 30 × 3 = 90 seconds. The return path matters as much as the exit: a member that comes back after one success, as BIG-IP's default and NGINX's active check do, can flap, and the [flapping](/glossary/flapping) entry and the BIG-IP article's Time Until Up describe the brake.

### The price of a threshold, worked

Requiring k consecutive failures before marking a target down delays detection by (k − 1) probe intervals and cuts false mark-downs sharply. An illustrative model: if each probe fails spuriously, independently, with probability p = 0.001, the expected number of false mark-downs per member is roughly (probes per day) × p^k.

| Probe interval | k = 1 | k = 2 | k = 3 | Detection delay added by k = 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 s (HAProxy) | 43 a day | 15.8 a year | 0.016 a year | 4 s |
| 5 s | 17 a day | 6.3 a year | 0.006 a year | 10 s |
| 30 s | 2.9 a day | 1.05 a year | 0.001 a year | 60 s |

The model's weakness is its own lesson. Spurious failures are rarely independent: when a shared dependency, a probe source or the network in front of the targets misbehaves, every probe of every member fails together, k consecutive failures arrive at once, and the threshold protects nothing. That is the failure in the reports below, and it is why most implementations have a rule for the case where everything looks dead.

### When every probe says no

| Implementation | What happens when all targets are unhealthy | Source |
|---|---|---|
| AWS Application and Network Load Balancers | "the load balancer fails open", sending traffic to all targets regardless of health | [AWS, ALB health checks](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/elasticloadbalancing/latest/application/target-group-health-checks.html); [AWS, NLB health checks](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/elasticloadbalancing/latest/network/target-group-health-checks.html) |
| Amazon Route 53 | "when all records are considered unhealthy, the Route 53 algorithm reverts to considering all records healthy" | [AWS, How Amazon Route 53 averts failover problems](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/Route53/latest/DeveloperGuide/dns-failover-problems.html) |
| Azure Traffic Manager | A "best effort" attempt that answers "as if all the Degraded status endpoints actually are in an online state" | [Microsoft Learn, Traffic Manager endpoint monitoring](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/traffic-manager/traffic-manager-monitoring) |
| Azure Front Door | Routes "in a round robin distribution across all of them" | [Microsoft Learn, Azure Front Door health probes](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/frontdoor/health-probes) |
| Envoy | Below the panic threshold, 50% by default, traffic goes to all hosts, or to none if so configured | [Envoy, Panic threshold](https://www.envoyproxy.io/docs/envoy/latest/intro/arch_overview/upstream/load_balancing/panic_threshold) |
| NGINX | A group with a single server: `max_fails` and `fail_timeout` are ignored and "such a server will never be considered unavailable" | [NGINX, ngx_http_upstream_module](https://nginx.org/en/docs/http/ngx_http_upstream_module.html) |

The documents give the reasoning. Route 53's: "An application can respond to users but still fail health checks, so this provides some protection against misconfiguration" ([AWS, How Amazon Route 53 averts failover problems](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/Route53/latest/DeveloperGuide/dns-failover-problems.html)). Envoy's: the threshold exists "to avoid a situation in which host failures cascade throughout the cluster as load increases", while sending no traffic at all suits a service "observed to fail in an all-or-nothing pattern" ([Envoy, Panic threshold](https://www.envoyproxy.io/docs/envoy/latest/intro/arch_overview/upstream/load_balancing/panic_threshold)). Microsoft adds the warning that comes with failing open: if health checks are misconfigured, "it might appear from the traffic routing as though Traffic Manager is working properly. However, in this case, endpoint failover cannot happen", so the profile must be checked for an Online status, not a Degraded one ([Microsoft Learn, Traffic Manager endpoint monitoring](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/traffic-manager/traffic-manager-monitoring)). The general idea is the [fail open versus fail closed](/glossary/fail-open-vs-fail-closed) choice, applied to availability rather than security.

### Where the probe comes from

A probe says what one vantage point saw. Google Cloud warns that without firewall rules admitting its probers, "the load balancer considers your backends to be unhealthy", and that each backend receives probes from between five and ten probers at the configured interval, so a 5-second check arrives one to two times a second ([Google Cloud, Health checks overview](https://cloud.google.com/load-balancing/docs/health-check-concepts)). Route 53 aggregates checkers around the world and calls an endpoint healthy when "more than 18% of health checkers" say so, a value "chosen to make sure that health checkers in multiple regions consider the endpoint healthy" ([AWS, How Amazon Route 53 determines whether a health check is healthy](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/Route53/latest/DeveloperGuide/dns-failover-determining-health-of-endpoints.html)). Its documentation illustrates the partition case with Brazil: during a partition affecting South America, the Route 53 servers in São Paulo "might have good access to the health check endpoints in the South America (São Paulo) AWS Region, but poor access to endpoints elsewhere", while US East (Ohio) concludes that the São Paulo records are unhealthy ([AWS, How Amazon Route 53 averts failover problems](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/Route53/latest/DeveloperGuide/dns-failover-problems.html)). BIG-IP 21.1 lets the in-TMM monitors take their source from a SNAT pool, for members whose networks do not route back to the self IPs, as [the monitors article](/learn/ltm-health-monitors) explains.

### Case: health checks that removed healthy capacity

AWS's summary of its October 2025 event in Northern Virginia describes a second phase, after the DNS failure that the hub tells: between 5:30 AM and 2:09 PM Pacific Daylight Time (PDT), Network Load Balancers saw increased connection errors ([AWS, Summary of the Amazon DynamoDB Service Disruption in the Northern Virginia (US-EAST-1) Region, October 2025](https://aws.amazon.com/message/101925/)). The health checking subsystem was bringing new instances into service before their network state had propagated, so "health checks would fail even though the underlying NLB node and backend targets were healthy"; the results alternated, nodes and targets were "removed from DNS, only to be returned to service when the next health check succeeded"; the extra load degraded the health check subsystem, "triggering automatic AZ DNS failover to occur" (AZ is an Availability Zone), and multi-zone load balancers lost capacity. Engineers disabled automatic health check failover at 9:36 AM, which "resolved the increased connection errors", and AWS is adding "a velocity control mechanism to limit the capacity a single NLB can remove when health check failures cause AZ failover" (same report). Computed from the report's times, the phase lasted 8 hours 39 minutes, monitoring detected it 1 hour 22 minutes in, and automatic failover was switched off 4 hours 6 minutes in. The probes were wrong in the false-positive direction, correlated across targets, and connected to an automatic action with no limit on how much it could remove.

Meta's account of its 4 October 2021 outage contains the same mechanism at the scale of a site. Its DNS servers "disable those BGP advertisements if they themselves can not speak to our data centers, since this is an indication of an unhealthy network connection"; when the backbone went down, every location declared itself unhealthy, and "our DNS servers became unreachable even though they were still operational" ([Meta Engineering, More details about the October 4 outage, 5 October 2021](https://engineering.fb.com/2021/10/05/networking-traffic/outage-details/)). A local health check that is right for one node can be wrong for all of them at once.

### Case: health checks that let a fault through

Microsoft's engineering account of the Azure Front Door incident of 29 October 2025 shows the opposite error in the system that guards configuration rollouts. A sequence of configuration changes produced incompatible metadata, and "Because the failure mode in the data-plane was asynchronous, the health checks validations embedded in our protection systems were all passed during the rollout"; the metadata "successfully propagated globally through a staged rollout and also updated the "last known good" (LKG) snapshot", and the later crash "impacted connectivity and DNS resolutions for all applications onboarded to our platform" ([Microsoft, Azure Networking Blog, Azure Front Door: Implementing lessons learned following October outages, 18 December 2025](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/azurenetworkingblog/azure-front-door-implementing-lessons-learned-following-october-outages/4479416)). Among the repairs Microsoft lists: forced synchronous configuration processing, stages with extended bake time, each stage "health-gated", and workers that keep serving "using the last known good configuration" when the master process crashes (same post). A health check that runs before the fault can appear is a false negative by construction.

## Pools, priority groups and backup tiers

A pool can hold members that should take traffic only when the preferred ones are not enough. The implementations agree on the idea and differ in the trigger.

- **F5 BIG-IP priority group activation.** Each member has a priority group, and `min-active-members` is "the minimum number of members that must be up for traffic to be confined to a priority group", where an active member is one that "is up (not marked down) and is handling fewer connections than its connection limit" ([F5, TMSH Reference v17.0, ltm pool](https://clouddocs.f5.com/cli/tmsh-reference/latest/modules/ltm/ltm_pool.html)). F5's own training lab gives two primary members priority 10, leaves a disaster-recovery member at the default, and sets activation to "less than 2 Available Members", so disabling one primary activates the backup ([F5 clouddocs, F5 certification class 8, Load Balancing lab](https://clouddocs.f5.com/training/community/f5cert/html/class8/module07/lab1.html)). The [request distribution article](/learn/bigip-ltm-request-distribution) and the [LTM simulator](/tools/bigip-ltm-lb-simulator) show the effect.
- **HAProxy and NGINX backup servers.** A `backup` server in HAProxy "is only used in load balancing when all other non-backup servers are unavailable", and only the first one unless `allbackups` is set; requests with a persistence cookie for the backup are still served ([HAProxy 3.2 Configuration Manual](https://docs.haproxy.org/3.2/configuration.html)). NGINX's `backup` server "will be passed requests when the primary servers are unavailable" ([NGINX, ngx_http_upstream_module](https://nginx.org/en/docs/http/ngx_http_upstream_module.html)).
- **Envoy priority levels.** Traffic spills to the next priority in proportion to health, with an overprovisioning factor of 1.4 by default: "level 0 endpoints will continue to receive all traffic until less than ~71.4% of them are healthy" ([Envoy, Priority levels](https://www.envoyproxy.io/docs/envoy/latest/intro/arch_overview/upstream/load_balancing/priority)).
- **DNS tiers.** Route 53's active-passive configuration returns the secondary only "If all the primary resources are unhealthy"; with weighted records, zero-weight records are used only when every non-zero record is unhealthy, which "can make your web application or website unreliable if the last healthy resource, such as a web server, can't handle all the traffic" ([AWS, Active-active and active-passive failover](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/Route53/latest/DeveloperGuide/dns-failover-types.html)). Traffic Manager's priority method always returns "The first available endpoint on the list" ([Microsoft Learn, Traffic Manager endpoint monitoring](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/traffic-manager/traffic-manager-monitoring)); BIG-IP DNS's Global Availability does the same at the wide IP, as [GSLB in two tiers](/learn/gslb-two-tier-pool-then-member) shows.

Envoy's rule, worked: a level's share is min(100, 1.4 × healthy percentage).

| Healthy share of priority 0 | Traffic kept on priority 0 | Spilled to priority 1 |
|---|---|---|
| 100% | 100% | 0% |
| 80% | 100% (1.4 × 80 = 112, capped) | 0% |
| 72% | 100% | 0% |
| 71% | 99% | 1% |
| 50% | 70% | 30% |
| 20% | 28% | 72% |

All four designs share one assumption, which Envoy states outright: "each priority level must be able to handle (100% * overprovision factor) of the traffic", and "If P=0 has 10 hosts but P=1 only has 2 hosts, that assumption probably will not hold" ([Envoy, Priority levels](https://www.envoyproxy.io/docs/envoy/latest/intro/arch_overview/upstream/load_balancing/priority)). A backup tier sized for comfort rather than for the whole load turns a failover into an overload, which is the survivor arithmetic of [the previous part](/learn/stateful-clusters-active-standby-active-active-and-split-brain) one layer up.

## Leaving and coming back: draining and slow start

Taking a member out of service is itself a failure, chosen and timed, and the implementations treat the connections already on it in different ways.

| Implementation | Gentle exit | Hard exit | What happens to live connections when the monitor marks a member down |
|---|---|---|---|
| F5 BIG-IP | Session disabled: clients with sessions "may create new connections", others may not; "used to gently drain connections" | `user-down` "forces the pool member offline, overriding monitors" | `service-down-action`: none by default; drop, reset or reselect if set ([F5, TMSH Reference v17.0, ltm pool](https://clouddocs.f5.com/cli/tmsh-reference/latest/modules/ltm/ltm_pool.html)) |
| HAProxy | `drain`: no new connections "other than those that are accepted via persistence" | `maint`: none at all, health checks stopped | `on-marked-down shutdown-sessions` closes them; "Actions are disabled by default" ([HAProxy 3.2 Configuration Manual](https://docs.haproxy.org/3.2/configuration.html)) |
| NGINX | `drain`: "only requests bound to the server will be proxied to it" | `down` | Not stated in the module documentation ([NGINX, ngx_http_upstream_module](https://nginx.org/en/docs/http/ngx_http_upstream_module.html)) |
| AWS Elastic Load Balancing | Deregistration delay, 300 seconds by default, the target in the draining state | Delay of 0 | NLB "sends a TCP RST for packets received on the client connections associated with the target, unless the unhealthy target triggers the load balancer to fail open" ([AWS, Edit target group attributes](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/elasticloadbalancing/latest/application/edit-target-group-attributes.html); [AWS, NLB health checks](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/elasticloadbalancing/latest/network/target-group-health-checks.html)) |
| Google Cloud | Connection draining timeout from 0 to 3,600 seconds; 0 "disables connection draining" | Timeout of 0 | "Unhealthy backends are not eligible to receive new connections", and existing connections stay open until a timeout or dropped traffic ([Google Cloud, Enabling connection draining](https://cloud.google.com/load-balancing/docs/enabling-connection-draining); [Health checks overview](https://cloud.google.com/load-balancing/docs/health-check-concepts)) |
| Kubernetes | Termination grace period, "Defaults to 30 seconds" | Grace period 0 | A failed readiness probe stops traffic to the Pod ([Kubernetes API reference, Pod v1](https://kubernetes.io/docs/reference/kubernetes-api/workload-resources/pod-v1/)) |

The details matter at the edge of each timer. AWS warns that if a target "terminates the connection before the deregistration delay elapses, the client receives a 500-level error response" ([AWS, Edit target group attributes](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/elasticloadbalancing/latest/application/edit-target-group-attributes.html)). Google Cloud's passthrough load balancers remove the connection tracking entry when the drain ends, after which the next packet goes to another backend that "has no record of an existing connection, so it might send a TCP reset (RST) packet"; its proxies close remaining connections within the 10-minute default idle timeout ([Google Cloud, Enabling connection draining](https://cloud.google.com/load-balancing/docs/enabling-connection-draining)). Whether a drain is long enough depends on how long connections last. An illustrative model with exponential durations: after a 300-second drain, 0.005% of connections with a 30-second mean are still open, but 60.7% of those with a 10-minute mean and 92% of those with a one-hour mean; long-lived streams need the application to reconnect, whatever the timer says.

Coming back needs the opposite care, because a cold member that receives a full share at once can fail its own checks and flap, as the [slow start and connection draining](/glossary/slow-start) entry explains. BIG-IP's `slow-ramp-time`, 10 seconds by default, gives a member that has been up for 30 seconds of a 60-second ramp "approximately half the amount of new traffic" and at 45 seconds "approximately three quarters" ([F5, TMSH Reference v17.0, ltm pool](https://clouddocs.f5.com/cli/tmsh-reference/latest/modules/ltm/ltm_pool.html)). HAProxy's `slowstart` grows weight and connection limits "linearly from 0 to 100%" and "never applies when HAProxy starts" ([HAProxy 3.2 Configuration Manual](https://docs.haproxy.org/3.2/configuration.html)). The Application Load Balancer's slow start mode ramps "linearly" too, but newly registered targets enter it "only when there is at least one healthy target that is not in slow start mode" ([AWS, Edit target group attributes](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/elasticloadbalancing/latest/application/edit-target-group-attributes.html)), and NGINX's `slow_start` defaults to zero, "i.e. slow start is disabled" ([NGINX, ngx_http_upstream_module](https://nginx.org/en/docs/http/ngx_http_upstream_module.html)). At the site level, BIG-IP DNS's `manual-resume` keeps a pool member that went down "disabled indefinitely until you manually re-enable it" ([F5, TMSH Reference v17.0, gtm pool a](https://clouddocs.f5.com/cli/tmsh-reference/latest/modules/gtm/gtm_pool_a.html)).

## Site failover by DNS: what the time to live really buys

DNS-based GSLB moves clients between sites by changing the answer to a query. The [two-tier article](/learn/gslb-two-tier-pool-then-member) explains the decision; the redundancy question is how long the change takes to reach clients. The answer has four terms: the probe's detection time, the time to change the answer, the time to live (TTL) of the old answer in every cache that holds it, and the behaviour of the caches that do not respect it.

### The rules, from the RFCs

RFC 1035 defines the TTL as the interval "that the resource record may be cached before the source of the information should again be consulted" ([RFC 1035, November 1987, section 3.2.1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc1035.txt)); RFC 2181 sets its range from 0 to 2,147,483,647 seconds ([RFC 2181, July 1997, section 8](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2181.txt)). Two later rules change what happens around a failure:

- **Negative answers are cached too.** When a name or record does not exist, the TTL of the negative answer is taken from the zone's start of authority (SOA) record, as the smaller of two values, "from the minimum of the MINIMUM field of the SOA record and the TTL of the SOA itself" ([RFC 2308, March 1998, section 5](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2308.txt)). A GSLB that answers NXDOMAIN during a failure, as Traffic Manager does for a disabled profile ([Microsoft Learn, Traffic Manager endpoint monitoring](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/traffic-manager/traffic-manager-monitoring)), has that answer cached for the zone's negative TTL.
- **Stale answers may be served.** RFC 8767 of March 2020 lets a resolver that cannot reach the authoritative servers answer from expired data, setting the TTL of such records to "a value greater than 0, with a RECOMMENDED value of 30 seconds", and suggests keeping expired records for "between 1 and 3 days"; the same RFC amends the definition of the TTL to recommend a cap of 604,800 seconds, 7 days, instead of the 68 years that RFC 2181 allows, which "reflects the current practice of major modern DNS resolvers" ([RFC 8767, March 2020, sections 4 and 5](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8767.txt)). If the GSLB's own name servers fail, as Akamai's DNS component did for up to an hour on 22 July 2021 after "a software configuration update triggered a bug" ([Akamai, Akamai Summarizes Service Disruption (RESOLVED), 22 July 2021](https://www.akamai.com/blog/news/akamai-summarizes-service-disruption-resolved)), resolvers that serve stale keep clients going to the last answered site, healthy or not.

### The time budget, worked

Microsoft documents a complete timeline for Traffic Manager with an HTTP probe every 30 seconds, a 10-second timeout, 3 tolerated failures and a 30-second TTL: a failed check, "three more times, at 30-second intervals", "After a fourth consecutive failure" the endpoint is Degraded, the name servers stop returning it, and "Clients continue to use the endpoint until the DNS cache expires" ([Microsoft Learn, Traffic Manager endpoint monitoring](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/traffic-manager/traffic-manager-monitoring)). Worked from those numbers: with the last good probe at time 0, the failing probes run at 30, 60, 90 and 120 seconds and the fourth failure is recorded at 130 seconds, so the endpoint leaves the answers between 100 and 130 seconds after the fault, and the last resolver that respects the TTL lets go of it by 160 seconds.

How fast the clients follow depends on the TTL. If the resolvers' cached copies were taken at evenly spread moments, the share still holding the old answer t seconds after the change is 1 − t/TTL for those that respect it:

| TTL | After 10 s | After 30 s | After 60 s | After 150 s | After 300 s |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 30 s | 66.7% | 0% | 0% | 0% | 0% |
| 300 s | 96.7% | 90.0% | 80.0% | 50.0% | 0% |
| 3,600 s | 99.7% | 99.2% | 98.3% | 95.8% | 91.7% |

BIG-IP DNS returns a pool's answers with a TTL of 30 seconds unless changed ([F5, TMSH Reference v17.0, gtm pool a](https://clouddocs.f5.com/cli/tmsh-reference/latest/modules/gtm/gtm_pool_a.html)), and its wide IP persistence, if enabled, keeps a resolver on the same answer for `ttl-persistence`, 3,600 seconds by default ([F5, TMSH Reference v17.0, gtm wideip a](https://clouddocs.f5.com/cli/tmsh-reference/latest/modules/gtm/gtm_wideip_a.html)).

### What resolvers and clients actually do

The table assumes every cache respects the TTL; the measurements say most do and some do not, by a long way. Pang and others, studying the logs of three large sporting events with worldwide audiences held in 2003 and a day of requests at a large content delivery network (CDN), found that "a significant fraction does not (up to 47% of clients and LDNSes collectively, and 14% of LDNSes in our measurements)", that "those that violate TTLs do so by a large amount, in excess of 2 hours", and that 75 to 85% of violating clients did so by more than two hours; their summary is that "DNS is at best a coarse-grained mechanism, and poorly suited for applications, such as route control, which require quick response to link failures" ([Pang and others, On the Responsiveness of DNS-based Network Control, IMC 2004](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/imc/2004/papers/p21-pang.pdf); [abstract as published by Carnegie Mellon University](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~srini/papers/multihoming/2004-Pang-imc-route/)). Moura and others, measuring from about 15,000 RIPE Atlas vantage points fifteen years later, found that "most resolvers trust their cache up to the 1-hour TTL", that after both records should have expired "305-390 VPs (about 2.25% of the total) continue with the old resolver" (the authors' "sticky resolvers"), that "many recursive resolvers have minimum caching times of tens of seconds", and recommended that "those using CDNs or load balancers may require short TTLs (5 or 15 minutes), but most others should prefer longer TTLs (a few hours)" ([Moura, Heidemann, Schmidt and Hardaker, Cache Me If You Can: Effects of DNS Time-to-Live, IMC 2019](https://ant.isi.edu/%7ejohnh/PAPERS/Moura19b.pdf)). Microsoft's own architecture guidance gives the practical reading: Traffic Manager "can't fail over as quickly as Azure Front Door. DNS caching and systems that ignore DNS time-to-live (TTL) values often cause this delay" ([Microsoft Learn, Load-balancing options, updated 21 October 2026](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/guide/technology-choices/load-balancing-overview)).

The GSLB also decides from the wrong address. RFC 7871 starts from the observation that most queries come through recursive resolvers, so "the source address is that of the Recursive Resolver rather than of the query originator", and defines the Extension Mechanisms for DNS (EDNS) Client Subnet option, ECS, to carry the client's network ([RFC 7871, May 2016, section 1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7871.txt)); BIG-IP DNS can prefer it for topology decisions and falls back to the source address when it is absent ([F5, TMSH Reference v17.0, gtm wideip a](https://clouddocs.f5.com/cli/tmsh-reference/latest/modules/gtm/gtm_wideip_a.html)). The [ECS](/glossary/ecs) entry and the [resolver article](/learn/choosing-a-public-dns-resolver) cover the privacy side.

Clients can shorten the wait themselves when the answer offers more than one address. Happy Eyeballs, RFC 8305, starts the next connection attempt after a "Connection Attempt Delay" for which "One recommended value for a default delay is 250 milliseconds" ([RFC 8305, December 2017, section 5](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8305.txt)), so a client given two addresses moves past a dead one in a fraction of a second without waiting for any TTL; the [Happy Eyeballs](/glossary/happy-eyeballs) entry has the details. Protocols that use service (SRV) records get the priority tiers of a pool in the DNS itself: "A client MUST attempt to contact the target host with the lowest-numbered priority it can reach" ([RFC 2782, February 2000](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2782.txt)).

## Anycast instead of DNS

Anycast moves the failover decision from the DNS to routing: every site announces the same prefix, and a failed site withdraws it. The [anycast article](/learn/anycast) explains the mechanism and Best Current Practice (BCP) 126's rules for coupling the route to the service's health; Google's Maglev load balancers do it per machine, where "the controller decides whether to announce or withdraw all the VIPs via BGP" from the forwarder's health ([Eisenbud and others, Maglev, NSDI 2016, section 2.2](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/nsdi16/nsdi16-paper-eisenbud.pdf)). Three measurements and three reports bound what it buys.

**Speed is routing convergence, not a TTL.** Labovitz and others, injecting faults at Internet exchange points over two years, found that "The delay in Internet inter-domain path failovers averaged three minutes", with some oscillations "lasting up to fifteen minutes", and that "forty percent of Tdown events", route withdrawals, "oscillated for more than three minutes" ([Labovitz, Ahuja, Bose and Jahanian, Delayed Internet Routing Convergence, SIGCOMM 2000](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2000/conf/paper/sigcomm2000-5-2.pdf)). The figures describe the Internet of 1998 to 2000 and are the published baseline, not a current measurement; they show that a withdrawal is not instantaneous either. [Part 6](/learn/layer-3-redundancy-ecmp-bfd-and-fast-reroute) covers what has been done to BGP convergence since.

**Stability is good for most and poor for a few.** Wei and Heidemann measured more than 9,000 vantage points against 11 anycast services and found that "about 1% of VPs are anycast unstable", some flipping between sites "in 10 seconds", with per-packet load balancing a likely cause ([Wei and Heidemann, Does Anycast Hang up on You?, IEEE Traffic Monitoring and Analysis conference, Dublin, July 2017](https://ant.isi.edu/%7ejohnh/PAPERS/Wei17b.pdf)).

**The withdrawal mechanism is itself a single point.** Cloudflare's report on 14 July 2025 describes a configuration error, dormant since 6 June, that reduced the service topology of its 1.1.1.1 resolver "from all locations down to a single, offline location", with "the global and immediate withdrawal of all 1.1.1.1 prefixes"; the resolver was unavailable from 21:52 to 22:54 UTC, 62 minutes, and reverting at 22:20 restored only about 77% of traffic because "approximately 23% of the fleet of edge servers had been automatically reconfigured to remove required IP bindings" ([Cloudflare, Cloudflare 1.1.1.1 incident on July 14, 2025](https://blog.cloudflare.com/cloudflare-1-1-1-1-incident-on-july-14-2025/)). The same report notes that most DNS-over-HTTPS users reached the resolver through a domain name that "uses a different set of IP addresses" and were mostly unaffected. Meta's 2021 withdrawal, above, is the health-check version of the same failure.

A Brazilian example shows anycast as a national design. NIC.br, which publishes the .br zone, told the GTER33 meeting on 4 May 2012 that it had put 17 more anycast copies of four of its name server letters into production, 15 of them in Brazil, bringing the .br service to about 60 servers, and that "a estabilidade, resiliência e principalmente independência de conectividade se encontra distribuída pelo país" (stability, resilience and above all independence of connectivity are spread across the country), raising survival of double failures of the domestic network and the international submarine cables ([NIC.br, Registro.br Updates, GTER33, 4 May 2012](https://ftp.registro.br/pub/gter/gter33/02-AtualizacaoDns.br.pdf)). The same talk lists the anomalies anycast meets in practice, among them networks that receive the prefix at an exchange point but do not announce their own customers there, which leaves the anycast copy with no route to return answers through the local exchange, and routing preferences under which "a consulta vem pelo IX, mas a resposta não" (the query arrives through the exchange, but the reply does not). By 2019 NIC.br described an anycast network of six autonomous systems, 80 DNS servers of which 30 are anycast, about 5 billion answers a day, and installed capacity "2 ordens de grandeza maior que o tráfego regular" (two orders of magnitude above regular traffic) ([NIC.br, Registro.br 30 anos, IX regional, June 2019](https://minhaagenda.cursoseventos.nic.br/files/apresentacao/arquivo/604/registrobr-30anos-ixregional.pdf)).

| | DNS-based site failover | Anycast |
|---|---|---|
| Who decides | The GSLB, from its probes | Each site, from its own health, through BGP |
| Time to move clients | Detection plus TTL, plus a tail of resolvers that ignore it | Detection plus routing convergence |
| Granularity | Per resolver, unless ECS is used | Per network path; per packet if a network balances per packet |
| Effect on established connections | None until the client resolves again | Connections may move to a site that does not know them |
| Characteristic failure | Caches outliving the change; the GSLB's own name servers | A withdrawal that reaches every site at once |
| Sources | [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/traffic-manager/traffic-manager-monitoring); [Pang 2004](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/imc/2004/papers/p21-pang.pdf); [Moura 2019](https://ant.isi.edu/%7ejohnh/PAPERS/Moura19b.pdf) | [Labovitz 2000](https://conferences.sigcomm.org/sigcomm/2000/conf/paper/sigcomm2000-5-2.pdf); [Wei 2017](https://ant.isi.edu/%7ejohnh/PAPERS/Wei17b.pdf); [Cloudflare 2025](https://blog.cloudflare.com/cloudflare-1-1-1-1-incident-on-july-14-2025/) |

The two are often combined: Maglev's paper describes each cluster with its own globally routed virtual address and the user "directed to one of them by DNS" ([Eisenbud and others, Maglev, section 4.2](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/nsdi16/nsdi16-paper-eisenbud.pdf)).

## Failure modes, and how they are found

- **A control plane that loses its state.** On 24 December 2012 a maintenance process run against production deleted part of the state data of AWS's Elastic Load Balancing control plane; running load balancers kept working until the control plane changed them, at which point load balancers that were modified "were improperly configured"; at the peak 6.8% of running load balancers were affected, and the service was declared normal at 12:05 PM Pacific Standard Time (PST) on 25 December ([AWS, Summary of the December 24, 2012 Amazon ELB Service Event in the US-East Region](https://aws.amazon.com/message/680587/)). From 12:24 PM on 24 December, that is 23 hours 41 minutes. The data plane's indifference to its control plane was the part that worked.
- **Correlated false positives.** Probes that fail together, from a shared dependency or a shared vantage point, defeat consecutive-failure thresholds; the AWS 2025 and Meta 2021 cases above are the examples, and fail-open rules the defence.
- **Checks that cannot fail.** A pool or profile that shows traffic flowing while its checks are Degraded, as Microsoft warns, or a one-member NGINX group that is never marked unavailable.
- **Probes blocked or multiplied.** Probers without firewall rules mark everything down; probes from many locations multiply the load on each target, two requests a minute from each Azure Front Door edge location at the default 30-second frequency ([Microsoft Learn, Azure Front Door health probes](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/frontdoor/health-probes)).
- **Changes that pass the gate.** Asynchronous faults pass synchronous health gates, as Azure Front Door's rollout did; configuration that reaches every site at once, as in Cloudflare's 2025 report and Akamai's 2021 summary, makes every site one failure domain, which the [hub's landmark table](/learn/redundant-architectures-how-networks-survive-failure) shows in a dozen outages.
- **Caches that outlive the plan.** Resolvers that ignore TTLs or serve stale data keep clients on a dead site; `dig` against several resolvers, built with the [dig command builder](/tools/dig-command-builder) and read with the [dig output explainer](/tools/dig-output-explainer), shows what each one is still handing out and with what remaining TTL.

The [GSLB decision-flow explainer](/tools/f5-gslb-decision-flow) and the [BIG-IP DNS GSLB simulator](/tools/bigip-dns-gslb-simulator) show which pool and member an answer comes from; the [Scripted monitor explainer](/tools/bigip-scripted-monitor-explainer) and the [BigD thread calculator](/tools/f5-bigd-thread-calculator) help with the BIG-IP monitors themselves. The way to find out what a design does when every probe fails is to make every probe fail, in a window, which is the subject of [part 10](/learn/testing-redundancy-failure-drills-chaos-and-the-failover-never-tried).

```python
# Detection, DNS tail and drain, redone in the Python playground.
print("HAProxy down/up:", 2 * 3, 2 * 2, "s; ALB down/up:", 30 * 2, 30 * 5, "s")
degraded = 120 + 10          # Traffic Manager: fourth failure at 120 s plus 10 s timeout
print("Traffic Manager Degraded at", degraded, "s, last compliant cache gone by", degraded + 30, "s")
for ttl in (30, 300, 3600):
    print(ttl, [round(100 * max(0, 1 - t / ttl), 1) for t in (10, 30, 60, 150, 300)])
import math
print("still open after a 300 s drain, 10-minute mean:", round(100 * math.exp(-300 / 600), 1), "%")
```

## What to remember

- A health check is a classifier with two errors: false positives remove capacity, false negatives keep faults in service; thresholds trade detection time for fewer false verdicts, and the trade fails when probe errors are correlated.
- Defaults differ by an order of magnitude: HAProxy leaves a member out after about 6 seconds, Google Cloud after 10, BIG-IP's http monitor after 16, Kubernetes readiness after 30, an AWS target group after 60, Traffic Manager after 100 to 130.
- Most platforms fail open when every target looks dead; know whether yours does, because the rule protects against broken checks and also hides them.
- Backup tiers, priority groups and DNS failover all assume the next tier can carry the whole load; Envoy's priority 0 keeps everything until less than 71.4% of it is healthy.
- Draining and slow start are timers with edges: a 300-second drain still cuts most long-lived connections, and a member that returns at full weight can flap.
- DNS failover is detection plus TTL plus a tail of caches that ignore the TTL, measured in hours for a minority; anycast replaces the TTL with routing convergence and adds a withdrawal mechanism that can fail everywhere at once.
