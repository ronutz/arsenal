Steganography, as [the opener of this series](/learn/steganography-hiding-that-a-message-exists) defines it, succeeds when nobody suspects a message. A watermark has a different job. Everyone may know a film is marked; what matters is that the mark survives copying, compression and deliberate tampering, and that it still says, at the end, who owns the work or which copy leaked. The techniques overlap with those of [the images part](/learn/hiding-in-images-lsb-jpeg-and-adaptive-steganography) and [the part on sound, video and text](/learn/hiding-in-sound-video-and-text-from-echo-hiding-to-zero-width-characters), but the goals pull the other way: capacity matters less, robustness more, and secrecy of the mark's existence hardly at all. After reading, you can tell a watermark from a fingerprint in both senses of the word, explain how a spread-spectrum mark is embedded and detected, describe the forensic marks the cinema, broadcast and streaming industries specify, follow a collusion-resistant fingerprint and a printer's tracking dots through a worked example, name the attacks that defeat marks, and say what a mark can and cannot prove.

## Watermark, fingerprint and the words around them

Fabien Petitcolas, Ross Anderson and Markus Kuhn set out the vocabulary in 1999. Copyright marking, unlike steganography, "has the additional requirement of robustness against possible attacks". Robust marks are those that cannot be removed "without destroying the object at the same time"; fragile marks "are destroyed as soon as the object is modified too much", which "can be used to prove that an object has not been 'doctored'". Among robust marks, fingerprints, also called labels, "are like hidden serial numbers which enable the intellectual property owner to identify which customer broke his license agreement", while watermarks "tell us who is the owner of the object" ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, Information Hiding: A Survey, Proceedings of the IEEE, July 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~fapp2/publications/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)).

"Fingerprint" has a second meaning that causes confusion. A content fingerprint is not embedded at all: it is computed from the content, so that the same picture or song can be recognized later. The Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA) keeps both kinds in one register of "soft bindings", and labels each entry as a watermark or a fingerprint ([C2PA soft binding algorithm list](https://spec.c2pa.org/softbinding-alg-list/softbinding-algorithm-list.json)). Industry documents are not consistent either: the Digital Cinema Initiatives (DCI) specification says that "Industry terminology for watermarking and Forensic Marking is not consistent" and uses "Forensic Marking" for all content marks ([DCI, Digital Cinema System Specification 1.5.1, approved 20 October 2026](https://documents.dcimovies.com/DCSS/release/1.5.1/Digital-Cinema-System-Specification-1.5.1.pdf)).

| Term | What it is | Same in every copy? | Typical question it answers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Robust watermark | An embedded mark meant to survive processing | Usually yes | Who owns this, or what is this? |
| Forensic watermark, fingerprint in the first sense | An embedded mark that differs per copy, recipient, session or screening | No | Which copy leaked? |
| Fragile or semi-fragile watermark | An embedded mark meant to break when content is changed (semi-fragile: only by malicious changes) | Usually yes | Has this been altered? |
| Content fingerprint, fingerprint in the second sense | A compact description computed from the content, nothing embedded | Not applicable | Have we seen this content before? |
| Visible watermark | A logo or text overlaid on the content | Usually yes | Whose is it, at a glance? |

## A short history: from paper mills to pixels

Paper watermarks appeared at the end of the thirteenth century to tell paper makers apart ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~fapp2/publications/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)). They are still a security feature of money: the Banco Central do Brasil's guide to the second family of the Real tells the public to hold a note against the light to see the "Marca-d'Água", which "é feita durante a fabricação do papel. Ela não é impressa no papel" (it is made during the manufacture of the paper and is not printed on it), and shows the animal and the value each note carries ([Banco Central do Brasil, Cartilha da segunda família do Real](https://www.bcb.gov.br/content/cedulasemoedas/cedulas_moedas_docs/material_apoio/cartilha_segunda_familia_real.pdf)).

Marking copies to catch copiers is just as old in spirit. Publishers of logarithm tables and astronomical tables in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries introduced deliberate errors in the last digits, and "To this day, database and mailing list vendors insert bogus entries in order to identify customers who try to resell their products" ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~fapp2/publications/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)). The survey cites, among these examples, Neal Wagner's paper titled simply "Fingerprinting", given at the 1983 IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy ([Crossref record, doi 10.1109/SP.1983.10018](https://doi.org/10.1109/SP.1983.10018)).

A 1954 patent shows the same idea applied to sound. Emil Frank Hembrooke, of Brooklyn, assignor to Muzak Corporation, filed "Identification of sound and like signals" on 29 April 1954; it was granted on 10 October 1961 as US 3,004,104. The patent addresses rebroadcasts of music that could not be proved to be copies, and says the invention "makes possible the positive identification of the origin of a musical presentation" and "may be likened to a watermark in paper". Its method: suppress a narrow band of frequencies, around 1,000 cycles per second for music, at timed intervals, so that the gaps spell a code, "for example, the name of the orchestra or the trademark of the recording company may be spelled out in International Code"; a band-pass filter and a recording meter read the code back. Because of "the insensitivity of the ear in detecting the absence of a particular frequency or narrow band of frequencies", the patent says, the code is imperceptible to the listener ([US patent 3,004,104](https://patents.google.com/patent/US3004104A/en)).

Leak tracing by document marking has a story attached that is often repeated and rarely sourced. Anderson and Petitcolas told it in 1998 with a hedge: "Apparently, during the 1980’s, Margaret Thatcher became so irritated at press leaks of cabinet documents that she had the word processors programmed to encode their identity in the word spacing, so that disloyal ministers could be traced" ([Anderson and Petitcolas, IEEE Journal on Selected Areas in Communications, May 1998](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rja14/Papers/jsac98-limsteg.pdf)). <Claim status="reported" by="by Anderson and Petitcolas">The Thatcher anecdote is told by two academics who introduce it with "apparently", and the passage cites no source.</Claim>

The digital era began in the early 1990s. A. Z. Tirkel and colleagues "were the first to note that spread spectrum techniques could be applied to digital watermarking" ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~fapp2/publications/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)); R. G. van Schyndel, A. Z. Tirkel and C. F. Osborne's "A digital watermark" appeared at the first IEEE International Conference on Image Processing in 1994 ([Crossref record, doi 10.1109/ICIP.1994.413536](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICIP.1994.413536)), and I. J. Cox, J. Kilian, F. T. Leighton and T. Shamoon's "Secure spread spectrum watermarking for multimedia" in IEEE Transactions on Image Processing in December 1997 ([Crossref record, doi 10.1109/83.650120](https://doi.org/10.1109/83.650120)). Companies followed: Digimarc was incorporated in Oregon on 3 January 1995, and its 1999 registration statement mentions software plug-ins licensed to Adobe Systems and one-time licensing fees from Adobe Systems and Corel Corporation in 1996 ([Digimarc, Form S-1, 1999](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001089443/000092962499001732/0000929624-99-001732.txt)); Verance was founded in April 1999 from the merger of two audio watermark developers ([Verance, About](https://www.verance.com/about/)).

## How a robust watermark works

Most robust marks borrow the logic of spread spectrum radio: a few bits are spread over many samples with a pseudo-random sequence that looks like noise to anyone without the key, and a receiver with the key gathers them back by correlation ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~fapp2/publications/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)).

**A worked example: one bit in eight samples.** Take eight samples of a host signal, x = (3, −1, 2, 0, −2, 1, −3, 2), and a secret chip sequence of plus and minus ones, p = (+1, −1, −1, +1, +1, −1, +1, −1). To embed a bit b (+1 for a one, −1 for a zero) with strength α = 1, add α·b·p to the host.

| Step | Bit 1 (b = +1) | Bit 0 (b = −1) |
|---|---|---|
| Marked signal y = x + α·b·p | (4, −2, 1, 1, −1, 0, −2, 1) | (2, 0, 3, −1, −3, 2, −4, 3) |
| Detector: sum of y·p | −6 + 8 = 2 | −6 − 8 = −14 |
| Divided by 8 | +0.25, read as 1 | −1.75, read as 0 |

The −6 in both columns is the host's own accidental correlation with the key; the ±8 is the mark. In this toy the host term is large enough to eat most of the margin for a one, which is why real systems spread each bit over far more samples and choose where to put the energy. Cox and colleagues placed the mark in the n most perceptually significant coefficients of the discrete cosine transform (DCT) of an image, scaling each by ṽᵢ = vᵢ(1 + αwᵢ) with wᵢ drawn from a Gaussian distribution, and tested for it by comparing the extracted sequence with the original one against a threshold. The scheme was robust to rescaling, JPEG (Joint Photographic Experts Group) compression, dithering, clipping, printing and scanning, but it needed the original image for detection and carried a single bit ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~fapp2/publications/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)). The survey sorts detectors by what they need: private systems need the original, semi-private ones only answer whether a given mark is present, and public or blind systems extract the bits with nothing but the key, the hardest and most useful case.

The ear and the eye decide how strong α can be. The broadcast audio watermark standardized for ATSC 3.0, the Advanced Television Systems Committee's next-generation television, illustrates the trade in normative language: the strength of each symbol "dictates the content-dependent tradeoff between imperceptibility of the audio watermark to the audience and the robustness of the audio watermark to distortion", and an average strength of 0.3 gave acceptable results in the implementation tested ([ATSC A/334, Audio Watermark Emission, 14 April 2026](https://www.atsc.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/A334-2026-04-Audio-Watermark-Emission.pdf)).

## Fragile and semi-fragile marks

A fragile mark is the opposite design: it should break at the first change, so that its survival proves integrity. Semi-fragile marks tolerate ordinary processing such as compression but break under malicious edits. A 2009 master's thesis at COPPE, the engineering graduate school of the Universidade Federal do Rio de Janeiro (UFRJ), supervised by Eduardo Antônio Barros da Silva, proposed both for video: a fragile mark for H.264 that reduces the error drift of an earlier insertion method, and a semi-fragile mark using quantization index modulation (QIM) with Watson's perceptual model to authenticate images and videos compressed with the DCT-based JPEG, MPEG-2 and H.264 standards ([Oliveira, COPPE/UFRJ, September 2009](https://eletrica.ufrj.br/pee/index.php/pt/producao-academica/dissertacoes-de-mestrado/2009-1/2009092401-2009092401/file)). Cryptographic signatures do the same job without touching the pixels, which is the route Content Credentials take, described below.

## Attacks: how marks are broken or abused

**Distortion.** StirMark, freely available since November 1997 and described by Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn as a generic tool for basic robustness testing, stretches, shears, shifts, bends and rotates an image by unnoticeable random amounts; such distortions defeated many marking schemes of the time, and the tool became a benchmark. Their general lesson: "given a target marking scheme, one can invent a distortion (or a combination of distortions) that will prevent detection of the watermark while leaving the perceptual value of the previously watermarked object undiminished", and "It is an open question whether there is any digital watermarking scheme for which a chosen distortion attack cannot be found" ([Petitcolas, Anderson and Kuhn, 1999](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~fapp2/publications/ieee99-infohiding.pdf)). The echo-cancellation attack on echo hiding is described in [the part on sound, video and text](/learn/hiding-in-sound-video-and-text-from-echo-hiding-to-zero-width-characters).

**Copying the mark.** Martin Kutter, Svyatoslav Voloshynovskyy and Alexander Herrigel proposed in 2000 an attack that leaves an embedded watermark intact but copies it from one image into a different one, and requires "neither algorithmic knowledge of the watermarking technology nor the watermarking key"; it estimates the mark by filtering and adds the estimate to the target, and it worked against commercial and non-commercial schemes ([Kutter, Voloshynovskiy and Herrigel, Watermark copy attack, SPIE Electronic Imaging 2000](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:47812)). A forged mark in an innocent file is the reverse of removal: it makes a mark's presence weaker evidence.

**Claiming the mark.** Scott Craver, Nasir Memon, B.-L. Yeo and M. M. Yeung showed in May 1998 that "rightful ownership cannot be resolved by current watermarking schemes alone": an attacker can run a counterfeit scheme on an already marked image so that two parties each appear to hold the original, and "In the absence of standardization and specific requirements imposed on watermarking procedures, anyone can claim ownership of any watermarked image". Their remedy was non-invertible schemes ([Craver, Memon, Yeo and Yeung, IEEE Journal on Selected Areas in Communications, May 1998](https://research.ibm.com/publications/resolving-rightful-ownerships-with-invisible-watermarking-techniques-limitations-attacks-and-implications)).

**Collusion.** People holding differently marked copies can compare them and spot where they differ. That attack has its own theory, in the section on traitor tracing below.

**The SDMI challenge.** The Secure Digital Music Initiative (SDMI), a consortium of music-industry companies, announced a public challenge in September 2000 to break four watermarking technologies and two other technologies within about three weeks. A team from Princeton and Rice universities led by Edward Felten reported: "We have defeated all four of their audio watermarking technologies", while acknowledging that "Some debate remains as to whether our attacks damaged the audio beyond standards measured by “golden ear” human listeners" ([Craver et al., Reading Between the Lines: Lessons from the SDMI Challenge, USENIX Security Symposium, August 2001](https://www.usenix.org/legacy/events/sec01/craver.pdf)). When they prepared to publish, the team was "threatened by SDMI and the Recording Industry Association of America (RIAA) to keep silent or face litigation under the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA)", in the account of the Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF), which sued on their behalf on 6 June 2001; a federal court in New Jersey dismissed the case in November 2001, and the researchers chose not to appeal, citing assurances from the government, the recording industry and the court that the threats were ill-conceived and would not be repeated ([EFF, Felten et al. v. RIAA et al.](https://www.eff.org/cases/felten-et-al-v-riaa-et-al)). The paper was published in the USENIX proceedings that August.

## The industry: who marks what, and why

**Currency.** The Central Bank Counterfeit Deterrence Group (CBCDG), "a group of 37 central banks and note printing authorities organized at the request of the Governors of the G10 central banks", runs a counterfeit deterrence system that "has been voluntarily adopted by hardware and software manufacturers, and prevents personal computers and digital imaging tools from capturing or reproducing the image of a protected banknote"; it states that "The technology does not have the capacity to track the use of a personal computer or digital imaging tools" ([CBCDG, Rules for Use](https://www.rulesforuse.org/)). Digimarc's 1999 registration statement reported a multi-year contract with a consortium of central banks to deter the use of personal computers in counterfeiting ([Digimarc, Form S-1, 1999](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001089443/000092962499001732/0000929624-99-001732.txt)); its annual report for 2025 says the banks have trusted it "to deter the counterfeiting of global currency" for "nearly 30 years", that the contract now runs to 31 December 2029, and that "Nearly half of our revenue came from our contract with the Central Banks in 2025 and 2024"; the company had 110 full-time employees at the end of 2025 and is based in Beaverton, Oregon ([Digimarc, Form 10-K for 2025, filed 11 March 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1438231/000143774926007793/dmrc20251231_10k.htm)). Digimarc also sells leak detection, saying it uses "the same trusted Digimarc technology built into Microsoft Windows for nearly a decade" ([Digimarc, Leak Detection](https://www.digimarc.com/solutions/leak-detection)), a claim made by the vendor.

**Discs and players.** Verance's own timeline records that its VCMS/A audio watermark was selected as a copyright protection standard for music distribution in July 1999 and became mandatory for new DVD-Audio players in June 2001; that the major studios adopted its Cinavia mark in January 2007; that Cinavia reached Blu-ray players in March 2009; and that it became mandatory for all new Blu-ray Disc playback devices in February 2012 ([Verance, About](https://www.verance.com/about/)). Cinavia sits in the soundtrack: a device with a detector that finds the mark in an unauthorized copy stops playback and displays an enforcement message ([Verance, Cinavia](https://www.verance.com/cinavia/)). MovieLabs' Specification for Enhanced Content Protection (ECP), version 1.4 of August 2024, recommends that "A compliant system should implement Cinavia playback controls on all content" ([MovieLabs, ECP Specification 1.4](https://movielabs.com/ngvideo/MovieLabs_ECP_Spec_v1.4.pdf)).

**Broadcast.** Since 2016 the ATSC 3.0 family includes two optional watermarks whose job is to carry the broadcaster's signalling data through redistribution paths that drop it, such as a consumer High-Definition Multimedia Interface (HDMI) connection. The audio watermark, VP1, sits in a perceptually important frequency band so that it is "retained through audio distribution paths" and resists "modern techniques for lossy signal compression"; each symbol is defined by the difference between the signal's autocorrelation at a fixed delay over the first and the second half of the symbol interval, and 159 bits (a 32-bit header and a 127-bit packet) form one cell every 1.5 seconds ([ATSC A/334, 2026](https://www.atsc.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/A334-2026-04-Audio-Watermark-Emission.pdf); first approved 19 September 2016 per [ATSC's A/334 page](https://www.atsc.org/atsc-documents/a3342016-audio-watermark-emission/)). The video watermark modulates the luma (brightness) of the top two lines of active video; in the standard's 1920-by-1080 example each group of 8 horizontal pixels carries one symbol. The "1X" version carries 30 bytes per frame using black and a dark grey, which keeps it unobtrusive "even if the display happens to present all 1080 lines"; the "2X" version doubles the capacity using four luma levels, black, white and two greys. Its authors are candid about its purpose: "It is not intended to be a tamper-resistant or indelible watermark, and it may be deliberately obliterated by an intermediary" ([ATSC A/335, Video Watermark Emission, 14 April 2026](https://www.atsc.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/A335-2026-04-Video-Watermark-Emission.pdf); [ATSC's A/335 page](https://www.atsc.org/atsc-documents/a3352016-video-watermark-emission/)). Verance says ATSC selected its technologies as the basis of these open watermark standards in April 2015 ([Verance, About](https://www.verance.com/about/)).

**Audience measurement.** Watermarks also count viewers and listeners. Nielsen explained in 2015 that "At the heart of Nielsen’s PPM audience measurement is its watermarking, or critical band encoding technology (CBET)", describing watermarking as "the process of embedding data that humans can’t hear directly into audio content"; each portable people meter (PPM) in Nielsen's panels picks up the codes and transmits complete and partial codes back to Nielsen, which then had 48 PPM markets ([Nielsen, Making Noise: Nielsen Audio Gets into the Spirit of Total Audience, 2015](https://www.nielsen.com/news-center/2015/making-noise-nielsen-audio-gets-into-the-spirit-of-total-audience/)). Cloud encoders embed such marks as a routine step: AWS Elemental MediaConvert documents Kantar watermarks, "audio watermarks that are audible to machines but not to humans", for "audience measurement and anti-piracy applications", and supports Nielsen watermarking too ([AWS, Using Kantar for audio watermarking](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/mediaconvert/latest/ug/kantar-watermarking.html); [AWS, Working with Nielsen watermarking](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/mediaconvert/latest/ug/nielsen-watermarking.html)).

**Cinemas.** The DCI specification requires every media block, the secure unit that decrypts the film for playback, to apply forensic marks to image and audio "to implant evidence of origin of the content" with "data indicating time, date and location of playback". Its numbers make a good worked example of payload design ([DCI, DCSS 1.5.1](https://documents.dcimovies.com/DCSS/release/1.5.1/Digital-Cinema-System-Specification-1.5.1.pdf)):

| Requirement | DCI's figure | The arithmetic |
|---|---|---|
| Time stamp | Every 15 minutes, 24 hours a day, 366 days, repeating each year | 4 × 24 × 366 = 35,136 stamps, which fit in 16 bits (65,536) |
| Location | Serial number of the marking device | 19 bits (524,288 values; DCI writes 524,000) or 20 bits (1,048,576) |
| Repetition | All payload bits in each five-minute segment | Any five-minute excerpt carries the whole payload |
| Recovery | Up to a 30-minute sample for a positive identification | Identification may need half an hour of material |
| Image survival | Digital-analogue-digital conversion, resampling, recompression, resizing, cropping, noise, frame swapping, collusion | Each listed as a requirement |
| Audio survival | Inaudible in critical A/B listening tests; survives linear speed changes within 10% and pitch-invariant time scaling within 4% | Each listed as a requirement |

**Streaming.** MovieLabs' specification asks for systems that can "securely forensically mark video at the server and/or client" and a mark that is "robust against corruption of the forensic information, including collusion attacks" ([MovieLabs, ECP Specification 1.4](https://movielabs.com/ngvideo/MovieLabs_ECP_Spec_v1.4.pdf)). One common design is A/B watermarking. The Kudelski Group, owner of NAGRA, acquired the watermarking company NexGuard Labs, formerly Civolution, on 8 July 2016 ([Kudelski Group, press release, 8 July 2016](https://www.nagra.com/sites/default/files/migration/pressreleases/NexGuard_PR_08_07_2016_E.pdf)). An Amazon Web Services (AWS) blog post describes NexGuard's live-streaming setup: two encoders produce variant A and variant B of every segment, each with its own invisible mark; a function at the content delivery network's edge reads the viewer's token and serves each viewer a unique sequence of A and B segments, which "works to individually identify millions of streaming sessions"; to trace a leak, NAGRA extracts the A/B sequence from a pirate copy, typically from an excerpt of about five minutes ([AWS for M&E Blog, Securing premium live content with NAGRA NexGuard forensic watermarking on AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/media/securing-premium-live-content-with-nagra-nexguard-forensic-watermarking-on-aws/)).

**Brazilian research on leak tracing.** A 2015 master's thesis at the Pontifícia Universidade Católica do Rio de Janeiro (PUC-Rio) started from a problem it describes as frequent in the television industry: leaks of filmed material while it circulates among production collaborators. It proposed a tool that uses the Speeded Up Robust Features (SURF) detector to find robust regions of each image and hides a small identifier there with a two-dimensional Haar discrete wavelet transform, so that the mark survives resizing and changes of resolution ([Escobar, PUC-Rio, April 2015](https://www.maxwell.vrac.puc-rio.br/25814/25814.PDF)). The font-based marking of police and court documents developed at the Universidade de Brasília with the Polícia Federal is described in [the part on sound, video and text](/learn/hiding-in-sound-video-and-text-from-echo-hiding-to-zero-width-characters).

## Traitor tracing: fingerprints that survive collusion

Marking each copy differently raises an obvious counter. Dan Boneh and J. Shaw put it in their abstract: "for digital data, two different fingerprinted objects can be compared and the differences between them detected. Hence, a set of users can collude to detect the location of the fingerprint. They can then alter the fingerprint to mask their identities" ([Boneh and Shaw, Collusion-secure fingerprinting for digital data, Crypto '95 and IEEE Transactions on Information Theory 44(5), 1998](https://crypto.stanford.edu/~dabo/pubs/abstracts/finger.html); technical report of [October 1994](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/research/techreps/168)). Benny Chor, Amos Fiat and Moni Naor's "Tracing Traitors" appeared at CRYPTO '94 ([Crossref record, doi 10.1007/3-540-48658-5_25](https://doi.org/10.1007/3-540-48658-5_25)).

The theory rests on one assumption, which Thijs Laarhoven states plainly: colluders can change the mark only where their copies differ; where they all received the same version, they output that version. This is "known in the literature as the Boneh-Shaw marking assumption", and under it "it is still possible to find all colluders using suitable fingerprinting codes" ([Laarhoven, Capacities and Capacity-Achieving Decoders for Various Fingerprinting Games, arXiv 1401.5688](https://arxiv.org/abs/1401.5688)). A/B streaming makes the assumption physical: a colluder who received segment A cannot produce segment B.

**A worked example: four viewers, eight segments, two colluders.** Each viewer receives a sequence of A and B variants:

| Viewer | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| V1 | A | A | B | B | A | B | A | B |
| V2 | A | B | A | B | B | A | A | B |
| V3 | B | A | A | B | A | A | B | A |
| V4 | B | B | B | A | B | B | A | A |

V1 and V2 compare copies. Segments 1, 4, 7 and 8 are the same for both (A, B, A, B), so under the marking assumption the pirate copy must carry those. In segments 2, 3, 5 and 6 they differ and can choose; say they take A, A, B, B. The leaked copy reads A A A B B B A B. Counting agreements with each viewer's sequence:

| Viewer | Agreements with the leak, out of 8 |
|---|---|
| V1 | 6 |
| V2 | 6 |
| V3 | 3 |
| V4 | 3 |

The colluders stand out, but only just. With more colluders and short codes, an innocent viewer can match by chance, which is why code length is the central question. Gábor Tardos proved that codes secure against c pirates among n users need length on the order of c²·log(n/ε) for an error probability ε, which "improves the codes proposed by Boneh and Shaw [1998] whose length is approximately the square of this length", and gave a matching lower bound ([Tardos, Optimal probabilistic fingerprint codes, Journal of the ACM 55(2), May 2008, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1145/1346330.1346335)). His explicit construction uses ℓ = 100c²·ln(n/ε₁), and later work brought the sufficient length down towards about 2c²·ln n for large n ([Laarhoven](https://arxiv.org/abs/1401.5688)). For c = 4 colluders among n = 1,000,000 viewers with ε₁ = 0.001, the original constant gives 100 × 16 × ln(10⁹) ≈ 1,600 × 20.72 ≈ 33,157 positions; the asymptotic figure gives 2 × 16 × ln(10⁶) ≈ 442. The cinema and streaming specifications both list collusion among the attacks a mark must survive, and identification in both works on minutes of material rather than seconds.

## Printer tracking dots

**The 2005 decoding.** On 16 October 2005 the EFF announced that "A research team led by the Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) recently broke the code behind tiny tracking dots that some color laser printers secretly hide in every document". In its words, the US Secret Service "admitted that the tracking information is part of a deal struck with selected color laser printer manufacturers, ostensibly to identify counterfeiters", and Xerox "previously admitted that it provided these tracking dots to the government, but indicated that only the Secret Service had the ability to read the code". EFF staff technologist Seth Schoen said: "We've found that the dots from at least one line of printers encode the date and time your document was printed, as well as the serial number of the printer". The dots are yellow, less than a millimetre across, repeated over the page, and visible under blue light or magnification ([EFF, Secret Code in Color Printers Lets Government Track You, 16 October 2005](https://www.eff.org/press/archives/2005/10/16)).

EFF's decoding guide, by Robert Lee, Seth Schoen, Patrick Murphy, Joel Alwen and Andrew "bunnie" Huang, gives the format of the Xerox DocuColor series: a grid of 15 by 8 dots printed repeatedly over every colour page, encoding up to 14 seven-bit bytes plus row and column parity, of which about 10 are used. The top row and the leftmost column are parity, with every column and every row except the top holding an odd number of dots. Each column is read top to bottom as a seven-bit number, rows weighted 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2 and 1. Column 2 holds the minute, 5 the hour, 6 the day, 7 the month and 8 the year without the century; 10 is a separator of all ones; 11 to 14 hold the printer's serial number in binary-coded decimal, two digits per column, so that serial 00654321 is coded as 00, 65, 43 and 21; 15 is unknown and often zero ([EFF, DocuColor Tracking Dot Decoding Guide](https://w2.eff.org/Privacy/printers/docucolor/)).

**A worked example: decoding a toy grid.** The grid below is invented for illustration, following the layout of EFF's guide (● is a dot, · is no dot; column 1 on the right).

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| parity | ● | ● | ● | ● | · | · | ● | ● | ● | · | · | ● | ● | ● | · |
| 64 | · | · | · | · | · | ● | · | · | · | · | · | · | · | · | · |
| 32 | · | · | · | ● | ● | ● | · | · | · | · | · | · | · | · | · |
| 16 | · | · | · | · | ● | ● | · | · | · | ● | · | · | · | ● | ● |

| Column | Meaning | Dots, top to bottom (64 to 1) | Value |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | Minute | 0011110 | 16 + 8 + 4 + 2 = 30 |
| 5 | Hour | 0001110 | 8 + 4 + 2 = 14 |
| 6 | Day | 0010000 | 16 |
| 7 | Month | 0001010 | 8 + 2 = 10 |
| 8 | Year | 0000101 | 4 + 1 = 5, that is 2005 |

The page was printed at 14:30 on 16 October 2005 on the printer with serial number 00123456. The parity works as the guide says: column 2 holds four data dots, so its parity dot in the top row is set, making five; row 16 holds four dots in columns 2 to 15 (columns 11, 10, 6 and 2), so its parity dot in column 1 is set, making five. Every column, and every row below the top, ends with an odd count, so a single misread dot shows up where a failing row crosses a failing column.

**What came after.** In a note added in 2017 to its list of printers, EFF wrote that it appears likely that all recent commercial colour laser printers print some kind of forensic tracking code, not necessarily yellow dots, whether or not the code is visible ([EFF, List of Printers Which Do or Do Not Display Tracking Dots](https://www.eff.org/pages/list-printers-which-do-or-do-not-display-tracking-dots)); documents EFF obtained suggested a later generation that "works by slightly rearranging dots that the printer is expected to print, rather than by adding new dots" ([EFF, Printer Tracking Dots Back in the News, 6 June 2017](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2017/06/printer-tracking-dots-back-news)). In Europe, the EFF reported in February 2008 that European Commissioner Frattini, answering a question by Finnish Member of the European Parliament Satu Hassi, said that "to the extent that individuals may be identified through material printed or copied using certain equipment, such processing may give rise to the violation of fundamental human rights, namely the right to privacy and private life" ([EFF, EU: Printer Tracking Dots May Violate Human Rights, 13 February 2008](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2008/02/eu-printer-tracking-dots-may-violate-human-rights)). The Commission's written answer, dated 23 January 2008, also says that it knew of no specific national or Community law governing tracking mechanisms in colour laser printers and photocopiers ([European Parliament, answer to written question E-5724/2007](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/doceo/document/E-6-2007-5724-ASW_EN.html)). Timo Richter, Stephan Escher, Dagmar Schönfeld and Thorsten Strufe, whose toolkit is published by a group at the Technische Universität Dresden (TU Dresden), reviewed the dots in 2018 as "an extrinsic signature embedded in nearly all modern colour laser printers", found a new pattern, decoded pattern information, and investigated anonymization ([Richter et al., Forensic Analysis and Anonymisation of Printed Documents, ACM IH&MMSec 2018, abstract](https://archive.cps-vo.org/node/60777)). Their open toolkit, DEDA, reads and decodes the dots and can print a per-printer mask to cover them, with the caveats that "monochrome pages as well as inkjet prints might not contain tracking dots" and that users should check under a microscope that the mask covers the dots ([DEDA repository](https://github.com/dfd-tud/deda)).

**The Reality Winner case.** On 5 June 2017 The Intercept published a top-secret National Security Agency (NSA) report, dated 5 May 2017, on a Russian hacking effort against elements of the United States election infrastructure, a document it said had been "provided anonymously" ([The Intercept, 5 June 2017](https://theintercept.com/2017/06/05/top-secret-nsa-report-details-russian-hacking-effort-days-before-2016-election/)). That day Robert Graham of Errata Security showed how to make the yellow dots visible in the published scans and read them: "The document leaked by the Intercept was from a printer with model number 54, serial number 29535218. The document was printed on May 9, 2017 at 6:20" ([Graham, How The Intercept Outed Reality Winner, Errata Security, 5 June 2017](http://blog.erratasec.com/2017/06/how-intercept-outed-reality-winner.html)). The affidavit of the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) filed the same day describes a different path to the suspect. The agency examined the copy the news outlet had shared and found the pages "appeared to be folded and/or creased, suggesting they had been printed and hand-carried out of a secured space"; an internal audit found that six individuals had printed the report; and an audit of their desk computers showed that only Reality Leigh Winner had e-mail contact with the outlet. She admitted printing the report when agents spoke to her on 3 June ([FBI affidavit in support of arrest warrant, case 1:17-mj-00024, filed 5 June 2017](https://www.justice.gov/opa/press-release/file/971331/download)). The EFF drew the conclusion the record allows: "The affidavit did not mention the use of the tracking dots at all, but referred only to other sources of information." <Claim status="attributed" by="by the EFF">The dots "could have played a role" in the arrest, but "It's quite possible that printer dots did not play any role in this investigation at all."</Claim> ([EFF, 6 June 2017](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2017/06/printer-tracking-dots-back-news)). Winner pleaded guilty to one count, "Willful retention and transmission of national defense information" under 18 U.S.C. § 793(e), and was sentenced on 23 August 2018 by Chief Judge J. Randal Hall to five years and three months in prison ([US District Court, Southern District of Georgia, judgment in case 1:17-cr-00034, 23 August 2018](https://www.justsecurity.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/Reality-Winner_Judgment-August-23-2018-.pdf); [US Department of Justice, 23 August 2018](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/federal-government-contractor-sentenced-removing-and-transmitting-classified-materials-news)). What the case does show is that the dots on published scans independently pointed to the printer, the date and the minute; leak investigators had more than one way in.

## Content Credentials and watermarks for AI-generated content

The C2PA's Content Credentials attach a signed history to a file; what they prove and what they do not is set out in [Verification and fact-checking](/learn/verification-and-fact-checking). Watermarks enter as a fallback for when that metadata is stripped. The C2PA's soft binding API describes the mechanism: a soft binding, "for example an invisible watermark or content fingerprint", is used to look up the manifest in a repository, so that a viewer who meets a photograph with its credentials removed can recover them, and so that a manifest substituted by a forger can be checked against the one the watermark points to ([C2PA, Soft Binding API, version 2.3](https://spec.c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.3/softbinding/Decoupled.html)). The C2PA's register of soft-binding algorithms, read on 17 November 2026, held 28 entries, 25 watermarks and 3 fingerprints. The six entries dated 17 May 2024 include Digimarc's watermark, the ATSC watermarks (A/334, A/335 and A/336), two variants of Adobe's TrustMark and two fingerprints, one of them the International Standard Content Code (ISCC) of ISO 24138; later entries include NAGRA's NexGuard (February 2025), two Microsoft marks (October 2025) and, in February 2026, MarkAny and Verimatrix ([C2PA soft binding algorithm list](https://spec.c2pa.org/softbinding-alg-list/softbinding-algorithm-list.json)). Digimarc says it "built the first implementation of invisible watermarking for C2PA purposes" ([Digimarc, Provenance and Authenticity](https://www.digimarc.com/solutions/provenance-authenticity)), a vendor's claim.

Watermarks placed in the output of generative models (statistical marks in text, such as the green-list scheme and SynthID-Text, and marks in images and audio) are described with their known weaknesses in [Generative AI and influence operations](/learn/generative-ai-and-influence-operations) and [Voice clones and deepfakes in fraud](/learn/voice-clones-and-deepfakes-in-fraud); the existing [AI watermarking](/glossary/ai-watermarking) entry collects the sources. Their theory and limits are the subject of a later part of this series on steganography and artificial intelligence.

## What a watermark can and cannot prove

**What it can show.** A mark found with the right key is evidence that the copy passed through a marked channel: a cinema's media block at a given hour, a streaming session, a printer on a given day. Its strength as evidence depends on the false-positive rate of the detector, on how much material it was found in (DCI allows up to 30 minutes; NAGRA's example uses about five), and on whether the copy could have been marked by someone else.

**What it cannot show on its own.** Ownership, as Craver and colleagues showed, unless the scheme is designed and registered so that a counterfeit claim cannot be built. Integrity, unless the mark is fragile by design. Innocence, because a mark can be copied into another file. And truth: a provenance mark says where a file came from, not whether its content is accurate, a point the C2PA makes about its own credentials (see [Verification and fact-checking](/learn/verification-and-fact-checking)). The Reality Winner case adds a practical lesson: a mark is one source among many, and investigators may never need it.

**The law around removal.** Most jurisdictions protect marks indirectly, by protecting rights management information. The WIPO Copyright Treaty, adopted in Geneva on 20 December 1996, requires remedies against anyone who knowingly removes or alters "any electronic rights management information without authority", defined as information that identifies the work, its author or owner, or the terms of use, "and any numbers or codes that represent such information" ([WIPO Copyright Treaty, Article 12](https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/text/295166)). The United States implements it in 17 U.S.C. § 1202, which forbids intentionally removing or altering copyright management information without authority ([17 U.S.C. § 1202, Legal Information Institute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/1202)); the European Union in Article 7 of Directive 2001/29/EC ([Directive 2001/29/EC, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32001L0029)). Brazil's copyright law, Lei 9.610 of 19 February 1998, makes liable for damages anyone who alters, suppresses or disables "dispositivos técnicos" introduced in copies to prevent or restrict copying, alters or suppresses "os sinais codificados" meant to restrict communication to the public or copying, or suppresses or alters "sem autorização, qualquer informação sobre a gestão de direitos", and anyone who distributes works knowing that such information or signals were removed ([Lei 9.610/1998, article 107, Câmara dos Deputados](https://www2.camara.leg.br/legin/fed/lei/1998/lei-9610-19-fevereiro-1998-365399-normaatualizada-pl.html)).

**A visible watermark in court.** Visible marks raised a new question when image generators began reproducing them. In Getty Images v Stability AI, the High Court of England and Wales found on 4 November 2025 that some images generated by early versions of Stable Diffusion carried iStock and Getty Images watermarks that infringed Getty's trade marks, but called its findings "both historic and extremely limited in scope", saying that "it is impossible to know how many (or even on what scale) watermarks* have been generated in real life", and dismissed the secondary copyright claim ([Getty Images v Stability AI, 2025 EWHC 2863 (Ch), 4 November 2025](https://www.judiciary.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Getty-Images-v-Stability-AI.pdf)). Getty's appeal, which concerns the secondary copyright claim and not the trade mark findings, was pending before the Court of Appeal, with skeleton arguments filed in July and October 2026 ([Appellants' replacement skeleton argument, CA-2026-000007, 21 October 2026](https://www.judiciary.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Appellants-Replacement-Appeal-Skeleton--CA-2026-000007-144861071_1.pdf)).

## What to remember

- Watermarks identify the owner or the content, fingerprints in the forensic sense identify the copy, fragile marks prove integrity, and content fingerprints recognize content without embedding anything.
- Marking is old: paper mills in the thirteenth century, deliberate errors in printed tables, a Muzak patent filed in 1954 that notched a band around 1 kHz in a coded pattern.
- Robust digital marks spread a few bits over many samples with a secret sequence and detect them by correlation; their strength trades imperceptibility against robustness.
- Every class of mark has a published attack: distortion (StirMark), removal (echo cancellation, the SDMI challenge), forgery (the copy attack), false ownership claims (the ambiguity attack) and collusion.
- Industry marks are everywhere: banknote-image deterrence, Cinavia in Blu-ray players, ATSC 3.0 audio and video watermarks, audience-measurement codes, DCI forensic marks with time and location, A/B session marks in streaming.
- Collusion-resistant codes need length on the order of c²·log(n/ε), and forensic identification in cinema and streaming works on minutes of material.
- Colour laser printers' tracking dots encode a serial number and time; in the Reality Winner case the FBI affidavit cites a print audit and e-mail records, not the dots.
- A mark is evidence of a channel, not of ownership, integrity or truth on its own; laws in Brazil, the US, the EU and the WIPO treaty protect rights management information against removal.

- Colour printers mark every page with a serial number and a time, and most buyers do not know. Should such marks require notice or consent, and who should be allowed to read them?
