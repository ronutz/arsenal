Every earlier part of this series has ended with a short note on the law, and each note has said the same thing in different words: the device is rarely the crime. This part reads the law itself, in Brazil, the United States (US), the European Union (EU), the United Kingdom (UK) and Canada. It is written from statutes, regulations and official records as they stood on 16 November 2026, with every provision dated and linked, and it is not legal advice: laws change, courts interpret them, and the facts of a particular case decide its outcome. Anyone planning to import, carry or use security tools should put the questions at the end of this article to a lawyer qualified where they live and work.

Three different bodies of law meet in a hacking gadget, and confusing them is the commonest mistake in public debate about the [Flipper Zero](/learn/the-flipper-zero-sub-ghz-rfid-nfc-infrared-and-the-panic) and its relatives.

| Question | Body of law | Typical test | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| May this product be sold, imported or used at all? | Radio and product regulation | Certification or declaration of conformity, spectrum limits | Anatel homologation; Federal Communications Commission (FCC) authorization; the EU Radio Equipment Directive |
| May I make, sell, hold or import this tool? | Criminal law on tools | Intent to commit an offence, or a device designed primarily for one | Código Penal art. 154-A § 1; 18 U.S.C. 1029 and 2512; the UK Computer Misuse Act 1990 (CMA) s. 3A; Budapest Convention art. 6 |
| May I use it on this system? | Criminal law on access and interception, plus contract | Authorization from whoever controls the system | Art. 154-A caput; the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA); written rules of engagement |

The first column decides whether a device can enter a market; the second and third decide whether a person commits a crime. The [opening part of the series](/learn/hacking-devices-from-the-blue-box-to-the-flipper-zero) summarized where legislators drew the line; what follows is the full reading.

## Brazil

### Anatel homologation: what must be certified

The Lei Geral de Telecomunicações, Lei 9.472 of 16 July 1997, sets the principle in article 162, paragraph 2: "É vedada a utilização de equipamentos emissores de radiofreqüência sem certificação expedida ou aceita pela Agência" (the use of radio-frequency emitting equipment without certification issued or accepted by the Agency is prohibited) ([Senado Federal, Lei 9.472/1997, original text](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/551348/publicacao/15754971)). The Agência Nacional de Telecomunicações (Anatel) regulates how that certification works in Resolução 715 of 23 October 2019, the Regulamento de Avaliação da Conformidade e de Homologação de Produtos para Telecomunicações, whose article 55 states that "A homologação é pré-requisito obrigatório para a utilização e a comercialização, no País, dos produtos abrangidos por este Regulamento" (homologation is a mandatory prerequisite for the use and sale in the country of the products the regulation covers) ([Anatel, Resolução 715/2019, consolidated text](https://informacoes.anatel.gov.br/legislacao/resolucoes/2019/1350-resolucao-715)). The agency's product-certification page explains the purpose: products that "respeitam padrões de qualidade, de segurança e de funcionalidades técnicas regulamentadas", the efficient use of spectrum and electromagnetic compatibility, and announces that a new platform, Certifica, "substitui o antigo SCH", the older Sistema de Certificação e Homologação (SCH) ([Anatel, Certificação de Produtos](https://www.gov.br/anatel/pt-br/regulado/certificacao-de-produtos)). The glossary entry on [Anatel](/glossary/anatel) gives the agency's origin.

Who may ask for homologation matters for a researcher who imports one tool. Article 20 lists "o fabricante do produto para telecomunicações", "o representante comercial de pessoa jurídica estrangeira" and "qualquer pessoa física ou jurídica, quando para uso próprio" (any natural or legal person, for their own use), with the condition that the applicant be resident in Brazil or a Brazilian company so that it can answer for the product ([Anatel, Resolução 715/2019, art. 20](https://informacoes.anatel.gov.br/legislacao/resolucoes/2019/1350-resolucao-715)). Article 33 says that the simpler declaration of conformity should preferably be used for "produtos de construção artesanal ou importados para uso do próprio Requerente", and article 64 limits what that buys: a certificate based on a declaration of conformity confers "o direito de uso do produto de telecomunicações pelo próprio titular", while only a certificate of conformity gives the right to use and sell the product throughout the country (same source). Anatel's manual for the procedure names the applicant type "Importador do produto para uso próprio", a person or company in Brazil that imports a product "normalmente para uso próprio e com restrições de comercialização" ([Anatel, Manual de Orientações, Homologação de Produtos, Uso próprio/pessoal, Declaração de Conformidade, version 2.0, 2018](https://sistemas.anatel.gov.br/anexar-api/publico/anexos/download/6b6b7fbe4c10d3369afbefe22a125180)).

Article 60 is the door that can stay shut. The request "deve ser indeferido" when, among other cases, "o produto se prestar a fins ilícitos, ou concorrer à facilitação de crime ou contravenção penal", or when it "puder prejudicar a prestação dos serviços de telecomunicações legalmente constituídos" ([Anatel, Resolução 715/2019, art. 60, II and III](https://informacoes.anatel.gov.br/legislacao/resolucoes/2019/1350-resolucao-715)). That is a product-level test applied by a regulator, not a criminal test of the buyer's intent.

The rule has recently reached the marketplaces. Resolução 780 of 1 August 2025 added to article 55 a paragraph making "As plataformas intermediadoras de comércio eletrônico ( marketplaces )" and other digital platforms involved in a sale, "ainda que somente realizando atividades de divulgação e propaganda", jointly liable with the seller, including for showing the homologation code in listings and checking it ([Anatel, Resolução 715/2019, art. 55, § 2, inserted by Resolução 780/2025](https://informacoes.anatel.gov.br/legislacao/resolucoes/2019/1350-resolucao-715)). Before that, on 21 June 2024, Anatel had published measures against marketplaces offering non-homologated mobile phones, with escalating daily fines and, as a last step, "o bloqueio do domínio da plataforma até a regularização dos anúncios"; its president, Carlos Baigorri, said that "A lei não tem preço. Deve ser cumprida sem discussão" ([Agência Gov, 21 June 2024](https://agenciagov.ebc.com.br/noticias/202406/anatel-adota-medidas-contra-venda-de-celulares-nao-homologados-em-plataformas-de-comercio-eletronico)).

The own-use route therefore exists in general; for this product, the agency chose not to open it. ### Enforcement at ports and warehouses

The Flipper is a small part of a large customs operation. In December 2025 Anatel and the Receita Federal held 473,500 non-homologated products at the port of Imbituba, in Santa Catarina, which Anatel called the largest seizure of the partnership; its anti-piracy action plan had by then removed about 9 million irregular products from the Brazilian market ([Teletime, 17 December 2025](https://teletime.com.br/17/12/2025/anatel-e-receita-fazem-maior-apreensao-de-produtos-irregulares-de-telecom/)). From 29 September to 1 October 2026 a joint operation near Fortaleza seized 1,303 electronic products without certification and homologation, and the Receita stated the rule that applies to any importer: products "sem certificação e homologação da Anatel retidos pela Receita Federal podem ser destinados" (may be disposed of) if the importer does not prove regularity or obtain homologation within the deadline ([Agência Gov, via Receita Federal, 6 October 2026](https://agenciagov.ebc.com.br/noticias/202610/receita-federal-em-operacao-conjunta-com-a-anatel-retem-mais-de-1-300-produtos-eletronicos-em-situacao-irregular)).

Jammers are a category of their own. Anatel's Resolution 760 of 6 February 2023 restricts radio signal blockers to certain public bodies, and Anatel states that "é vedada a utilização desses equipamentos por pessoa física ou jurídica" ([Agência Gov, 15 September 2025](https://agenciagov.ebc.com.br/noticias/202509/operacao-conjunta-desmantela-fabrica-clandestina-de-bloqueadores-de-sinais-em-sao-paulo)); Ato 10.988 of July 2024 set the technical requirements for those authorized blockers ([Teletime, 21 August 2024](https://teletime.com.br/21/08/2024/anatel-aprova-requisitos-tecnicos-para-bloqueadores-de-sinais/)). Transmitting without authorization can also be a crime: article 183 of Lei 9.472 punishes whoever "Desenvolver clandestinamente atividades de telecomunicação" with "detenção de dois a quatro anos", and article 184 defines clandestine activity as one carried out "sem a competente concessão, permissão ou autorização de serviço, de uso de radiofreqüência e de exploração de satélite" ([Senado Federal, Lei 9.472/1997, original text](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/551348/publicacao/15754971)). The Pontifícia Universidade Católica de São Paulo's legal encyclopedia classifies the offence as one of abstract danger ([Enciclopédia Jurídica da PUC-SP, O crime de telecomunicação clandestina](https://enciclopediajuridica.pucsp.br/verbetes/o-crime-de-telecomunicacao-clandestina/)). The [radio part](/learn/radio-for-hackers-sdr-hackrf-rtl-sdr-and-rolling-codes) sets out the power limits for low-power devices.

### The Receita Federal: Remessa Conforme and the import tax

Tax is separate from homologation. The Programa Remessa Conforme (PRC) "foi instituído pela Instrução Normativa (IN) RFB nº 2.146, de 29 de junho 2023", RFB standing for Receita Federal do Brasil, in force "no dia 1º de agosto de 2023", and its differentiated rate came from an ordinance of the Ministério da Fazenda (MF), Portaria MF 612 of 29 June 2023 ([Receita Federal, Nota Copad/Coana/Suana 49, 22 April 2024](https://www.gov.br/receitafederal/pt-br/assuntos/aduana-e-comercio-exterior/manuais/remessas-postal-e-expressa/nota-49_2024.pdf)). Its scale is visible in the Receita's own report: 30,605,649 international parcels arrived in February and March 2024, and declarations under the programme covered 96.06 per cent of them (same source).

The zero rate did not last. Lei 14.902 of 27 June 2024 set an import tax of 20 per cent on goods up to US$50 and 60 per cent above that, up to US$3,000, with a US$20 deduction, from August 2024; consumers also pay the states' tax on the circulation of goods and services (ICMS), then 17 per cent ([Senado Notícias, 28 June 2024](https://www12.senado.leg.br/noticias/materias/2024/06/28/lula-sanciona-taxacao-de-compra-internacional-e-criacao-do-programa-mover)). The Senado's report notes that, since August 2023, purchases up to US$50 from platforms in the Remessa Conforme programme, such as Amazon, Shein and Shopee, had been exempt (same source). Paying the tax does not cure a missing homologation: as the October 2026 operation shows, the Receita holds radio products that Anatel has not homologated whatever their tax status ([Agência Gov, 6 October 2026](https://agenciagov.ebc.com.br/noticias/202610/receita-federal-em-operacao-conjunta-com-a-anatel-retem-mais-de-1-300-produtos-eletronicos-em-situacao-irregular)).

### The Código Penal: article 154-A

Brazil's computer-intrusion offence was created by Lei 12.737 of 30 November 2012, known after the actress whose case prompted it (the glossary entry [Lei Carolina Dieckmann](/glossary/lei-carolina-dieckmann) has the background). In its original text, article 154-A punished whoever would "Invadir dispositivo informático alheio, conectado ou não à rede de computadores, mediante violação indevida de mecanismo de segurança e com o fim de obter, adulterar ou destruir dados ou informações sem autorização expressa ou tácita do titular do dispositivo ou instalar vulnerabilidades para obter vantagem ilícita", with "detenção, de 3 (três) meses a 1 (um) ano, e multa"; and its paragraph 1 reads: "Na mesma pena incorre quem produz, oferece, distribui, vende ou difunde dispositivo ou programa de computador com o intuito de permitir a prática da conduta definida no caput" ([Senado Federal, Lei 12.737/2012](https://legis.senado.leg.br/norma/588113/publicacao/15773241)). Article 154-B makes the offence prosecutable only on the victim's complaint, "salvo se o crime é cometido contra a administração pública" or against concessionaires of public services (same source).

Lei 14.155 of 27 May 2021 rewrote the caput. The offence is now to "Invadir dispositivo informático de uso alheio, conectado ou não à rede de computadores, com o fim de obter, adulterar ou destruir dados ou informações sem autorização expressa ou tácita do usuário do dispositivo ou de instalar vulnerabilidades para obter vantagem ilícita", punished with "reclusão, de 1 (um) a 4 (quatro) anos, e multa"; the requirement of breaking a security mechanism disappeared, the increase for economic loss became one third to two thirds, and the aggravated form in paragraph 3 rose to two to five years ([Diário Oficial da União, Lei 14.155/2021](https://www.in.gov.br/en/web/dou/-/lei-n-14.155-de-27-de-maio-de-2021-322698993)). Lei 14.155 did not touch paragraph 1, which still applies "a mesma pena" as the caput; on a plain reading of the two texts, the device offence now carries the caput's new penalty, a point on which no court decision was read for this article.

Three features of the text matter for anyone who owns tools. First, paragraph 1 names the "dispositivo" as well as the computer program, and turns on "o intuito de permitir" an intrusion: the intent, not the object. Second, authorization is part of the offence: what is punished is intrusion "sem autorização expressa ou tácita do usuário do dispositivo", which is why a written authorization is the core of any lawful test in Brazil. Third, intercepting communications is a different and older crime: article 10 of Lei 9.296 of 24 July 1996, as amended in 2019, punishes whoever would "realizar interceptação de comunicações telefônicas, de informática ou telemática ... sem autorização judicial ou com objetivos não autorizados em lei" with two to four years ([Câmara dos Deputados, Lei 9.296/1996, updated text](https://www2.camara.leg.br/legin/fed/lei/1996/lei-9296-24-julho-1996-349049-normaatualizada-pl.html)). The [part on keystroke injection](/learn/keystroke-injection-rubber-ducky-bash-bunny-badusb-and-the-om-g-cable) and the article on [bug bounties and vulnerability disclosure](/learn/bug-bounties-and-vulnerability-disclosure) discuss how researchers live with these texts; the article on [Brazilian cybercrime from boleto to Pix](/learn/brazilian-cybercrime-from-boleto-to-pix) shows what the fraud provisions of Lei 14.155 were written against.

### The Budapest Convention: article 6

Brazil is now bound by the Council of Europe's Convention on Cybercrime. Decreto 11.491 of 12 April 2023 promulgated it, reciting that Brazil "firmou a Convenção sobre o Crime Cibernético, em Budapeste, em 23 de novembro de 2001", that Congress approved it by Decreto Legislativo 37 of 16 December 2021, that the instrument of ratification was deposited "em 30 de novembro de 2022", and that it entered into force for Brazil "em 1º de março de 2023" ([Câmara dos Deputados, Decreto 11.491/2023, original text](https://www2.camara.leg.br/legin/fed/decret/2023/decreto-11491-12-abril-2023-794051-publicacaooriginal-167576-pe.html)). The 2001 date is the date of the Convention's text, opened for signature in Budapest; Brazil's own commitment came with the 2022 ratification.

Article 6, "Uso indevido de aparelhagem" in the official Portuguese text (misuse of devices), requires each party to criminalize, "quando dolosas e não autorizadas", the "produção, venda, aquisição para uso, importação, distribuição ou a disponibilização" of an "aparelho, incluindo um programa de computador, desenvolvido ou adaptado principalmente para o cometimento" of the Convention's offences of illegal access, interception, data interference and system interference, as well as passwords and access codes, and the possession of such items "com a intenção de usá-los para a prática" of those crimes (same source, article 6, paragraph 1). Paragraph 2 is the research clause: the article "não deve ser interpretado para estabelecer responsabilidade criminal" when these acts are not meant to commit those crimes, "como para, por exemplo, a realização de testes autorizados ou a proteção de um sistema de computador" (same source, article 6, paragraph 2). Paragraph 3 allows reservations, except for selling or distributing passwords and access codes (same source). The official English text could not be read for this article because the Council of Europe's site refused automated access; the summary above follows the Portuguese text promulgated by the decree.

## United States

### 18 U.S.C. 1029: access devices

The access-device statute is older than most of the tools in this series and was written with telephone fraud in mind; the [opening part](/learn/hacking-devices-from-the-blue-box-to-the-flipper-zero) quotes its telephone-instrument provisions. Its definitions are broad: an "access device" means "any card, plate, code, account number, electronic serial number, mobile identification number, personal identification number, or other telecommunications service, equipment, or instrument identifier, or other means of account access that can be used ... to obtain money, goods, services, or any other thing of value", and "device-making equipment" means "any equipment, mechanism, or impression designed or primarily used for making an access device or a counterfeit access device" ([Legal Information Institute, 18 U.S. Code 1029(e)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1029)). Subsection (a)(4) punishes whoever "knowingly, and with intent to defraud, produces, traffics in, has control or custody of, or possesses device-making equipment"; a "scanning receiver" is "a device or apparatus that can be used to intercept a wire or electronic communication" or a telephone identifier (same source). The research defence is narrow: only for subsection (a)(9), and not for producing or trafficking, "it is an affirmative defense ... that the conduct charged was engaged in for research or development in connection with a lawful purpose" (same source, subsection (g)(2)). Card cloners of the kind discussed in the [badges and cards part](/learn/badges-and-cards-rfid-nfc-the-proxmark-and-mifare) sit closest to this statute; intent to defraud is what makes possession a crime.

### 18 U.S.C. 1030: the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act

The CFAA governs use rather than ownership. Its history is in the glossary entry [CFAA](/glossary/cfaa); two recent events changed how it applies to security work. On 3 June 2021, in Van Buren v. United States, the Supreme Court held that an individual "exceeds authorized access" when he "accesses a computer with authorization but then obtains information located in particular areas of the computer", such as files, folders or databases, "that are off-limits to him", describing the question as "a gates-up-or-down inquiry" ([Supreme Court of the United States, Van Buren v. United States, No. 19-783, decided 3 June 2021](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/20pdf/19-783_k53l.pdf)). On 19 May 2022 the Department of Justice (DOJ) announced a charging policy that "for the first time directs that good-faith security research should not be charged", defining such research as "accessing a computer solely for purposes of good-faith testing, investigation, and/or correction of a security flaw or vulnerability", carried out "in a manner designed to avoid any harm to individuals or the public", and warning that "claiming to be conducting security research is not a free pass for those acting in bad faith" ([US Department of Justice, 19 May 2022; read again 17 November 2026 in a browser](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/department-justice-announces-new-policy-charging-cases-under-computer-fraud-and-abuse-act)). A charging policy binds federal prosecutors; it does not change the statute, state laws or civil liability.

### 18 U.S.C. 2512: interception devices

This is the US statute that most directly targets owning a type of device. It punishes whoever "manufactures, assembles, possesses, or sells any electronic, mechanical, or other device, knowing or having reason to know that the design of such device renders it primarily useful for the purpose of the surreptitious interception of wire, oral, or electronic communications", where interstate or foreign commerce is involved, and also mailing or advertising such devices, with up to five years ([Legal Information Institute, 18 U.S. Code 2512](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2512)). The exceptions cover communication providers "in the normal course of the business" and government officers or contractors in the normal course of their activities (same source, paragraph (2)). The words "primarily useful" and "surreptitious" carry the weight: a general-purpose radio, analyser or debugger is not designed primarily for covert interception, while a bug disguised as an everyday object can be.

### The FCC: equipment authorization

Radio products sold in the United States need FCC authorization under Part 2 of its rules, in Title 47 of the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR). "Marketing" includes sale, lease, advertising "or importation, shipment, or distribution for the purpose of selling", and also "the listing of regulated equipment on an online marketplace" combined with services such as warehousing or fulfilment, "even if that equipment is sold or offered for sale by a third-party seller"; the general rule is that "No person may market a radio frequency device unless the radio frequency device is authorized" ([eCFR, 47 CFR 2.803, read 17 November 2026](https://www.ecfr.gov/api/renderer/v1/content/enhanced/current/title-47?part=2&section=2.803)). Authorization takes two forms: a Supplier's Declaration of Conformity (SDoC), "a procedure where the responsible party ... makes measurements or completes other procedures found acceptable to the Commission" ([eCFR, 47 CFR 2.906](https://www.ecfr.gov/api/renderer/v1/content/enhanced/current/title-47?part=2&section=2.906)), and certification, "approved by the Commission or issued by a Telecommunication Certification Body (TCB)" on the applicant's test data ([eCFR, 47 CFR 2.907](https://www.ecfr.gov/api/renderer/v1/content/enhanced/current/title-47?part=2&section=2.907)). The Communications Act backs the rules: "No person shall manufacture, import, sell, offer for sale, or ship devices ... or use devices, which fail to comply with regulations" ([Legal Information Institute, 47 U.S. Code 302a(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/302a)).

For an individual, the import rule has a personal-use exception with limits. Radio devices may be imported if "Three or fewer radio frequency devices are being imported for the individual's personal use and are not intended for sale", but only in listed categories: unintentional radiators such as receivers and computers, consumer industrial, scientific and medical (ISM) equipment, "Intentional radiators subject to part 15 rules only if they can be used in client modes as specified in § 15.202", and licensed-service subscriber transmitters ([eCFR, 47 CFR 2.1204(a)(7)](https://www.ecfr.gov/api/renderer/v1/content/enhanced/current/title-47?part=2&section=2.1204)). A receive-only software-defined radio fits the first category; a transmitter without an FCC grant does not obviously fit any, which is why the authorization status of a transmitting gadget matters before it crosses the border. The [radio part](/learn/radio-for-hackers-sdr-hackrf-rtl-sdr-and-rolling-codes) covers the Part 15 limits for remotes and similar transmitters.

### Jamming and deauthentication

The FCC is categorical about jammers: "the operation, marketing, sale, importation, distribution, or shipment of RF jammers is a violation of federal law", with "no exceptions for personal, commercial, educational, or vehicular use", and unauthorized users "may face substantial civil penalties, seizure of illegal equipment, and criminal sanctions including imprisonment" ([FCC, Jammer Enforcement, updated September 2026](https://www.fcc.gov/general/jammer-enforcement)); the Communications Act says that "No person shall willfully or maliciously interfere with or cause interference to any radio communications" of authorized stations ([Legal Information Institute, 47 U.S. Code 333](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/333)). The rule reaches Wi-Fi deauthentication, the technique described in the [WiFi Pineapple part](/learn/the-wifi-pineapple-rogue-access-points-and-the-karma-attack). In Enforcement Advisory 2015-01 of 27 January 2015, the FCC's Enforcement Bureau warned that "Wi-Fi blocking is prohibited" and recalled that Marriott International "deployed a Wi-Fi deauthentication protocol to deliberately block consumers" using their own hotspots and settled "by paying a civil penalty of $600,000" ([FCC, Enforcement Advisory No. 2015-01, DA 15-113, 27 January 2015](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-15-113A1_Rcd.pdf)). A deauthentication frame sent at someone else's network is interference in the FCC's view, whatever tool sends it.

### The copyright exemption for security research

Hardware research on one's own devices often means getting past software locks, which the anti-circumvention rule of the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA) forbids except under exemptions renewed every three years (glossary: [DMCA](/glossary/dmca)). The current exemption, in the rule of 28 October 2024, covers circumvention "on a lawfully acquired device or machine on which the computer program operates, or ... on a computer, computer system, or computer network on which the computer program operates with the authorization of the owner or operator", "solely for the purpose of good-faith security research", and defines that research as testing, investigation or correction of a flaw "carried out in an environment designed to avoid any harm to individuals or the public" ([eCFR, 37 CFR 201.40(b)(18), as amended at 89 FR 85446, 28 October 2024](https://www.ecfr.gov/api/renderer/v1/content/enhanced/current/title-37?part=201&section=201.40)). The same paragraph warns that such research "may nevertheless incur liability under other applicable laws, including without limitation the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act", and that the exemption "is not a safe harbor from, or defense to, liability under other applicable laws" (same source).

### Export controls

Some tools are also export-controlled. "In 2013, the Wassenaar Arrangement (WA) added cybersecurity items" to its control lists, and the US Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) implemented the arrangement's 2013 decision, as amended in 2017, in an interim final rule published on 21 October 2021 and effective on 19 January 2022, creating a licence exception called Authorized Cybersecurity Exports ([Federal Register, 86 FR 58205, 21 October 2021, Information Security Controls: Cybersecurity Items](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2021-10-21/pdf/2021-22774.pdf)). Hak5's Code of Ethics says its products are "only available to BIS recognized license exception ENC favorable treatment countries pursuant to US 15 CFR Supplement No 3 to Part 740" ([Hak5, Code of Ethics](https://hak5.org/pages/code-of-ethics)), a list of 36 countries in which Brazil does not appear ([eCFR, 15 CFR Part 740, Supplement No. 3](https://www.ecfr.gov/api/renderer/v1/content/enhanced/current/title-15?part=740&appendix=Supplement%20No.%203%20to%20Part%20740)); the [Hak5 part](/learn/hak5-from-a-video-show-to-a-hacking-gear-company) discusses how that sentence differs from the company's compliance page.

## United Kingdom

Section 3A of the Computer Misuse Act 1990, inserted by the Police and Justice Act 2006 and in force from 1 October 2007 in Scotland and 1 October 2008 elsewhere, creates three tool offences: making, adapting, supplying or offering to supply "any article intending it to be used to commit, or to assist in the commission of" an offence under sections 1, 3 or 3ZA; supplying or offering to supply an article "believing that it is likely to be used" for such an offence; and obtaining an article intending to use it or with a view to its supply for that purpose; "article" includes "any program or data held in electronic form", and the maximum on indictment is two years ([legislation.gov.uk, Computer Misuse Act 1990, section 3A](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1990/18/section/3A)).

The Crown Prosecution Service (CPS) guidance, updated on 5 February 2020 and 3 August 2023, explains how prosecutors read it. "The rationale behind the creation of this offence is the market in electronic malware or 'hacker tools'"; "The prosecution has to prove the defendant had the necessary intent. Possession alone is not an offence"; and for the "likely" limb, "mere suspicion is not enough" ([CPS, Computer Misuse Act, legal guidance](https://www.cps.gov.uk/legal-guidance/computer-misuse-act)). Prosecutors are told to ask whether the article was "developed primarily, deliberately and for the sole purpose of committing a CMA offence", whether it is "available on a wide scale commercial basis and sold through legitimate channels", whether it is "widely used for legitimate purposes" with "a substantial installation base", and whether it was "circulated to a closed and vetted list of IT security professionals or was posted openly" (same source). Those questions are as close as any official text comes to a test for dual-use tools.

## European Union

### Directive 2013/40/EU: tools and intent

The EU's criminal-law instrument requires member states to punish "the intentional production, sale, procurement for use, import, distribution or otherwise making available" of a tool "without right and with the intention that it be used to commit" the offences of illegal access, system and data interference and interception, the tools being "a computer programme, designed or adapted primarily for the purpose of committing" those offences and passwords or access codes ([EU, Directive 2013/40/EU of 12 August 2013 on attacks against information systems, article 7](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32013L0040)). Recital 16 acknowledges that a tool "suitable or particularly suitable" for an offence may have been "produced for a legitimate purpose", and requires "a direct intent" to avoid criminalizing tools made "to test the reliability of information technology products or the security of information systems"; recital 17 excludes liability "in the case of mandated testing or protection of information systems, such as where a person is assigned by a company or vendor to test the strength of its security system" (same source). Article 7 speaks of computer programmes and passwords, not hardware; member states transpose it in their own words, and a hardware tool is reached through national law or through the Convention's broader "aparelho", "device" in English.

### The Radio Equipment Directive

The Radio Equipment Directive (RED), Directive 2014/53/EU, "establishes a regulatory framework for the making available on the market and putting into service in the Union of radio equipment" ([EU, Directive 2014/53/EU, article 1](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32014L0053)). Its essential requirements protect health, safety and electromagnetic compatibility, require that equipment "effectively uses and supports the efficient use of radio spectrum in order to avoid harmful interference", and allow the Commission to add, by category, requirements that equipment "does not harm the network", "incorporates safeguards" for personal data and privacy and "supports certain features ensuring protection from fraud" (same source, article 3). Manufacturers must carry out the conformity assessment, "draw up an EU declaration of conformity and affix the CE marking" (same source, article 10). Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2022/30 switched on those three cybersecurity requirements for internet-connected radio equipment, equipment that processes personal data and equipment that can transfer money ([EU, Delegated Regulation (EU) 2022/30, article 1](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R0030)); its date of application was moved by Delegated Regulation (EU) 2023/2444 to "1 August 2025" ([EU, Delegated Regulation (EU) 2023/2444 of 20 July 2023](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32023R2444)). The Flipper's own compliance page lists EU declarations of conformity ([Flipper, Compliance](https://flipper.net/pages/compliance)): in the EU, a hacking gadget is first of all a radio product like any other.

### The Cyber Resilience Act

The Cyber Resilience Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 of 23 October 2024, adds horizontal cybersecurity requirements for products with digital elements: they must be made available "without known exploitable vulnerabilities", "with a secure by default configuration", and be "designed, developed and produced to limit attack surfaces, including external interfaces"; the regulation applies from 11 December 2027, with its article 14 reporting obligations from 11 September 2026 ([EU, Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, Annex I and article 71](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R2847)). For makers of security tools it is a product obligation like any other; for buyers of the products those tools test, it is the reason manufacturers now close debug ports, as the [hardware bench part](/learn/the-hardware-hackers-bench-bus-pirate-jtag-uart-and-chipwhisperer) explains.

## Canada

### The 2024 announcement and what followed

On 8 February 2024 the federal government's National Summit on Combatting Auto Theft announced that it was "Pursuing all avenues to ban devices used to steal vehicles by copying the wireless signals for remote keyless entry", "such as the Flipper Zero, which would allow for the removal of those devices from the Canadian marketplace" ([Public Safety Canada, 8 February 2024; read again 17 November 2026 in a browser](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-safety-canada/news/2024/02/government-of-canada-hosts-national-summit-on-combatting-auto-theft.html)). The industry minister, François-Philippe Champagne, posted that "we are banning the importation, sale and use of consumer hacking devices, like flippers, used to commit these crimes", and Flipper Devices' chief operating officer, Alex Kulagin, replied that the device "can't be used to hijack any car, specifically the ones produced after the 1990s, since their security systems have rolling codes" ([BleepingComputer, 9 February 2024](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/canada-wants-to-ban-the-flipper-zero-to-stop-surge-in-car-thefts/)). By 20 March 2024 Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada (ISED) told PCMag that "The intent is to move forward with measures to restrict the use of such devices to legitimate actors only" ([PCMag, 20 March 2024](https://www.pcmag.com/news/canada-walks-back-ban-of-flipper-zero-targets-illegitimate-use-cases)); the EFF called the restriction "troublesome for security researchers" ([EFF, 28 March 2024](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2024/03/restricting-flipper-zero-accountability-approach-security-canadian-government)).

Parliament then gave the minister a power. Bill C-69, the budget implementation act, amended the Radiocommunication Act ([Department of Justice Canada, Bill C-69, Division 36](https://www.justice.gc.ca/eng/trans/bm-mb/other-autre/c69/division36-section36.html)) and "received royal assent on June 20" 2024, and an ISED consultation "concluded in August 2024", according to an ISED briefing note received on 20 September 2024, which adds that the government "remains committed to ensuring that legitimate uses for these devices will be maintained" ([ISED, Question Period Note ISI-2024-QP-00053](https://search.open.canada.ca/qpnotes/record/ic%2CISI-2024-QP-00053)). The consultation paper itself is dated May 2024, with reply comments accepted "until August 12, 2024" ([ISED, Consultation Relating to the Use of Radiocommunication Devices in Auto Theft](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/spectrum-management-telecommunications/en/node/2103)). The amended Act, in the consolidation current to 29 October 2026 and last amended on 20 June 2024, now says that "no person shall manufacture, import, distribute, lease, offer for sale, sell or possess" any apparatus, device or component "specified by the Minister under subsection 5.01(1)", and section 5.01 lets the minister specify by order any device "likely used, or intended to be used, to intercept and make use of, or to intercept and divulge, any radiocommunication for a purpose that the Minister specifies", and exempt persons or classes of persons on conditions ([Justice Laws Website, Radiocommunication Act, subsections 4(5) and 5.01](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/r-2/FullText.html)). On 8 March 2025 the government still described itself as analysing the consultation feedback ([Public Safety Canada, 8 March 2025, read through a fetch tool](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-safety-canada/news/2025/03/government-of-canada-reports-on-progress-to-reduce-auto-theft.html)). No order under subsection 5.01(1) was found in the consolidated regulations or the other sources read on 17 November 2026: the power exists; its use, if any, was not found.

### The Criminal Code

Canada's general tool offence predates the Flipper. Section 342.2 punishes whoever, "without lawful excuse, makes, possesses, sells, offers for sale, imports, obtains for use, distributes or makes available a device that is designed or adapted primarily to commit an offence under section 342.1 or 430, knowing that the device has been used or is intended to be used to commit such an offence", with up to two years on indictment, and "device" includes a component and a computer program ([Justice Laws Website, Criminal Code, section 342.2](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/c-46/section-342.2.html)). Interception devices have their own rule: section 191 punishes possessing, selling or buying a device "knowing that its design renders it primarily useful for surreptitious interception of private communications", with exemptions for police, authorized interceptions, Crown servants and holders of a licence from the Minister of Public Safety ([Justice Laws Website, Criminal Code, section 191](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/c-46/section-191.html)).

### Jammers in Canada

The Radiocommunication Act is absolute about jammers: "No person shall install, use, possess, manufacture, import, distribute, lease, offer for sale or sell a jammer" ([Justice Laws Website, Radiocommunication Act, subsection 4(4)](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/r-2/FullText.html)). Exemptions come by ministerial order: a 2025 order lets correctional services use jammers in prisons under conditions ([Justice Laws Website, SOR/2025-63, Radiocommunication Act Exemption Order (Jammers, Certain Correctional Services)](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/SOR-2025-63/page-1.html)), and a proposed order published on 10 October 2026 would exempt employees of certain law enforcement agencies, noting that "The Radiocommunication Act (the RA) prohibits the use of jammers and other jammer-related activities in Canada" ([Canada Gazette, Part I, Volume 160, Number 41, 10 October 2026](https://gazette.gc.ca/rp-pr/p1/2026/2026-10-10/html/reg2-eng.html)).

## Rules of engagement and written authorization

Every regime above turns, in the end, on authorization or intent. A tester's protection is a document that proves both, signed before the work starts. Three public frameworks describe what that document contains: a guide from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), the Penetration Testing Execution Standard (PTES) and Brazil's federal framework for public bodies.

### NIST SP 800-115

NIST's Special Publication 800-115, Technical Guide to Information Security Testing and Assessment, of September 2008, distinguishes an assessment plan from rules of engagement (ROE): "The ROE contains the same information in an assessment plan, and also addresses testing activities that are usually prohibited by the organization", such as "issuing attacks to compromise systems", and "provides authorization for the assessors to conduct such activities" ([NIST SP 800-115, section 6.5](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-115.pdf)). External assessors should ensure "that proper authority is granted, and maintaining a signed copy of the assessment plan", and legal advisors should "always be involved for intrusive tests such as penetration testing" (same source, sections 6.4 and 6.6).

Its Appendix B is a rules-of-engagement template, and three of its sections speak directly to hardware. Section 2.4, Test Equipment, asks the team to identify its equipment and the method of telling the organization's systems from the testers', and says "tools authorized for use on the network should be identified" (same source, Appendix B). Section 4 requires "authorized and unauthorized IP addresses or other distinguishing identifiers" and an "exclude list" of systems not to be touched. Section 5.1 contains, in a sentence, what practitioners call the get-out-of-jail letter: if physical security is in scope, "procedures should be determined and a form", with "appropriate signatures and contact information", should be "generated for the test team to show to law enforcement or onsite security personnel in the event that they are questioned" (same source, Appendix B, section 5.1). Section 7 requires a signature page on which, "At a minimum, the test team leader and the organization's senior management (CSO, CISO, CIO, etc.) should sign", meaning the chief security, information security or information officer (same source).

### PTES

PTES calls the central document "Permission to Test": it "states the scope and contains a signature which acknowledges awareness of the activities of the testers", and "It is critical that testing does not begin until this document is signed by the customer" ([PTES, Pre-engagement Interactions, PTES Team, 2016](https://pentest-standard.readthedocs.io/en/latest/preengagement_interactions.html)). It warns about the gap that hardware and cloud testing often fall into: "while permission may have been granted by the client, they do not speak for their third party providers. Thus, permission must be obtained from them as well" (same source). Its scoping questions for a physical test include "Is the use of lock picks or bump keys allowed? (also consider local laws)", and its legal section says that "Some activities common in penetration tests may violate local laws", giving the example that "any VOIP calls captured in the course of the penetration test may be considered wiretapping in some areas" (same source), VOIP being voice over Internet Protocol.

### Brazil's federal framework

Brazil's federal information-security framework for public bodies says the same in Portuguese. The guide to the Programa de Privacidade e Segurança da Informação (PPSI 2.0), version 1.2 of 30 January 2026, published by the Ministério da Gestão e da Inovação em Serviços Públicos, warns that penetration tests "são complexos, caros e apresentam riscos" and can corrupt data, and states that "Cada organização deve definir um escopo claro e regras de engajamento", with rules covering "horários, duração e abordagem" and a person responsible for incidents; its control 18.1 asks each body to maintain a pentest programme that sets scope, including "controles de instalações físicas", frequency, limitations "como horários aceitáveis e tipos de ataque excluídos" and contact information, citing Decreto 12.573 of 2025 ([Governo Digital, Guia do Framework de Privacidade e Segurança da Informação, PPSI 2.0, version 1.2, 30 January 2026, control 18](https://www.gov.br/governodigital/pt-br/privacidade-e-seguranca/ppsi-2.0/arquivos/guiafwppsi2-0-v1-2-11062026.pdf)).

### A case: Dallas County, Iowa, 2019

The best-known failure of authorization involved a physical test. On 11 September 2019 two employees of Coalfire, a security company "hired by the state of Iowa to test the physical and network security of its judicial system", were arrested at the Dallas County courthouse, charged with burglary and held on US$100,000 bail; their contract with the State Court Administration let them impersonate staff, "tailgate" employees and enter restricted areas, but not "subvert alarm systems, force-open doors" ([Krebs on Security, 31 January 2020](https://krebsonsecurity.com/2020/01/iowa-prosecutors-drop-charges-against-men-hired-to-test-their-security/)). When an alarm sounded they waited for the deputies and "produced an authorization letter detailing the job they'd been hired to do"; the sheriff doubted it, one contact on the letter did not answer, and the courthouse turned out to belong to the county, not the state; Coalfire's chief executive, Tom McAndrew, said later that his company would likely insist that "local, state and even federal law enforcement be informed in advance of any penetration tests", and that "there's some things the state owns and some things the county owns, and that was something we weren't aware of" (same source). The charges, reduced to trespass, were dropped on 30 January 2020, the county attorney saying that the interests of justice "are best served by all the parties working together to ensure that there is clear communication" (same source). The lesson in the documents matches PTES's warning: permission must come from whoever actually owns the system or the building, and the people named in the letter must answer the phone.

## What remains unsettled

Several questions are open in the sources read. Brazil's article 60, II, lets Anatel refuse a product because of its possible misuse, while article 154-A § 1 punishes only those who make or sell a device "com o intuito" of enabling an intrusion: the regulator's product test and the criminal law's intent test can point in different directions, and the 2023 Flipper decisions show the first prevailing at the border. Canada has a power to restrict devices by order and no order was found. The United States protects good-faith research through a prosecutors' policy and a copyright exemption, not through the text of the CFAA. And everywhere, the line between a test and a crime is a document that many small engagements never write down.

## What to remember

- Three bodies of law apply to a hacking gadget: radio and product rules (may it be sold and imported?), criminal law on tools (intent or primary design), and the law of access and interception (authorization to use it on a system).
- Brazil: homologation is mandatory for use and sale (Lei 9.472 art. 162 § 2; Res. 715 art. 55); individuals may homologate for own use by declaration of conformity, but Anatel refuses products that lend themselves to unlawful ends (art. 60, II), as with the Flipper Zero from 2023; since August 2025 marketplaces are jointly liable; the Receita retains non-homologated imports.
- Remessa Conforme (August 2023) changed taxation, and Lei 14.902 (June 2024) set 20 per cent import tax up to US$50; tax status does not replace homologation.
- Art. 154-A § 1 punishes producing, offering, distributing, selling or spreading a device or program "com o intuito de permitir" an intrusion; Lei 14.155 (2021) raised the caput's penalty to one to four years; the Budapest Convention, in force for Brazil since 1 March 2023, excludes authorized testing in article 6(2).
- United States: 1029 (intent to defraud), 2512 (devices primarily useful for surreptitious interception), the CFAA as narrowed by Van Buren (2021) and the DOJ's 2022 policy; FCC authorization, a three-device personal-import exception with limits, and an absolute ban on jamming.
- UK: CMA s. 3A requires intent or belief of likely use; possession alone is not an offence. EU: Directive 2013/40 requires direct intent; the RED's cybersecurity requirements apply from 1 August 2025 and the Cyber Resilience Act from 11 December 2027.
- Canada: the 2024 ban became a ministerial power (Radiocommunication Act s. 5.01, June 2024) with no order found by November 2026; Criminal Code s. 342.2 and s. 191 already covered tools and interception devices.
- Rules of engagement (NIST SP 800-115 Appendix B, PTES, PPSI 2.0) are the tester's proof of authorization; Dallas County, 2019, shows why the signer must own what is tested.
