On some nights many people look up and see the same thing, or believe they do. A pilot reports bright objects over a mountain range; within two weeks newspapers count sightings in dozens of states; a government opens a file, then another; decades later the files are still being argued over. Researchers call a cluster of reports in a short time a "flap" or a wave. Waves of sightings are a form of collective belief that is easy to misread in both directions: as proof that something extraordinary is in the sky, or as proof that the witnesses were foolish. The records show neither. They show ordinary people and trained observers reporting real lights and real radar returns, most of which were later identified, a minority that stayed unexplained for lack of data, governments that sometimes misled the public for reasons of their own, and a press and an audience that made each wave larger than the last. This part of the series on [mass hysteria and collective belief](/learn/mass-hysteria-how-groups-come-to-believe-anything) reads those records. After reading, you can say what makes a flap grow and fade, which explanations rest on evidence and which on missing evidence, and where someone steered the belief. The Brazilian episode of Colares in 1977 and the Air Force's Operação Prato are told in [Phantom attackers and contested outbreaks](/learn/phantom-attackers-and-contested-outbreaks); the laboratory finding that a fixed light in a dark room seems to move, and that groups converge on how far, is in [Conformity, cascades and contagion](/learn/conformity-cascades-and-contagion-the-mechanisms).

## The words: saucer, flap, UFO, UAP

The words changed with the institutions that used them. On 26 June 1947 an Associated Press (AP) dispatch from Pendleton, Oregon, printed in Washington's Evening Star, described "nine mysterious objects" reported over Western Washington, "shiny, flat objects", moving with a peculiar weaving motion "like the tail of a kite", without yet using the word saucer ([Evening Star, 26 June 1947, page 5, Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045462/1947-06-26/ed-1/?sp=5)). Ten days later the same paper's front page carried the headline "Hundreds in 31 States Report Seeing Weird 'Flying Saucers'" ([Evening Star, 6 July 1947, page 1](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045462/1947-07-06/ed-1/?sp=1)).

The University of Colorado study commissioned by the US Air Force, known as the Condon report (1969), defined an unidentified flying object (UFO) not as a craft but as "the stimulus for a report made by one or more individuals of something seen in the sky (or an object thought to be capable of flight but seen when landed on the earth) which the observer could not identify", and named the clusters: "Such a concentration of reports in a small region in a short time is called a 'flap.'" ([Condon report, volume 1, 1969, Defense Technical Information Center edition, Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_AD0680975)). In 2021 the Office of the Director of National Intelligence (ODNI) adopted "unidentified aerial phenomena" (UAP) for its preliminary assessment ([ODNI, Preliminary Assessment: Unidentified Aerial Phenomena, 25 June 2021](https://archive.dni.gov/files/ODNI/documents/assessments/Prelimary-Assessment-UAP-20210625.pdf)); by 2023 the US government's annual reports spoke of "unidentified anomalous phenomena", a term that also covers objects reported in space and under water ([ODNI and Department of Defense, Fiscal Year 2023 Consolidated Annual Report on Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena, October 2023](https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/UNCLASSIFIED-FY23_Consolidated_Annual_Report_on_UAP-Oct_25_2023_1236.pdf)). Brazil's Arquivo Nacional (National Archives) explains that "OVNI" (objeto voador não identificado) designates any flying object of origin not immediately identified, which can include satellites, drones, balloons or natural phenomena, and does not necessarily mean flying saucers or extraterrestrials ([Arquivo Nacional, Conheça o fundo sobre OVNIs do Arquivo Nacional, 13 September 2018, updated 28 October 2020](https://www.gov.br/arquivonacional/pt-br/canais_atendimento/imprensa/noticias/conheca-o-fundo-sobre-ovnis-do-arquivo-nacional)).

Each word carries an assumption. "Saucer" suggests a craft; "UFO" says only that someone could not identify what they saw; "UAP" and "anomalous" were chosen partly to make reporting respectable for pilots. Which word an institution uses tells the reader what it was trying to count.

## Before 1947: the airships of 1896

Waves did not begin with aviation. In November 1896 the San Francisco Call reported from Sacramento that "The one topic of conversation in this city to-day" was the reported appearance of an airship, with hundreds of people who "are extremely skeptical" and hundreds of others who swore they had seen its searchlight and heard the voices of its crew; the paper's local representative said he had interviewed "scores of reputable citizens" whose accounts agreed ([San Francisco Call, 19 November 1896, page 1, Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn85066387/1896-11-19/ed-1/?sp=1)). Five days later the paper listed among new witnesses the city's mayor, Adolph Sutro, a railway agent, its own advertising manager and a high-school professor, who "all tell practically the same story": a light high overhead that moved very fast, though not all in the same direction; a dentist rumoured to have invented a flying machine told the paper he knew nothing about an airship, while conceding that if he had one he would say nothing until it was patented ([San Francisco Call, 24 November 1896, page 1](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn85066387/1896-11-24/ed-1/?sp=1)).

The pattern of the next century is already there: a striking report, a newspaper that seeks out more witnesses, respectable names that make the story credible, an inventor who neither confirms nor denies, and descriptions that agree on the light and disagree on the details.

## Kenneth Arnold and the summer of 1947

### The first report

According to a history published by the Central Intelligence Agency (CIA), "The first report of a flying saucer over the United States came on 24 June 1947, when Kenneth Arnold, a private pilot and reputable businessman, while looking for a downed plane sighted nine disk-shaped objects near Mt. Rainier, Washington, traveling at an estimated speed of over 1,000 mph"; it was "followed by a flood of additional sightings, including reports from military and civilian pilots and air traffic controllers all over the United States" ([Gerald K. Haines, CIA's Role in the Study of UFOs, 1947-90, Studies in Intelligence, 1997](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/cia-role-study-UFOs.pdf)). The AP dispatch of 26 June gave the reaction of the day: "Army and Civil Aeronautics Administration spokesmen expressed skepticism", while Arnold "clung, however, to his story" of objects as big as a DC-4 passenger plane; an Army spokesman said that as far as he knew "nothing flies that fast except a V-2 rocket" ([Evening Star, 26 June 1947](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045462/1947-06-26/ed-1/?sp=5)).

### Ten days, thirty-one states, forty-one states

The wave that followed can be read day by day in one newspaper. On 6 July the AP reported "flying saucers" seen in 31 states by hundreds of people; "Official Government sources took a 'Let's see one' stand", two Chicago astronomers said the discs were probably "man-made", the chairman of the Atomic Energy Commission told the Denver Post they had nothing to do with atomic experiments, and the paper noted that belief in the saucers, widely laughed off at their first appearance, had grown as hundreds of observers, many of them trained flyers, reported seeing them ([Evening Star, 6 July 1947, page 1](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045462/1947-07-06/ed-1/?sp=1)). On 7 July an Army Air Forces (AAF) public relations officer, Captain Tom Brown, said that the AAF believed "there's something to this" and that the tales were too widespread to be groundless; aircraft, including a jet, were alerted along the West Coast to chase them, and the Navy said it had checked all its facilities and found nothing it was sending aloft that could explain them ([Evening Star, 7 July 1947, page 1](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045462/1947-07-07/ed-1/?sp=1)).

By 8 July the count stood at "41 States had joined the disc brigade since the objects first were reported June 25". The same page offered the first ordinary explanation from a military airman, that four jet fighters cruising in formation could look, near the horizon, like "a flat, circular object", and the first dismissive one, that the nation was suffering "a touch of mid-summer madness accompanied by spots before the eyes"; the AAF assigned intelligence officers to sift the reports, and the White House press secretary told reporters that a juggler had telegraphed President Truman that the saucers were his, thrown too high ([Evening Star, 8 July 1947, page 2](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045462/1947-07-08/ed-1/?sp=2)). The Condon report's bibliography lists a Gallup release of 15 August 1947 titled "Nine out of Ten People Heard of Flying Saucers" ([Condon report, volume 1, references](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_AD0680975)); the release itself was not found for this part.

### Roswell: a balloon, a cover story and a fifty-year belief

The best-known episode of that July happened in New Mexico. The National Archives (NARA) says it has found no documentation about the 1947 incident at Roswell among the Project Blue Book records. In 1994, at the request of Congressman Steven Schiff, the General Accounting Office (GAO) audited the records, and the Air Force searched its own files and interviewed witnesses after releasing them from security obligations; its 1995 publication, The Roswell Report: Fact vs. Fiction in the New Mexico Desert, concluded that the material recovered was "consistent with a balloon device of the type used in a then-classified project" and that "No records indicated or even hinted at the recovery of 'alien' bodies or extraterrestrial materials" ([National Archives, Project BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects, page reviewed 25 June 2024](https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos)). <Claim status="disputed" by="by pro-UFO researchers, against the Air Force's 1995 report">That a spacecraft and its occupants were recovered and the fact kept from the public</Claim> is the claim the archives page records as the researchers' position. Gallup noted in 2019 that the West, home to Roswell and the Nevada base known as Area 51, led the country in the belief that some UFOs are alien spacecraft: 40% of its residents, against 33% nationally ([Gallup, Americans Skeptical of UFOs, but Say Government Knows More, 6 September 2019](https://news.gallup.com/poll/266441/americans-skeptical-ufos-say-govt-knows.aspx)).

## From Sign to Blue Book: the Air Force counts the sky

### Sign and Grudge

In 1948 General Nathan Twining set up Project Sign, initially named Project Saucer, "on the premise that UFOs might be real and of national security concern"; the Air Force soon concluded that UFOs were real but easily explained, and its report found that almost all sightings stemmed from one or more of three causes: "mass hysteria and hallucination, hoax, or misinterpretation of known objects" ([Haines, 1997](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/cia-role-study-UFOs.pdf)). Its successor, Project Grudge, "tried to alleviate public anxiety over UFOs via a public relations campaign designed to persuade the public that UFOs constituted nothing unusual or extraordinary", and recommended reducing the project because "the very existence of Air Force official interest encouraged people to believe in UFOs and contributed to a war hysteria atmosphere"; its termination was announced on 27 December 1949 (same). The officials who ran the projects had reached, in 1949, the hypothesis this part examines: that official attention itself fed the reports.

### July 1952: the Washington flap

Project Blue Book was created in 1952. That July radar scopes at Washington National Airport and Andrews Air Force Base tracked unexplained blips on 19 and 20 July and again on 27 July; interceptors found nothing; the incidents "caused headlines across the country", the White House asked what was happening, and the Air Force offered the explanation that the blips might be the result of temperature inversions, which a Civil Aeronautics Administration investigation later confirmed as common ([Haines, 1997](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/cia-role-study-UFOs.pdf)). The Evening Star of 30 July reported the Pentagon press conference of the day before, at which Major General John A. Samford, chief of Air Force intelligence, said: "In my own mind, I am satisfied that the recent Washington sightings were caused by temperature inversion, but it is my obligation to learn more about it", adding that the task was to give "adequate attention, but not frantic attention" to such reports and that "We have found nothing associated with the sighting to show them vehicles, materials or missiles directed against the United States"; another radar blip appeared that very night, and an aircraft sent to look found nothing ([Evening Star, 30 July 1952, page 1](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045462/1952-07-30/ed-1/?sp=1)). The day before, the paper had suggested that some saucers might be weather balloons, released four times a day and carrying a light at night bright enough to stand out among the stars ([Evening Star, 29 July 1952, page 1](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045462/1952-07-29/ed-1/?sp=1)). The Condon report later used this episode to define the word: the reports near Washington National Airport over several days in July 1952 were its example of a "flap" ([Condon report, volume 1](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_AD0680975)).

### The Robertson panel, January 1953: fear of hysteria, a plan to debunk

The 1952 wave alarmed the CIA. Its study group, according to the agency's own historian, "believed that the Soviets could use UFO reports to touch off mass hysteria and panic in the United States" and might use sightings to overload the air warning system so that it could not distinguish real targets from phantom UFOs ([Haines, 1997](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/cia-role-study-UFOs.pdf)). A panel of scientists chaired by the physicist H. P. Robertson met from 14 to 17 January 1953, judged that reasonable explanations could be suggested for most if not all sightings (film from Tremonton, Utah, showed sunlight reflecting off seagulls; film from Great Falls, Montana, reflections from two interceptors), and concluded unanimously that there was no evidence of a direct threat to national security or of extraterrestrial objects. It also found that continued emphasis on UFO reporting might threaten "the orderly functioning of the government by clogging the channels of communication with irrelevant reports and by inducing hysterical mass behavior harmful to constituted authority" (same).

Its remedy was a campaign. The panel "recommended that the National Security Council debunk UFO reports and institute a policy of public education to reassure the public of the lack of evidence behind UFOs. It suggested using the mass media, advertising, business clubs, schools, and even the Disney corporation to get the message across", and, "Reporting at the height of McCarthyism", that private UFO groups be monitored for subversive activities (same). The CIA kept its sponsorship of the panel secret, an attitude that Haines says "would later cause the Agency major problems relating to its credibility" (same). These are the CIA historian's words, summarizing the panel's report.

### Spy planes seen as saucers

The largest single explanation in the record was secret. From 1955 the CIA's U-2 reconnaissance aircraft flew above 60,000 feet, when most airliners flew between 10,000 and 20,000 feet; commercial pilots and controllers began reporting many more UFOs, and Blue Book investigators, aware of the flights, "tried to explain away such sightings by linking them to natural phenomena such as ice crystals and temperature inversions" ([Haines, 1997](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/cia-role-study-UFOs.pdf)). Haines wrote that, "According to later estimates from CIA officials who worked on the U2 project and the OXCART (SR-71, or Blackbird) project, over half of all UFO reports from the late 1950s through the 1960s were accounted for by manned reconnaissance flights (namely the U-2) over the United States", and that "This led the Air Force to make misleading and deceptive statements to the public in order to allay public fears and to protect an extraordinarily sensitive national security project"; the share the Air Force considered unexplained fell to 5.9% in 1955 and 4% in 1956 (same). The estimate is a recollection of former officials, not a count of case files, and Haines gives it as such.

### What the public believed

Gallup asked in April 1966 whether Americans had ever seen anything they thought was a "flying saucer": 5% said yes, 96% had heard or read about flying saucers, and 46% of those thought they were "something real" rather than imagination; asked what they were, 7% said vehicles from outer space and 9% Air Force tests or experimental projects ([Gallup Vault, Eyewitnesses to "Flying Saucers", 2016](https://news.gallup.com/vault/190592/gallup-vault-eyewitnesses-flying-saucers.aspx)). From 1973 to 2019 Gallup found between 47% and 57% saying UFO sightings involved "something real", a wording that did not separate alien craft from drones or aircraft ([Gallup, Larger Minority in U.S. Says Some UFOs Are Alien Spacecraft, 20 August 2021](https://news.gallup.com/poll/353420/larger-minority-says-ufos-alien-spacecraft.aspx)).

### The end of Blue Book

In March 1966 an ad hoc committee of the Air Force Scientific Advisory Board chaired by Brian O'Brien reaffirmed that a few unexplained reports posed no security threat but suggested a more detailed scientific study; the Air Force Office of Scientific Research wrote to the University of Colorado on 31 August 1966, and the contract, announced on 7 October 1966, provided $313,000 for the first fifteen months ([Condon report, volume 1](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_AD0680975)). On 17 December 1969 the Secretary of the Air Force announced the end of Blue Book: from 1947 to 1969, 12,618 sightings had been reported to the project, and 701 remained "Unidentified"; the decision rested on the Colorado report, a review of it by the National Academy of Sciences, earlier studies and the Air Force's own experience, and the Air Force's conclusions were that no UFO had given any indication of threat to national security, that there was no evidence that the "unidentified" sightings represented technology beyond present-day science, and no evidence that they were extraterrestrial vehicles ([Air Force fact sheet of January 1985, reproduced by the National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos)). The same fact sheet states that "There are not now nor ever have been, any extraterrestrial visitors or equipment on Wright-Patterson Air Force Base" (same).

## The Condon report, 1969

### What it concluded

Edward U. Condon's summary opened: "Our general conclusion is that nothing has come from the study of UFOs in the past 21 years that has added to scientific knowledge. Careful consideration of the record as it is available to us leads us to conclude that further extensive study of UFOs probably cannot be justified in the expectation that science will be advanced thereby" ([Condon report, volume 1, conclusions and recommendations](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_AD0680975)). It nonetheless recommended that agencies consider "clearly defined, specific" research proposals on their merits, rejected the charge of secrecy ("We have no evidence of secrecy concerning UFO reports"), said the subject had been "widely misrepresented to the public by a small number of individuals who have given sensationalized presentations in writings and public lectures", and recommended that teachers "refrain from giving students credit for school work based on their reading of the presently available UFO books and magazine articles" (same).

The report also named the questions it did not study. It suggested that "a content-analysis of press and television coverage of UFO reports might yield data of value both to the social scientist and the communications specialist", and that "the rigorous study of the beliefs" held without valid evidence by individuals and groups "might prove of scientific value to the social and behavioral sciences" ([Condon report, volume 1](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_AD0680975)).

### Why flaps happen, in the report's words

On waves, the report was explicit and modest: "No quantitative study of this is available, but we believe that the clustering tendency is partly due to changing amounts of attention devoted to the subject by the news media. Publicity for some reports stimulates more reports, both because people pay more attention to the sky at such a time, and because they are more likely to make a report of something which attracts their attention" ([Condon report, volume 1, section II](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_AD0680975)). Its example was a flap near Harrisburg, Pennsylvania, in the summer of 1967, "in part produced by the efforts of a local NICAP member working in close association with a reporter for the local afternoon newspaper who wrote an exciting UFO story for his paper almost daily"; the morning paper scarcely ever carried one, "from which we conclude that one editor's news is another's filler" (same; the National Investigations Committee on Aerial Phenomena, NICAP, was a private research group). The report also estimated, from its own survey and from Gallup's, that the number of Americans who had seen something they took for a UFO ranged from as few as one million to as many as five million (same).

### Its critics

The report did not end the argument among scientists. It quoted the atmospheric physicist James McDonald, who told the American Society of Newspaper Editors on 22 April 1967 that "There is, in my present opinion, no sensible alternative to the utterly shocking hypothesis that the UFOs are extraterrestrial probes from somewhere else" ([Condon report, volume 1](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_AD0680975)). Before the study began, J. Allen Hynek, the Air Force's scientific consultant on UFOs according to the report, had argued in Science that "UFO's Merit Scientific Study"; while it ran, the same journal reported internal conflict under the title "UFO Project: Trouble on the Ground" ([Hynek, Science 154, 21 October 1966, PubMed record](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=17751686); [Boffey, Science 161, 26 July 1968, PubMed record](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=17776734)). In 1970 the same journal published a paper by Warren applying a sociological theory, status inconsistency, to flying saucer sightings ([Warren, Science 170, 6 November 1970, PubMed record](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=5528429)), an early attempt to study the witnesses rather than the sky.

## How a flap grows and fades

The official records, read together, point to several mechanisms. None needs anyone to be lying or deluded; each turns ordinary perception into a count.

**Attention.** Publicity sends people outdoors to look and makes them more willing to report, as the Condon report wrote, and newspapers compete on the story, as in Harrisburg ([Condon report, volume 1](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_AD0680975)). The Evening Star's front pages of July 1947 show a story growing from 31 to 41 states in two days while official spokesmen alternately doubted it and said "there's something to this" ([Evening Star, 6 July 1947](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045462/1947-07-06/ed-1/?sp=1); [8 July 1947](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045462/1947-07-08/ed-1/?sp=2)).

**New things in the sky.** Each era added objects that ordinary observers had never seen: weather and research balloons, jets in formation, high-flying reconnaissance aircraft ([Haines, 1997](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/cia-role-study-UFOs.pdf)); in 2022, 163 of 366 new US government reports were characterized as balloons or balloon-like objects ([ODNI, 2022 Annual Report on Unidentified Aerial Phenomena](https://archive.dni.gov/files/ODNI/documents/assessments/Unclassified-2022-Annual-Report-UAP.pdf)); in 2024 the Pentagon's UAP office said it was increasingly able to resolve cases to Starlink satellites ([Department of Defense, Fiscal Year 2024 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP, November 2024](https://archive.dni.gov/files/ODNI/documents/assessments/DOD-AARO-Consolidated-Annual-Report-on-UAP-Nov2024.pdf)).

**Sensors and channels.** What is counted depends on what is watched and who is invited to report. When the US military encouraged aviators to report anomalies, it received 247 new reports between 5 March 2021 and 30 August 2022, against 263 filed in the 17 years before, an increase that the intelligence community attributed partly to "reduced stigma surrounding UAP reporting" ([NASA, UAP Independent Study Team final report, September 2023](https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf)). A geographical study of more than 98,000 public reports in the United States from 2001 to 2020 found that reports correlate with sky view, aircraft and military installations, results that, its authors wrote, "suggest people see more 'phenomena' when they have more opportunity to" ([Medina, Brewer and Kirkpatrick, Scientific Reports 13, December 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-49527-x)). One of its authors, Sean Kirkpatrick, had directed the Pentagon's UAP office (same).

**Perception in the dark.** A single light against a dark sky gives the eye little to judge distance, size or motion by; the classic experiment on how groups settle on what such a light is doing is described in [Conformity, cascades and contagion](/learn/conformity-cascades-and-contagion-the-mechanisms). Reports in the record agree on lights and disagree on direction, as in San Francisco in 1896 ([San Francisco Call, 24 November 1896](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn85066387/1896-11-24/ed-1/?sp=1)).

**Hoaxes and their afterlife.** A single convincing photograph can anchor a wave for decades, as the Belgian case below shows.

**Investigators and witnesses.** Interviewers who already believe can shape what witnesses come to remember, as one of the first investigators of the Varginha case later said of himself (below). How suggestion alters memory is treated in [The Satanic panic, false memories and the Escola Base case](/learn/satanic-panic-false-memories-and-the-escola-base-case).

**Fading.** Waves end when attention moves on, when an explanation is announced, or when reporting channels close: after Blue Book shut, the Air Force told callers to contact private organizations or the police ([Air Force fact sheet, 1985, National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos)). How rumours rise and stop is treated in [Rumour, panic and lynching](/learn/rumour-panic-and-lynching).

## The Belgian wave, 1989 to 1990

### Eupen, November 1989

On a clear November night in 1989, near the town of Eupen, close to the German border, two police officers reported a triangular object with three spotlights and a red flashing light; by the end of the evening, according to The Week's retrospective, at least 30 groups and three separate pairs of police officers said they had seen it ([The Week, 30 years later, we still don't know what really happened during the Belgian UFO wave, 30 March 2020](https://theweek.com/articles/905215/30-years-later-still-dont-know-what-really-happened-during-belgian-ufo-wave)). The same account counts some 2,000 sightings in Belgium between November 1989 and April 1990 and quotes Patrick Ferryn of the Belgian society for the study of space phenomena (Société belge d'étude des phénomènes spatiaux, SOBEPS): "You must know that most of these sightings will have the most banal explanation but there is a residue, which we simply can't explain" (same).

### The F-16s, March 1990

The Belgian Air Force's chief of operations at the time, Wilfried De Brouwer, wrote in an account given to the journalist Leslie Kean, as quoted by The Week, that he first assumed American experimental aircraft and asked the US embassy, whose memo confirmed that "no USAF stealth aircrafts were operating in the... area during the periods in question" (USAF: United States Air Force); on 30 March 1990, when police and two military radar stations reported an unknown object, two F-16s were scrambled, recorded radar targets "with unusual behavior", never saw an object, and "the Air Force's decision was that the evidence was insufficient to prove that there were real crafts in the air on that occasion" ([The Week, 2020](https://theweek.com/articles/905215/30-years-later-still-dont-know-what-really-happened-during-belgian-ufo-wave)). De Brouwer also said, in the same account: "I can conclude with confidence that the observations during what is now known as the Belgian wave were not caused by mass hysteria" (same). The Air Force's own report on the night of 30 to 31 March 1990 was not found on an official host for this part.

### The photograph

The image that became the emblem of the wave was a slide said to have been taken at Petit-Rechain, near Liège, in April 1990: a dark triangle with lights at its corners and centre. Analyses by scientists in several countries failed to explain it; a physicist associated with the Université catholique de Louvain concluded that the lights were probably plasma jets from a high-tech alien spacecraft, and a professor at Belgium's Royal Military School judged that the photographer could not have faked it in the way sceptics proposed ([Wim van Utrecht, Caelestia, The Belgian 1989-1990 UFO wave: the Petit-Rechain photo](https://caelestia.be/article05ad.html)). On 26 July 2011 the photographer confessed on the Belgian channel RTL-TVI that he and a colleague had built the triangle from polystyrene, fitted it with lights and photographed it as a joke for their workmates; for convinced ufologists, Le Figaro wrote, the photograph had been proof that the wave of concordant sightings "n'était pas un simple phénomène de contagion psychosociale" ("was not a simple phenomenon of psychosocial contagion") ([Le Figaro, 28 July 2011](https://www.lefigaro.fr/sciences/2011/07/28/01008-20110728ARTFIG00353-la-photo-d-un-ovni-belge-celebre-etait-un-trucage.php)). He told the paper "On arrive à tromper tout le monde avec une bête maquette en frigolite" ("You can fool everyone with a silly polystyrene model"), and apologized to those who had believed him (same). He also said afterwards that he still believed the Belgian wave "was the real thing" ([Caelestia](https://caelestia.be/article05ad.html)).

<Claim status="disputed" by="by SOBEPS investigators and former Belgian Air Force officers, against sceptical researchers">Whether a residue of the Belgian sightings shows something unexplained</Claim> remains argued. The photograph that best seemed to support it was a hoax; the radar returns of 30 March were judged insufficient by the Air Force itself; the testimonies of the police officers have not been withdrawn.

## Brazil: the official night of 19 May 1986

### What was reported

On the evening of 19 May 1986, according to accounts published on the episode's anniversaries, an air traffic controller at São José dos Campos reported a light he could not identify; civil and military radars of the integrated air defence and air traffic control centre (Centro Integrado de Defesa Aérea e Controle de Tráfego Aéreo, CINDACTA) registered unknown returns; a civil aircraft carrying Ozires Silva, who was to take office as president of Petrobras the next day, followed lights near São José dos Campos; about 2,000 military personnel at an Air Force school in Guaratinguetá were said to have watched lights in the sky; and five Brazilian Air Force (Força Aérea Brasileira, FAB) fighters, F-5s and Mirages, took off from Santa Cruz (Rio de Janeiro) and Anápolis (Goiás) without catching anything ([Correio Braziliense, 14 January 2022](https://www.correiobraziliense.com.br/ciencia-e-saude/2022/01/amp/4977539-a-noite-em-que-21-ovnis-invadiram-o-espaco-aereo-brasileiro-e-foram-perseguidos-por-cacas-da-fab.html); [Fábio Pescarini, Folhapress, republished by TNH1, 16 May 2026](https://www.tnh1.com.br/noticia/nid/40-anos-da-noite-em-que-cacas-da-forca-aerea-perseguiram-21-ovnis/)). Correio Braziliense attributes the figure of 2,000 witnesses at Guaratinguetá to a ufologist; Folhapress repeats it without attribution (same).

On 23 May 1986 the Minister of Aeronautics, Brigadier Octávio Moreira Lima, told a press conference that five fighters had pursued 21 objects and said: "Tecnicamente, diria aos senhores que não temos explicação" ("Technically, I would tell you that we have no explanation"); scientists consulted by Folha de S.Paulo at the time said there were not enough data to define the phenomena ([Pescarini, Folhapress, 2026](https://www.tnh1.com.br/noticia/nid/40-anos-da-noite-em-que-cacas-da-forca-aerea-perseguiram-21-ovnis/)). A report dated 2 June 1986 and signed by the interim commander of the air defence command concluded, as quoted by Correio Braziliense, that "os fenômenos são sólidos e refletem de certa forma inteligência, pela capacidade de acompanhar e manter distância dos observadores, como também voar em formação, não forçosamente tripulados" ("the phenomena are solid and reflect a kind of intelligence, by their capacity to follow and keep their distance from the observers, and to fly in formation, not necessarily crewed") ([Correio Braziliense, 2022](https://www.correiobraziliense.com.br/ciencia-e-saude/2022/01/amp/4977539-a-noite-em-que-21-ovnis-invadiram-o-espaco-aereo-brasileiro-e-foram-perseguidos-por-cacas-da-fab.html)). The two papers disagree on when the documents came out: Correio Braziliense dates the report's release to 25 September 2009, Folhapress says the documents have been available on the Arquivo Nacional's site since 2015 (same; [Pescarini, 2026](https://www.tnh1.com.br/noticia/nid/40-anos-da-noite-em-que-cacas-da-forca-aerea-perseguiram-21-ovnis/)).

### What remains open

The night of 1986 is the strongest Brazilian case precisely because radar, pilots and ground observers coincided and the state said so publicly. It is also the case in which the state said least about causes. The historian João Francisco Schramm, who studied the Air Force's investigations, told SBT News in 2026 that "Essas interceptações estão documentadas. No entanto, não há nas fontes da Força Aérea reflexões sobre o que poderia estar por trás desses fenômenos" ("These interceptions are documented. But in the Air Force's sources there are no reflections on what might be behind these phenomena") ([SBT News, 6 March 2026](https://sbtnews.sbt.com.br/noticia/brasil/arquivo-nacional-reune-mais-de-800-registros-de-ovn-is-no-brasil-e-debate-chega-ao-congresso-1)). An astrophysicist at the Ibirapuera Planetarium, asked by Folhapress for the 40th anniversary, recalled that an unidentified object is just that: "Não necessariamente é uma vida alienígena, um extraterrestre" ("It is not necessarily alien life, an extraterrestrial") and that anyone lying on the ground at night sees satellites, meteors and other lights ([Pescarini, 2026](https://www.tnh1.com.br/noticia/nid/40-anos-da-noite-em-que-cacas-da-forca-aerea-perseguiram-21-ovnis/)). <Claim status="disputed" by="by ufologists, who say the objects were craft under intelligent control, and by scientists who say the data cannot define them">What the radars and pilots registered on 19 May 1986</Claim> has no official explanation; the Air Force said in 2022 that all its material on UFOs had gone to the Arquivo Nacional and that it has no specialists to investigate or give opinions on such phenomena ([Correio Braziliense, 2022](https://www.correiobraziliense.com.br/ciencia-e-saude/2022/01/amp/4977539-a-noite-em-que-21-ovnis-invadiram-o-espaco-aereo-brasileiro-e-foram-perseguidos-por-cacas-da-fab.html)).

### The archive, the order of 2010 and two hearings

The Arquivo Nacional holds the fund Objeto Voador Não Identificado (OVNI, reference BR DFANBSB ARX), produced by the Air Force Command: in 2018 it described 743 records, from 1952 to 2016, including reports, questionnaires, correspondence, photographs, drawings, videos, recordings and press cuttings ([Arquivo Nacional, 2018](https://www.gov.br/arquivonacional/pt-br/canais_atendimento/imprensa/noticias/conheca-o-fundo-sobre-ovnis-do-arquivo-nacional)). In March 2026 an archivist told SBT News that the collection held more than 800 documents and that the Air Force sends "cerca de 60 dossiês e relatórios de avistamentos" ("about 60 dossiers and sighting reports") every year ([SBT News, 2026](https://sbtnews.sbt.com.br/noticia/brasil/arquivo-nacional-reune-mais-de-800-registros-de-ovn-is-no-brasil-e-debate-chega-ao-congresso-1)). The rule behind the flow is Air Force Order (Portaria) 551/GC3 of 9 August 2010, whose first article reads: "As atividades do Comando da Aeronáutica (COMAER) relativas ao assunto 'objetos voadores não identificados' (OVNI) restringem-se ao registro de ocorrências e ao seu trâmite para o Arquivo Nacional" ("The activities of the Air Force Command concerning 'unidentified flying objects' are limited to recording occurrences and sending them to the Arquivo Nacional"), as transcribed in a federal appeals ruling signed on 27 February 2023 ([Justiça Federal de Santa Catarina, Turma Recursal, appeal 5002417-48.2022.4.04.7200, Migalhas copy](https://www.migalhas.com.br/arquivos/2026/1/7F5E4CC12D7AE7_terceiraacao.pdf)). The two press accounts date the oldest item differently: SBT News places the first officially recorded sighting, photographs over Barra da Tijuca later judged false, in 1954; Correio Braziliense dates the Barra da Tijuca sighting by two reporters of O Cruzeiro to 1952 ([SBT News, 2026](https://sbtnews.sbt.com.br/noticia/brasil/arquivo-nacional-reune-mais-de-800-registros-de-ovn-is-no-brasil-e-debate-chega-ao-congresso-1); [Correio Braziliense, 2022](https://www.correiobraziliense.com.br/ciencia-e-saude/2022/01/amp/4977539-a-noite-em-que-21-ovnis-invadiram-o-espaco-aereo-brasileiro-e-foram-perseguidos-por-cacas-da-fab.html)).

The same appeals ruling quotes, as background, the Senate request for a special session that told the campaigners' version of how the files were opened: a 2004 campaign by Revista UFO and the Comissão Brasileira de Ufólogos, "UFOs: Liberdade de Informação Já", a 2005 meeting at the air defence centre in Brasília, documents sent to the archive from 2007, and a search for the reference code that returned 758 documents ([Turma Recursal, 2023](https://www.migalhas.com.br/arquivos/2026/1/7F5E4CC12D7AE7_terceiraacao.pdf)). The Federal Senate approved that request (Requerimento 193 of 2022, presented by Senator Eduardo Girão and others) on 16 March 2022 and held the special session, "destinada a comemorar os 75 anos do Dia Mundial da Ufologia" ("to commemorate the 75 years of the World UFO Day"), on 24 June 2022, the 75th anniversary of Arnold's sighting ([Senado Federal, Requerimento nº 193, de 2022](https://www25.senado.leg.br/web/atividade/materias/-/materia/152222)). On 16 September 2025 the Chamber of Deputies' committee on participatory legislation (Comissão de Legislação Participativa, CLP) held a public hearing on "Conexão entre a ufologia, o uso da Lei de Acesso à Informação e seus potenciais impactos para a informação da sociedade e manutenção da soberania nacional" ("the connection between ufology, the use of the Access to Information Law and its potential impacts on public information and national sovereignty"), with four ufologists as guests ([Câmara dos Deputados, open data, event 79033](https://dadosabertos.camara.leg.br/api/v2/eventos/79033)). The deputy who called it, Chico Alencar, told SBT News: "não se pode tratar esse tema como maluquice" ("this subject cannot be treated as madness") ([SBT News, 2026](https://sbtnews.sbt.com.br/noticia/brasil/arquivo-nacional-reune-mais-de-800-registros-de-ovn-is-no-brasil-e-debate-chega-ao-congresso-1)). A biologist writing in The Skeptic called the hearing respectful but unbalanced, with no robust sceptical counterpoint on the panel, and published a reply he had obtained from the North American Aerospace Defense Command (NORAD) saying it could not confirm any involvement in the 1996 Varginha events ([João Lucas da Silva, The Skeptic, 2025](https://www.skeptic.org.uk/?p=51890)).

## Varginha, 1996: a creature, a town and an inquiry

### The sighting and the inquiry

On the afternoon of 20 January 1996, in the Jardim Andere district of Varginha, in southern Minas Gerais, three young women said they had seen a creature crouching by a wall in a vacant lot; within days the story spread locally and then nationally, with accounts of lights in the sky and of army and fire-brigade vehicles, and a version took hold that the army had captured the being and taken it to Campinas and then to the United States ([Migalhas, 7 January 2026](https://www.migalhas.com.br/quentes/447448/et-de-varginha-relato-foi-apurado-pelo-exercito-e-virou-caso-judicial)). In 1997 the command of the army's sergeants' school (Escola de Sargentos das Armas, EsSA) opened a military police inquiry (Inquérito Policial Militar, IPM 18/97) into a book by two ufologists, Incidente em Varginha, which named soldiers as participants in the capture; the case file, now kept by the Superior Military Court (Superior Tribunal Militar, STM), runs to about 600 pages (same; [Estado de Minas, 8 January 2026](https://www.em.com.br/gerais/2026/01/7329138-versao-oficial-vs-alternativas-o-que-nao-foi-explicado-no-caso-varginha.html)).

The military prosecutor (Ministério Público Militar, MPM) wrote that the facts attributed to the military institutions were "a toda evidência, inverídicos" ("by every evidence, untrue"), that "Tudo não passou de mera rotina de trabalho, naquele dia, quando alguns caminhões do Exército saíram do quartel para serviço normal de manutenção numa oficina" ("It was nothing but ordinary work that day, when some army trucks left the barracks for normal maintenance at a workshop"), and that the authors had drawn "deduções totalmente descabidas" ("wholly unwarranted deductions") from statements by unnamed people; he asked for the inquiry to be closed because he saw no intent to commit a crime against the army's reputation ([MPM, request for archiving of IPM 18/97, Migalhas copy](https://www.migalhas.com.br/arquivos/2026/1/FE8617CF7CE40E_pedidodearquivamento.pdf)). A substitute military judge in Juiz de Fora archived it on 4 July 1997; on the origin of the sighting, the inquiry recorded that the day had been one of heavy rain and hail and suggested, from testimony and photographs, that the young women had probably seen a local man with a mental disability, known by a nickname, who often crouched in the streets ([Migalhas, 2026](https://www.migalhas.com.br/quentes/447448/et-de-varginha-relato-foi-apurado-pelo-exercito-e-virou-caso-judicial)). The inquiry attributed the death of a young military policeman in February 1996, which ufologists linked to contact with the creature, to septic shock after routine surgery ([Estado de Minas, 2026](https://www.em.com.br/gerais/2026/01/7329138-versao-oficial-vs-alternativas-o-que-nao-foi-explicado-no-caso-varginha.html)).

### The believers' answer, and an investigator's retraction

The ufologists dispute the inquiry. Vitório Pacaccini, co-author of the book, told Estado de Minas that the young women knew the man the army named, "Ele era uma figura comum da cidade" ("He was a familiar figure in town"), and asked how three frightened young women could describe slimy skin, an ammonia smell and three protuberances on the head if they had seen a neighbour crouching; Marco Antonio Petit, of the Comissão Brasileira de Ufólogos, regards the official explanation as a cover-up of military movements and said: "O acobertamento é uma política de Estado" ("Cover-up is a state policy") ([Estado de Minas, 2026](https://www.em.com.br/gerais/2026/01/7329138-versao-oficial-vs-alternativas-o-que-nao-foi-explicado-no-caso-varginha.html)). The sharpest change came from inside the investigation. Ubirajara Rodrigues, one of the first ufologists to interview the young women, said in a documentary quoted by the paper: "Cometi o grande erro que os ufólogos cometem. Eu disse a elas que o que elas avistaram, a ufologia acredita que sejam seres de outros planetas" ("I made the great mistake that ufologists make. I told them that what they saw, ufology believes to be beings from other planets"); after almost thirty years he said "Eu não acredito mais em nada disso" ("I no longer believe any of it"), while Petit says Rodrigues was pressured into silence (same). <Claim status="disputed" by="by ufologists, against the army's inquiry and the military prosecutor">That a creature was captured in Varginha</Claim> was found untrue by the only official investigation on record. Pacaccini repeated the capture account at the Chamber of Deputies hearing of September 2025, where, according to a sceptic who attended, no documentary or physical evidence was presented to support it ([The Skeptic, 2025](https://www.skeptic.org.uk/?p=51890)).

## Washington again, 2017 to 2026

### Videos, a task force and a preliminary assessment

The modern US wave began with publicity. In 2019 leaked footage of objects filmed by Navy pilots received wide coverage; the Navy acknowledged that the video was authentic and in 2020 commissioned a task force on unidentified aerial phenomena ([Gallup, 20 August 2021](https://news.gallup.com/poll/353420/larger-minority-says-ufos-alien-spacecraft.aspx)). The ODNI's preliminary assessment of 25 June 2021 examined 144 reports by US government sources from November 2004 to March 2021, of which 80 involved several sensors; it identified one with high confidence, a large deflating balloon; 18 incidents described in 21 reports showed unusual movement, which might reflect sensor errors, spoofing or observer misperception; and it proposed five possible categories of explanation: airborne clutter, natural atmospheric phenomena, US government or industry programmes, foreign adversary systems, and "other" ([ODNI, 25 June 2021](https://archive.dni.gov/files/ODNI/documents/assessments/Prelimary-Assessment-UAP-20210625.pdf)).

Belief moved with the coverage. A Pew Research Center survey of 10,417 adults, taken from 14 to 24 June 2021, found 51% saying that UFOs reported by military personnel were likely evidence of intelligent life outside Earth, while 87% said they had heard little or nothing about the government's release; the 12% who followed it closely were the most inclined to believe, and the survey could not tell whether the news changed minds or drew those already inclined ([Pew Research Center, 30 June 2021](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2021/06/30/most-americans-believe-in-intelligent-life-beyond-earth-few-see-ufos-as-a-major-national-security-threat/)). Gallup found 41% in July 2021 saying some UFOs have been alien spacecraft, up from 33% in 2019, a change it set beside "a spate of articles in 2019 focused on leaked footage" ([Gallup, 2021](https://news.gallup.com/poll/353420/larger-minority-says-ufos-alien-spacecraft.aspx)).

### Reports multiply, and most resolve into ordinary objects

The 2022 annual report counted 510 reports by 30 August 2022, 366 of them new; of those, 163 were characterized as balloons or balloon-like, 26 as unmanned aircraft systems, 6 as clutter such as birds or plastic bags, and 171 remained uncharacterized; the rise was put down partly to "a concentrated effort to destigmatize the topic of UAP" ([ODNI, 2022 Annual Report](https://archive.dni.gov/files/ODNI/documents/assessments/Unclassified-2022-Annual-Report-UAP.pdf)). The office created in July 2022 to resolve such reports, the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) ([Medina, Brewer and Kirkpatrick, 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-49527-x)), received 291 more by April 2023, for a total of 801, and wrote that "the unidentified and purported anomalous nature of most UAP will likely resolve to ordinary phenomena" ([FY2023 Consolidated Annual Report](https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/UNCLASSIFIED-FY23_Consolidated_Annual_Report_on_UAP-Oct_25_2023_1236.pdf)). Its 2024 report counted 757 new reports from May 2023 to June 2024 and 1,652 in total by 24 October 2024; 21 merited further analysis, 444 lacked enough data, and AARO "has discovered no evidence of extraterrestrial beings, activity, or technology" ([FY2024 Consolidated Annual Report](https://archive.dni.gov/files/ODNI/documents/assessments/DOD-AARO-Consolidated-Annual-Report-on-UAP-Nov2024.pdf)). The same report gives two different counts of cases resolved during the period, 118 in its executive summary and 49 in its third section (same).

### February 2023: a radar filter and a wave of shoot-downs

The clearest demonstration of how sensors make waves came in February 2023. After a Chinese balloon crossed the United States, the North American Aerospace Defense Command adjusted its radar to pick up slow-moving objects, and three unidentified objects were shot down over Alaska, Canada and Lake Huron. President Biden said on 16 February: "We don't have any evidence that there has been a sudden increase in the number of objects in the sky. We're now just seeing more of them, partially because the steps we've taken to increase our radars", and the intelligence community's current assessment was that the three were "most likely balloons tied to private companies, recreation, or research institutions" ([White House, Remarks by President Biden on the United States response to recent aerial objects, 16 February 2023](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefing-room/speeches-remarks/2023/02/16/remarks-by-president-biden-on-the-united-states-response-to-recent-aerial-objects)). Three days earlier, the press secretary had found it necessary to say that there was no "indication of aliens or extraterrestrial activity" ([AP, via WKMG ClickOrlando, 13 February 2023](https://www.clickorlando.com/news/politics/2023/02/13/aliens-us-lack-of-info-on-shootdowns-breeds-wild-ideas/)).

### A hearing, a historical review and its critics

On 26 July 2023 a former intelligence officer, David Grusch, told a House of Representatives subcommittee under oath that he had been "informed in the course of my official duties of a multi-decade UAP crash retrieval and reverse engineering program to which I was denied access", and, asked about the crews of such craft, that "biologics came with some of these recoveries", "Nonhuman, and that was the assessment of people with direct knowledge on the program I talked to" ([House Committee on Oversight and Accountability, hearing of 26 July 2023, Serial No. 118-53, GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-118hhrg53022/html/CHRG-118hhrg53022.htm)). He said that the documentation would have to be discussed in a secure facility (same).

AARO's Report on the Historical Record of U.S. Government Involvement with Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena, Volume I, released on 8 March 2024, reviewed official efforts from 1945 onward. As reported by The Washington Post, it found "no evidence that any [U.S. government] investigation, academic-sponsored research, or official review panel has confirmed that any sighting of a UAP represented extraterrestrial technology", attributed sightings to "ordinary objects and phenomena and the result of misidentification", and judged the claims about reverse-engineering programmes "inaccurate"; about 30 people who said they had insight into such programmes were interviewed, some code names given by them did not exist, and a "Kona Blue" proposal said to concern "nonhuman biologics" had been a never-approved idea pitched to the Department of Homeland Security by supporters of an earlier Pentagon study ([Shane Harris and Dan Lamothe, The Washington Post, via Stars and Stripes, 8 March 2024](https://www.stripes.com/theaters/us/2024-03-08/pentagon-no-evidence-alien-visits-13253101.html)). AARO's acting director, Tim Phillips, told reporters: "We saw a small group of people who knew each other, who all cited their observations as the purpose for their beliefs or for their observations" (same). Space.com quoted the report's phrase for the mechanism, "circular reporting from a group of individuals who believe this to be the case, despite the lack of any evidence" ([Space.com, 8 March 2024](https://www.space.com/pentagon-ufo-office-aaro-historical-report-no-emprical-evidence-alien-technology)). National Public Radio reported the same findings ([NPR, 8 March 2024](https://www.npr.org/2024/03/08/1237100622/pentagon-ufo-report-no-evidence-alien-technology)).

The review has critics. Christopher Mellon, a former deputy assistant secretary of defence for intelligence, told Space.com that "This report is not going to satisfy critics in part because there are many witnesses who did not trust AARO and would not speak with them", and warned against conflating "There is no recovered alien technology" with "we don't have evidence of craft doing things beyond our present understanding of science and technology" ([Space.com, 2024](https://www.space.com/pentagon-ufo-office-aaro-historical-report-no-emprical-evidence-alien-technology)). AARO's former director answered the critics in Scientific American that "they have not provided any verifiable evidence of this" (same, quoting Kirkpatrick's essay; [Kirkpatrick, Scientific American 330, April 2024, PubMed record](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=39017293)). NASA's independent study team, for its part, wrote that "there is no conclusive evidence suggesting an extraterrestrial origin for UAP" in the peer-reviewed literature, and reported that at least one of its scientists received hate mail from colleagues and that others were ridiculed on social media, which it took as confirmation of the stigma that keeps observers from reporting ([NASA, 2023](https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf)).

### New Jersey, late 2024: a drone flap

In November and December 2024 residents and officials in New Jersey, then across the north-east, reported drones at night. On 16 December the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and the Department of Defense said in a joint statement that the FBI had received more than 5,000 reports in a few weeks, generating about 100 leads, and that "the sightings to date include a combination of lawful commercial drones, hobbyist drones, and law enforcement drones, as well as manned fixed-wing aircraft, helicopters, and stars mistakenly reported as drones" ([FOX 29 Philadelphia, 16 December 2024](https://www.fox29.com/news/drone-sightings-no-national-security-risk-us-government-agencies-statement)). On 28 January 2025 the new White House press secretary, Karoline Leavitt, said the drones over New Jersey "were authorized to be flown by the FAA for research and various other reasons", that many were hobbyists, and: "In time, it got worse due to curiosity. This was not the enemy" ([ABC7 New York, 28 January 2025](https://abc7ny.com/post/white-house-says-many-drones-spotted-new-jersey-east-coast-were-flown-faa-was-not-enemy/15844019/)).

### 2026: files released, reports overdue

In February 2026 Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth said the department would comply with the president's direction to release government files on UAP and extraterrestrial life; a Pentagon spokesperson said AARO was examining more than 2,000 cases, about 1,000 of them lacking enough data, and DefenseScoop noted that AARO had published neither its 2025 annual report nor the second volume of its historical review ([DefenseScoop, 25 February 2026](https://defensescoop.com/2026/02/25/hegseth-ufo-disclosure-trump-aaro-uap-caseload/)). On 8 May 2026 the Pentagon began releasing new files, saying that the public "can ultimately make up their own minds", while experts warned that UAP videos are often misinterpreted by those unfamiliar with military technology ([Collin Binkley and Seung Min Kim, Associated Press, via ABC30, 8 May 2026](https://abc30.com/amp/post/pentagon-ufo-files-release-amid-new-disclosure-officials-say-public-can-draw-conclusions-unidentified-anomalous-phenomena/19063755/)). The National Archives has established a UAP Records Collection, Record Group 615, under sections 1841 to 1843 of the 2024 National Defense Authorization Act, and adds records to its online catalogue as agencies send them ([National Archives, Records Related to UFOs and UAPs](https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps)).

## Who steered, and who did not

Most of the wave is unsteered. The reports in the record come from people who saw something they could not name, and the explanations most often found (balloons, aircraft, stars, satellites, radar effects) need no deceiver. Nobody planned the summer of 1947, the 2,000 Belgian sightings or the 5,000 New Jersey tips; attention, new objects in the sky and new channels for reporting were enough.

Governments steered, in both directions, and the record documents it. The Air Force's Project Grudge ran a public relations campaign to persuade the public that UFOs were nothing extraordinary; the CIA's panel recommended that the National Security Council debunk reports through mass media, schools and Disney, and that private UFO groups be watched; the CIA hid its role; and the Air Force, by the account of the CIA's own historian, made "misleading and deceptive statements" to protect the U-2 ([Haines, 1997](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/cia-role-study-UFOs.pdf)). At Roswell the material, according to the Air Force's 1995 report, came from a then-classified balloon project ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos)). Secrecy for real programmes and debunking for public calm are both forms of managed belief, and Haines concluded that the deception "added fuel to the later conspiracy theories and the coverup controversy of the 1970s" ([Haines, 1997](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/cia-role-study-UFOs.pdf)). The techniques, from the "small lie" told for a defensible reason to the campaign, are mapped in [the psychological operations series](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign); why a correction rarely catches up with a belief is in [why misbeliefs persist](/learn/why-misbeliefs-persist); how cover-up beliefs grow from real secrets is in [Conspiracy theories at scale](/learn/conspiracy-theories-at-scale). The fear that runs the other way, that an adversary might use UFO reports to "touch off mass hysteria and panic" and saturate air defences, was the CIA's in 1952 (same); states' influence operations on the record are in [state influence campaigns](/learn/state-influence-campaigns-on-the-record) and the history of psychological warfare in [the short history of propaganda](/learn/a-short-history-of-propaganda-and-psychological-warfare).

Individuals steered too. A hoaxer in Petit-Rechain gave a wave its icon; a ufologist in Varginha says he told the witnesses what they had seen; a "small group of people who knew each other", in AARO's account, kept a story of crashed craft alive by citing one another ([Caelestia](https://caelestia.be/article05ad.html); [Estado de Minas, 2026](https://www.em.com.br/gerais/2026/01/7329138-versao-oficial-vs-alternativas-o-que-nao-foi-explicado-no-caso-varginha.html); [The Washington Post, via Stars and Stripes, 2024](https://www.stripes.com/theaters/us/2024-03-08/pentagon-no-evidence-alien-visits-13253101.html)). How repetition among a few becomes apparent consensus is in [the human attack surface](/learn/the-human-attack-surface-why-repetition-works), and how reach multiplies it in [from the lodge to the network](/learn/from-the-lodge-to-the-network-how-reach-changed). Checking a viral sky video is a task for the methods in [verification and fact-checking](/learn/verification-and-fact-checking).

## What to remember

- A "flap" is a cluster of reports in a small region and a short time; the Condon report attributed clustering partly to news coverage, which sends people to look and makes them report.
- The summer of 1947 grew from one pilot's report on 24 June to 41 states by 8 July in the Washington press; the Air Force's first study blamed hysteria, hoaxes and misidentification.
- Blue Book logged 12,618 reports from 1947 to 1969 and left 701 unidentified; it found no threat, no advanced technology and no extraterrestrial vehicles.
- The CIA feared an adversary could use UFO reports to cause panic, recommended debunking through mass media and schools, hid its role, and, by its historian's account, the Air Force misled the public to protect the U-2.
- The emblematic photograph of the Belgian wave was a confessed hoax; Brazil's night of 19 May 1986 remains officially unexplained; the army's inquiry into Varginha found no capture.
- US reports rose from 144 (2021) to more than 2,000 cases (2026) as reporting was destigmatized; resolved cases were ordinary objects, and AARO found no evidence of extraterrestrial technology, which its critics dispute.

<ReaderQuestions>

- When a government keeps a real programme secret and lets the public believe in something else, is that a lie, and does it make later disbelief of official explanations reasonable?
- Reports rose sharply when pilots were encouraged to report and radars were tuned to slow objects. How should anyone compare the number of sightings across decades when the counting itself changed?
- The Condon report told teachers not to give credit for school work based on UFO books. Is discouraging a belief by authority a good way to build the critical habits the report wanted?
- In Varginha one investigator says he told witnesses what they had seen. How much of any famous sighting can be separated from what was said to the witnesses afterwards?
- A small unexplained residue remains in the Belgian and Brazilian cases. What would count as enough evidence to settle such a residue in either direction, and who should hold it?
