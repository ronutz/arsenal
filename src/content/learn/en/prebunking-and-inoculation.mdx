<MethodNote />

[Fact-checking](/learn/verification-and-fact-checking) answers a falsehood after it has spread, and [the human attack surface](/learn/the-human-attack-surface-why-repetition-works) explains why the answer never fully erases it. Prebunking tries to get there first. Google's prebunking site defines it as "a technique to forewarn people of attempts to manipulate them, equipping them to spot and refute misleading claims and building resilience for the future" ([Prebunking with Google](https://prebunking.withgoogle.com/)). This part of the series opened by [the map from the small lie to the campaign](/learn/psychological-operations-from-the-small-lie-to-the-campaign) traces the idea from a 1961 paper to video advertisements seen by tens of millions, sets out what each study and campaign actually measured, and presents the dispute over whether inoculated people judge news better or just doubt more.

## The idea: a weakened dose of persuasion

### McGuire's theory

A practitioners' guide written by the University of Cambridge, Jigsaw (a Google unit) and BBC Media Action, the British Broadcasting Corporation's development charity, says that prebunking "is built on inoculation theory, which was developed in the 1960s by social psychologist William McGuire, and designed to be used as a psychological 'vaccine for brainwash'", and that "Much like how medical vaccines confer physiological resistance against future infection, psychological inoculations confer resistance against future attempts of attitudinal manipulation" ([Harjani, Roozenbeek, Biddlestone, van der Linden and colleagues, A Practical Guide to Prebunking Misinformation, November 2022](https://prebunking.withgoogle.com/docs/A_Practical_Guide_to_Prebunking_Misinformation.pdf)). Among the founding papers is William McGuire and Demetrios Papageorgis's "The relative efficacy of various types of prior belief-defense in producing immunity against persuasion", published in March 1961 ([Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology 62(2), 327-337, Crossref record](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0042026)); the paper's text was not available for this part.

The guide describes two parts. A **forewarning** "activates the viewer's mental defenses against unwanted attempts to persuade them by alerting them that they are likely to encounter misleading messages in the near future", and a **preemptive refutation** gives "tools to counter misleading information", ideally including what it calls a "micro-dose", a weakened example of the misinformation ([Practical Guide, 2022](https://prebunking.withgoogle.com/docs/A_Practical_Guide_to_Prebunking_Misinformation.pdf)). John Cook, Stephan Lewandowsky and Ullrich Ecker give the same structure, "(1) an explicit warning of an impending threat and (2) a refutation of an anticipated argument that exposes the imminent fallacy", and add that "Inoculation messages have been found to be more effective at conveying resistance to misinformation than supportive messages", that is, messages that promote accurate information without mentioning the misinformation ([Cook, Lewandowsky and Ecker, PLOS ONE, 5 May 2017](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0175799)).

### What half a century of studies found

A meta-analysis of 54 tests of the theory found inoculation messages "superior to both supportive messages and no-treatment controls at conferring resistance", and that refuting the same arguments a person later meets and refuting different ones were "equally effective at reducing attitude change". Some predictions of earlier narrative reviews did not hold: "No significant increase in resistance as a function of threat or involvement was found", and resistance held between immediate and moderate delays, "with a decay in resistance after two weeks" ([Banas and Rains, Communication Monographs, September 2010](https://doi.org/10.1080/03637751003758193)).

The theory was built to protect attitudes people already held. Josh Compton, Sander van der Linden, John Cook and Melisa Basol describe how its "50+ year history" reached contested science and conspiracy theories, and distinguish the conventional "prophylactic" use from "therapeutic" inoculation, which "can have positive effects" even when people already hold the undesirable position ([Compton et al., Social and Personality Psychology Compass, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12602)). The practitioners' guide notes the same: while inoculation is usually most effective beforehand, "it's still possible to inoculate someone after they have been exposed to misinformation but haven't yet been persuaded" ([Practical Guide, 2022](https://prebunking.withgoogle.com/docs/A_Practical_Guide_to_Prebunking_Misinformation.pdf)).

## From issues to techniques

### Issue-based inoculation: climate change

Early applications to misinformation targeted a single topic. Van der Linden, Anthony Leiserowitz, Seth Rosenthal and Edward Maibach reported in January 2017 "evidence is provided that it is possible to pre-emptively protect ('inoculate') public attitudes about climate change against real-world misinformation" ([van der Linden et al., Global Challenges, 23 January 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/gch2.201600008)). In Cook, Lewandowsky and Ecker's experiments, "false-balance media coverage (giving contrarian views equal voice with climate scientists) lowered perceived consensus overall", while inoculating messages "that (1) explain the flawed argumentation technique used in the misinformation or that (2) highlight the scientific consensus on climate change were effective in neutralizing those adverse effects"; their example of a technique was the rhetorical use of "fake experts" to feign a lack of consensus ([Cook et al., 2017](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0175799)).

### Technique-based inoculation: the games

Explaining a technique, rather than a topic, can be done "without prior knowledge of specific misleading content, thus potentially providing broad resilience", as Roozenbeek and colleagues later put it ([Roozenbeek et al., 2022](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9401631/fullTextXML)). Jon Roozenbeek and van der Linden turned the idea into games in which the player produces misinformation.

- **The fake news game (2018).** Participants wrote a news article about "a strongly politicized issue (the European refugee crisis) using misleading tactics"; a randomized field study with 95 participants in a public high school setting gave "some preliminary evidence" that playing reduced the perceived reliability and persuasiveness of fake news articles ([Roozenbeek and van der Linden, Journal of Risk Research, 26 February 2018](https://doi.org/10.1080/13669877.2018.1443491)).
- **Bad News (2019).** Players "take on the role of a fake news producer and learn to master six documented techniques commonly used in the production of misinformation: polarisation, invoking emotions, spreading conspiracy theories, trolling people online, deflecting blame, and impersonating fake accounts". In an evaluation of about 15,000 participants with a before-and-after design, the authors reported "initial evidence that people's ability to spot and resist misinformation improves after gameplay, irrespective of education, age, political ideology, and cognitive style" ([Roozenbeek and van der Linden, Palgrave Communications, now Humanities and Social Sciences Communications, 25 June 2019](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-019-0279-9)).
- **A controlled replication (2020).** Addressing "the lack of a control group" and other shortcomings of the first study, Melisa Basol, Roozenbeek and van der Linden compared Bad News with a gamified control (N = 196) on 18 fake headlines and found that the game "significantly improves people's ability to spot misinformation techniques" and raised confidence, but only among those "who updated their reliability assessments in the correct direction" ([Basol, Roozenbeek and van der Linden, Journal of Cognition, 10 January 2020](https://doi.org/10.5334/joc.91)).
- **Harmony Square (2020).** A ten-minute game about political misinformation, made with the Dutch media collective DROG and others in collaboration with the United States (US) Department of State's Global Engagement Center (GEC) and the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), and modelled on an approach of that department's cybersecurity agency; players found manipulative posts "significantly less reliable after playing" and were "less likely to report sharing it with others in their network". The authors declare that "Neither DROG nor GEC and DHS were involved in any way in this study's design, analysis, or write-up", and that one of them was paid by DROG for the work ([Roozenbeek and van der Linden, Harvard Kennedy School Misinformation Review, 30 October 2020](https://misinforeview.hks.harvard.edu/article/breaking-harmony-square-a-game-that-inoculates-against-political-misinformation/)).
- **Go Viral! (2021).** A five-minute game about COVID-19 misinformation, tested in English, French and German in two studies (n = 1,771 and n = 1,777), increased the perceived manipulativeness of misinformation and confidence and reduced self-reported willingness to share, the first two effects lasting "for at least one week"; reading infographics from the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) also helped, "albeit with descriptively smaller effect sizes than the game" ([Basol et al., Big Data and Society, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/20539517211013868)).
- **Real-world items and cross-protection (2022).** In two experiments with 2,188 players recruited through Bad News, a 15-minute game gave resistance to real viral misinformation using the techniques taught (Cohen's d = 0.37), and "cross-protection is achieved but at a reduced effect size" (d = 0.10) against techniques not taught ([Roozenbeek, Traberg and van der Linden, Royal Society Open Science, May 2022](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.211719)).

### How long it lasts

Rakoen Maertens and colleagues compared Bad News with Tetris. When participants were tested at regular intervals, "the inoculation effect remains stable for at least 3 months"; without regular testing they "found significant decay over a 2-month time period so that the long-term inoculation effect was no longer significant" ([Maertens, Roozenbeek, Basol and van der Linden, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/xap0000315)). The practitioners' guide reports an experiment by Jigsaw and the Universities of Cambridge and Bristol in which videos protected people "initially for around 10 days, and a 30-second booster video at Day 10 was a useful reminder that extended the protection to at least 30 days" ([Practical Guide, 2022](https://prebunking.withgoogle.com/docs/A_Practical_Guide_to_Prebunking_Misinformation.pdf)).

| Intervention | Format | Main measured outcome | Reported duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Climate consensus inoculation (2017) | Messages on the scientific consensus | Perceived consensus protected against misinformation | Not stated in the abstracts read |
| Bad News (2019, 2020) | 15-minute game | Rated reliability of fake headlines; confidence | Stable 3 months with testing; decays over 2 months without |
| Harmony Square (2020) | 10-minute game | Rated reliability; willingness to share | Not stated in the abstract read |
| Go Viral! (2021) | 5-minute game | Perceived manipulativeness; confidence; sharing | At least one week |
| Inoculation videos (2022) | 90-second videos | Technique recognition; discernment; sharing | Measured within 24 hours on YouTube |

## The 2022 YouTube field study

The step from laboratory to platform came in August 2022. Roozenbeek, van der Linden, Beth Goldberg of Jigsaw, Steve Rathje and Lewandowsky made five short animated videos, each exposing one technique: "emotionally manipulative language, incoherence, false dichotomies, scapegoating, and ad hominem attacks". Each gave a forewarning, a preemptive refutation and a "microdose" of misinformation "in the form of innocuous and humorous examples (such as an example of incoherence from the animated television series Family Guy)", all "nonpolitical and fictitious" to "avoid any appearance of partisan bias" ([Roozenbeek et al., Science Advances, 24 August 2022](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abo6254)).

Six randomized controlled studies (n = 6,464) found that the videos "improve manipulation technique recognition, boost confidence in spotting these techniques, increase people's ability to discern trustworthy from untrustworthy content, and improve the quality of their sharing decisions". The seventh study ran two of the videos as YouTube advertisements for 15 days: about 5.4 million users were shown them, 967,347 watched one for 30 seconds or more, and "A random 30% of this group of users was also shown a single (voluntary) test question" within 24 hours, asking them to identify the technique in a fictional post; 22,632 answered across treatment and control ([Roozenbeek et al., full text via Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9401631/fullTextXML)).

The results, in the authors' own terms:

- The videos boosted "manipulation technique recognition by about 5% on average", at an "average cost for each ad view" of "approximately $0.05".
- <Claim status="reported" by="by the study's authors">"we narrowly failed to reach our preregistered smallest effect size of interest (SESOI) of h = 0.10, with an observed effect size of h = 0.09 across all six items"</Claim>; the threshold was met for the false dichotomies video but not for the emotional language one.
- Limitations they list: effects were measured within a day (median 18.4 hours), so they "were unable to study how long the inoculation effect remains significant"; videos and samples were tailored to the US; YouTube does not make behavioural data available, so sharing could not be measured on the platform.
- "This study was funded by Google Jigsaw", and one author was affiliated with Jigsaw (Google LLC); the authors declared no competing interests.

Jigsaw's own case-study page summarizes the same work as "73% of individuals who watched a prebunking video were more likely to spot the misinformation technique online", and elsewhere on the site as an increase of "5 percentage points on average" ([Prebunking with Google, case studies](https://prebunking.withgoogle.com/)). The full text read for this part contains no 73% figure; it reports the average gain.

## Jigsaw's campaigns

From 2022 Jigsaw ran prebunking as public campaigns, measuring them on YouTube with single survey questions: viewers answered one question some time after the video, and their answers were compared with those of a group that had not seen it.

### Central and Eastern Europe, 2022

Jigsaw partnered with local organizations in Poland, Czechia and Slovakia "to prebunk emerging false narratives about Ukrainian migrants" ([Prebunking with Google, case studies](https://prebunking.withgoogle.com/)). According to Jigsaw's head of research, Beth Goldberg, the videos "ran across YouTube, Facebook, Twitter and TikTok in each of the three countries" in two periods in the autumn and winter of 2022, with partners including Demagog, and "ultimately reached almost a third of the Polish, Czech and Slovak populations, garnering over 38 million views". The share of viewers able to identify the techniques "increased by as much as 8 percentage points after viewing one of these videos", with the greatest gain in Poland; "The results in Czechia were more mixed", and in Slovakia "Only in the first campaign when combining both audience segments can a measurable change be detected", which Goldberg suggests "may have been due to the videos being dubbed instead of created specifically for the Slovakian market", while "further research is required". No sample sizes were given ([Goldberg, Jigsaw on Medium, February 2023, read through a fetch tool](https://medium.com/jigsaw/defanging-disinformations-threat-to-ukrainian-refugees-b164dbbc1c60)).

### Germany, 2023

In the summer of 2023 Jigsaw ran videos in Germany "on Facebook, Instagram, and YouTube" with Google Germany, the implementation partner Moonshot and German organizations including Correctiv, on three manipulative techniques, decontextualization, fearmongering and whataboutism, achieving "over 42 million views". Within 48 hours viewers saw a Brand Lift survey on YouTube, and <Claim status="reported" by="by Jigsaw">"viewers were 5.4% better at identifying any of the three manipulation techniques than Germans who had not seen our videos"</Claim>. Jigsaw noted that "easily translated concepts performed better", since decontextualization and whataboutism "are more technical terms and loanwords"; it gave no sample sizes ([Jigsaw on Medium, 25 October 2023, read through a fetch tool](https://medium.com/jigsaw/prebunking-to-build-defenses-against-online-manipulation-tactics-in-germany-a1dbfbc67a1a)).

### Indonesia and the European Union, 2023 and 2024

Ahead of Indonesia's February 2024 elections, Jigsaw and Google ran a campaign with a popular influencer against "emotional manipulation, decontextualization, and discrediting or defamation", which "reached 57.6M unique users, including more than half of voters aged 18–34", according to the case study, which reports reach but no effect size ([Prebunking with Google, case studies](https://prebunking.withgoogle.com/)). The site also says that, "Together with Moonshot and local partners", Google and Jigsaw are running prebunking videos in the European Union (EU), and presents Elections24Check, a database collected ahead of the 2024 European elections by "over 40 members of the European Fact-Checking Standards Network (EFCSN) with support from Google".

## The dispute: discernment or general scepticism?

All of these studies measure whether people rate manipulative content as less reliable, or recognize a technique. A critic's question is what happens to their judgment of reliable content at the same time. If people simply call more things false, their score on fake items rises while their ability to tell true from false stays the same.

### The critique

Ariana Modirrousta-Galian and Philip Higham reanalysed five earlier studies of Bad News and Go Viral! with receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, a method from signal detection theory that separates discrimination from response bias. They found that "when comparable true and fake news items were used, Bad News and Go Viral! did not improve discrimination, but rather elicited more 'false' responses to all news items (more conservative responding)", and concluded that <Claim status="disputed">these interventions "are not as effective as previously thought and may even be counterproductive"</Claim> ([Modirrousta-Galian and Higham, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0001395)). Higham, Modirrousta-Galian and T. Seabrooke later argued that the common measure, the difference between mean ratings of target and non-target items, "is confounded by the configuration of response criteria, leading researchers to incorrectly conclude that an intervention improves discernment", and recommended "the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve" instead ([Higham, Modirrousta-Galian and Seabrooke, Current Opinion in Psychology, December 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.copsyc.2023.101785)). The YouTube study defined its main outcome as discernment, "the difference between averaged scores for the manipulative and neutral stimuli", which is the measure this critique targets ([Roozenbeek et al., 2022](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9401631/fullTextXML)).

Two further lines of evidence point the same way. In five studies with 7,286 online participants using headlines based on real true and false content, Gordon Pennycook and colleagues, among them Lewandowsky, found that "Although an emotional manipulation inoculation did help participants identify emotional manipulation, there was no improvement in participants' ability to tell truth from falsehood"; paired with a prompt that draws attention to accuracy, the combined intervention "did successfully improve truth discernment (by increasing belief in true content)" ([Pennycook et al., Nature Human Behaviour, 4 November 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-02023-2)). A preregistered replication in India (N = 150) of a study that had found Bad News improved discrimination of Indian headlines found, once the headline sets were counterbalanced, "no significant differences in discrimination or response bias" between before and after ([Seabrooke, Modirrousta-Galian and Higham, Psychonomic Bulletin and Review, 15 December 2025](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-025-02827-x)).

Warnings about misinformation in general carry a related risk. In an experiment with 1,305 participants, Toni van der Meer, Michael Hameleers and Jakob Ohme found that "warning about the threat of misinformation can prime general distrust in authentic news" ([van der Meer, Hameleers and Ohme, Journalism Studies, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/1461670X.2023.2187652)).

### The replies

The proponents answered with new data and reanalyses. In a meta-analysis applying signal detection theory to 33 inoculation experiments (combined N = 37,025), Simchon and colleagues, among them van der Linden and Lewandowsky, report that "both gamified and video-based interventions consistently improve discrimination between reliable and unreliable news, without increasing response bias", meaning that participants did not become uniformly more sceptical or more credulous ([Simchon et al., Current Opinion in Psychology, published online 14 October 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.copsyc.2025.102194)). An independent meta-analysis of 42 studies (42,530 participants) found that inoculation reduced the credibility given to misinformation (d = -0.36), raised the credibility given to real information (d = 0.20) and improved credibility discernment (d = 0.20), but did not significantly change the intention to share misinformation ([Lu, Hu, Li, Bi and Ju, Journal of Medical Internet Research, 29 August 2023](https://doi.org/10.2196/49255)). And Leder and colleagues, among them Roozenbeek and van der Linden, acknowledging "a potentially inadvertent impact of such games on people's evaluation of non-misinformation ('real news')" and decay over time, found that adding feedback exercises after Bad News and Harmony Square "significantly boosts discernment compared with playing the game without a feedback exercise, primarily by improving accuracy at detecting real news", confirmed with signal detection measures ([Leder et al., Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, August 2024](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0001603)).

| Question | Critics' finding | Proponents' finding |
|---|---|---|
| Do people learn to recognize the techniques? | Not disputed | Yes, in labs and on YouTube |
| Do games improve true/false discrimination? | No; they shift responses towards "false" (five reanalysed studies, India replication) | Yes, without added bias (33-study reanalysis); with feedback exercises, yes |
| Does a technique video improve belief accuracy on real headlines? | No, unless paired with an accuracy prompt (five studies) | The 2022 studies reported improved discernment on their stimuli |
| Do general warnings hurt trust in true news? | Yes, in one experiment | Inoculation did not raise uniform scepticism (meta-analysis) |

The disagreement runs largely between teams, though not strictly: Stephan Lewandowsky is a co-author of the 2022 YouTube study, of the 2024 study that found no gain in truth discernment from inoculation alone, and of the 2025 meta-analysis that found improved discrimination.

## Who prebunks, and to what end

Prebunking campaigns need money, platforms and messengers. The YouTube study was funded by Google's Jigsaw; Harmony Square was made in collaboration with two US government departments; Jigsaw's prebunking site tells campaign builders to set up advertiser accounts with a profile "that transparently identifies your affiliation, goals and funding source" ([Prebunking with Google](https://prebunking.withgoogle.com/)). The guide also lists limits: oversimplifying a short message "may make it ineffective, cause confusion, and even risk spreading misinformation further"; and while "backfire effects ... do not appear to be a significant cause for concern", one study "found that messages prebunking white supremacist narratives had no effect on people with extreme rightwing beliefs" ([Practical Guide, 2022](https://prebunking.withgoogle.com/docs/A_Practical_Guide_to_Prebunking_Misinformation.pdf)).

**Brazil.** No Brazilian prebunking campaign evaluated in the way the European ones were was found in the sources read for this part, and Brazil does not appear among Jigsaw's published case studies. The nearest public effort is the electoral court's: ahead of the 2026 elections the Tribunal Superior Eleitoral (TSE) launched "V de Verdade" (V for truth), subtitled "Em terra de fatos, fake não tem vez" (in the land of facts, fakes have no place), six one-minute videos on its social networks and YouTube channel, each covering one of "5 Vs da Desinformação" (volume, variety, velocity, virality and verisimilitude, the last including artificially produced content) that explain how false content spreads and becomes credible ([Brasil 61, 4 April 2026](https://brasil61.com/n/eleicoes-2026-campanha-do-tse-ensina-eleitor-a-reconhecer-fake-news-bras2616191); [TSE, 12 February 2026](https://www.tse.jus.br/comunicacao/noticias/2026/Fevereiro/tse-lanca-campanha-para-ajudar-eleitores-a-desvendar-conteudos-falsos)). The campaign teaches warning signs before exposure, as prebunking does, but no evaluation of its effect was found.

## Prebunking and fact-checking side by side

| | Fact-checking and debunking | Prebunking |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | After a claim circulates | Before exposure, or before persuasion |
| Target | A specific claim | A topic (issue-based) or a technique (technique-based) |
| Measured effect | Lower belief in the corrected claim, often decaying | Better recognition of techniques; discernment disputed |
| Weak points | Reach and speed; implied truth effect of labels | Transfer to real claims; duration; possible general scepticism |
| Who pays, in the cases above | Platforms (until 2025 in the US), foundations, media | Google's Jigsaw, governments, universities |

A 2018 meta-analysis of 65 correction studies found "Rebuttals (r = .38) are more effective than forewarnings (r = .16)" ([Walter and Murphy, Communication Monographs, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1080/03637751.2018.1467564)), and a 2024 toolbox of individual-level interventions lists both inoculation and debunking among nine types with an evidence base ([Kozyreva et al., Nature Human Behaviour, 13 May 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-01881-0)). The authors of the 2024 study in which inoculation helped only in combination with accuracy prompts wrote that their results "provide evidence for synergy between popular misinformation interventions" ([Pennycook et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-02023-2)).

## What defenders can do

- **Campaign designers** can follow the two-part structure, forewarning plus refutation with a harmless example, and plan boosters, since effects fade over weeks ([Practical Guide, 2022](https://prebunking.withgoogle.com/docs/A_Practical_Guide_to_Prebunking_Misinformation.pdf); [Maertens et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/xap0000315)); Jigsaw's German results suggest using concepts that translate easily ([Jigsaw, 2023](https://medium.com/jigsaw/prebunking-to-build-defenses-against-online-manipulation-tactics-in-germany-a1dbfbc67a1a)).
- **Evaluators** can measure the effect on reliable content as well as on manipulative content, with signal detection measures such as the area under the ROC curve, as Higham and colleagues recommend ([Higham et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.copsyc.2023.101785)), and publish sample sizes with campaign results.
- **Educators** can pair technique lessons with prompts to think about accuracy, the combination that improved truth discernment in 2024 ([Pennycook et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-02023-2)), and with feedback exercises ([Leder et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0001603)).
- **Readers** can learn the named techniques the studies used (emotional language, false dichotomies, scapegoating, ad hominem attacks, incoherence, fake experts, decontextualization, whataboutism) and then check claims on their merits, since in the 2024 study recognizing a technique did not by itself improve judgments of truth ([Pennycook et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-02023-2)).

## What to remember

- Prebunking forewarns people and refutes a manipulation in advance, usually with a harmless example; it rests on William McGuire's inoculation theory of the 1960s.
- A 2010 meta-analysis of 54 tests found inoculation beats supportive messages, with resistance decaying after about two weeks.
- Technique-based games (Bad News, Harmony Square, Go Viral!) and 90-second videos teach people to recognize techniques such as emotional language, false dichotomies and scapegoating.
- On YouTube in 2022, inoculation videos raised technique recognition by about 5% at about $0.05 per view, narrowly missing the preregistered threshold across all items; the study was funded by Google's Jigsaw.
- Jigsaw's campaigns reported up to 8 percentage points in Poland, Czechia and Slovakia and 5.4% in Germany, measured by single survey questions without published sample sizes.
- Critics using signal detection methods find that games shift people towards calling everything false rather than improving discrimination; a 33-study reanalysis by proponents finds improved discrimination without added bias.
- A technique video did not improve truth discernment on real headlines unless combined with an accuracy prompt.
- In Brazil, the electoral court's 2026 "V de Verdade" videos teach warning signs in advance; no evaluation was found.
