Every earlier part of this series ends in the same place: a second path, a second gateway, a second cluster member, a second region, each of them correct on paper. Paper is where most of them stay. The operators' own reports show redundancy failing less often because a design was wrong than because a design was never tried in the shape the failure took: a facility removed whole, a backbone taken down at once, a restore run for hundreds of customers instead of one. This article is about trying. It reads what the standards and regulators require, what the operators who invented failure drills, game days and chaos engineering published about how they do it, and what their post-mortems say happened when they had not. After reading, you can build a test programme for your own redundancy from a tabletop exercise up to a production failover, choose what to break, when and how far, write the stop conditions and the rollback before you start, instrument the monitoring that shows a standby is ready between tests, and find the recovery path that nobody has tried. It is part 10, the last, of the series that opens with [Redundant architectures: how networks survive failure](/learn/redundant-architectures-how-networks-survive-failure); [part 9](/learn/data-and-service-redundancy-replication-quorum-rpo-and-rto) defines the recovery objectives that the tests measure.

## Why an untested standby decays

Google's site reliability engineering (SRE) book opens its chapter on testing with an epigraph of unknown origin: "If you haven't tried it, assume it's broken" ([Google, Site Reliability Engineering, chapter 17, Testing for Reliability](https://sre.google/sre-book/testing-reliability/)). The same chapter explains why the assumption is reasonable for redundancy in particular. A configuration that exists to shorten recovery and "is only modified when there’s a failure, has a release cadence slower than the MTBF", the mean time between failures (same chapter). The code and settings of a failover path change rarely, while everything around them changes weekly; the path is exercised only when the thing it protects fails.

Amazon Web Services (AWS) makes the point from its own operations: "Our experience has shown that the only error recovery that works is the path you test frequently. This is the reason why having a small number of recovery paths is best" ([AWS, Disaster Recovery of Workloads on AWS, Testing disaster recovery](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/whitepapers/latest/disaster-recovery-workloads-on-aws/testing-disaster-recovery.html)). Its example is the secondary data store: "The capacity of the secondary, which might have been sufficient when you last tested, might no longer be able to tolerate the load under this scenario, or service quotas in the secondary Region might not be sufficient" (same page). The current framework names the drift behind it: "Over time, the primary and recovery sites diverge as changes land on the primary but not the recovery site. This drift is only exposed when a failover occurs" ([AWS Well-Architected Framework, REL13-BP04, Manage configuration drift at the DR site or Region](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/framework/rel13-bp04.html)). The Netflix engineers who named chaos engineering wrote the same about their own experiments: because the system changes continuously, "our confidence in the results of past experiments decreases over time" ([Basiri and others, Chaos Engineering, IEEE Software 33, 3, May-June 2016, author's copy on arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1702.05843)).

A rough count shows the scale. Take an illustrative pair of devices whose failover path runs only when one of them fails, about once in two years, in a network that changes weekly: about 104 changes go into production between one real exercise of the path and the next. A quarterly drill cuts that to about 13 changes, a monthly drill to about 4. The numbers are illustrative; the ratio between the change rate and the exercise rate is the quantity to estimate for any real standby.

## The ladder of tests, from a table to production

### What the standards define

The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) separates tests from exercises in its contingency planning guide. Tests "are evaluation tools that use quantifiable metrics to validate the operability of an information system or system component in an operational environment", such as "removing power from a system or system component"; exercises are simulations "designed to validate the viability of one or more aspects" of a plan ([NIST SP 800-34 Rev. 1, Contingency Planning Guide for Federal Information Systems, May 2010, sections 3.5.1 and 3.5.3](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-34r1.pdf)). It then grades the exercise to the system's impact level, as defined by the Federal Information Processing Standard (FIPS) 199 (same guide, section 3.5.4):

| System impact | Exercise NIST asks for | What it adds |
|---|---|---|
| Low | Tabletop exercise | "discussion-based only and does not involve deploying equipment or other resources" |
| Moderate | Functional exercise | Duties performed "in a simulated operational environment", including "an element of system recovery from backup media" |
| High | Full-scale functional exercise | "a system failover to the alternate location", recovery from backup and "a full recovery and reconstitution of the information system to a known state" |

The guide lists what a contingency plan test should cover: notification procedures, "System recovery on an alternate platform from backup media", internal and external connectivity, system performance on alternate equipment and "Restoration of normal operations"; it asks for training "at least annually" and for each test or exercise to end in an after-action report whose lessons update the plan (same guide, sections 3.5 to 3.5.2). A footnote names an opportunity that every network team has: "Planned and unplanned maintenance activities may also present opportunities to execute and document a Functional Exercise" (same guide, footnote 28). The [tabletop exercise](/glossary/tabletop) entry covers the first rung.

The International Organization for Standardization (ISO) makes exercising a requirement of a business continuity management system: clause 8.5 of ISO 22301:2019 is the exercise programme ([ISO 22301:2019, contents, preview published by iTeh Standards](https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/75106/93796e84e77c4ea283721dff7333671e/ISO-22301-2019.pdf)). The standard for the information and communication technology (ICT) side, ISO/IEC 27031:2025, gives the subject a whole clause, "Testing, exercise, and auditing", with subclauses on the test and exercise programme, the scope of exercises, "Alert based and different recovery stages", managing an exercise and "Learning from tests" ([ISO/IEC 27031:2025, contents, preview published by VDE Verlag](https://www.vde-verlag.de/iec-normen/preview-pdf/info_isoiec27031%7Bed2.0%7Den.pdf)).

### What the regulators require

The European Union's Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) turns the ladder into an obligation for financial entities. They must "test the ICT business continuity plans and the ICT response and recovery plans in relation to ICT systems supporting all functions at least yearly, as well as in the event of any substantive changes to ICT systems supporting critical or important functions", and, other than microenterprises, include in the test plans "scenarios of cyber-attacks and switchovers between the primary ICT infrastructure and the redundant capacity, backups and redundant facilities" (article 11(6)); "Testing of the backup procedures and restoration and recovery procedures and methods shall be undertaken periodically" (article 12(2)) ([Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, 14 December 2022, applicable from 17 January 2025, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2554)). The switchover clause is the one that matters here: a regulator asking, in law, for the failover to be tried.

Brazil's rules for financial institutions say the same in their own words. Resolution 4.557 of 2017 of the National Monetary Council (Conselho Monetário Nacional, CMN) requires "testes e revisões dos planos de continuidade de negócios com periodicidade adequada" (tests and reviews of business continuity plans at suitable intervals), and the management reports must include their results (article 20, IV and § 2) ([Banco Central do Brasil (BCB), Resolução CMN nº 4.557, consolidated version](https://normativos.bcb.gov.br/Lists/Normativos/Attachments/50344/Res_4557_v13_P.pdf)). Resolution CMN 4.893 of 2021 requires the annual cyber security report to cover "os resultados dos testes de continuidade de negócios, considerando cenários de indisponibilidade ocasionada por incidentes" (the results of business continuity tests, considering scenarios of unavailability caused by incidents), in the wording given by Resolution CMN 5.274 of 18 December 2025 (article 8, § 1, IV), and its cyber policy must set out the incident scenarios those tests consider (article 3, V, a) ([BCB, Resolução CMN nº 4.893, as amended](https://www.bcb.gov.br/api/conteudo/app/normativos/exibenormativo?p1=Resolu%C3%A7%C3%A3o%20CMN&p2=4893)).

### What the cloud providers advise

The three large providers publish the same ladder with their own emphasis:

| Provider document | Rungs named | The line that matters for redundancy |
|---|---|---|
| [Microsoft Learn, Architecture strategies for disaster recovery, updated 19 November 2025](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/well-architected/reliability/disaster-recovery) | Tabletop exercises, dry runs in non-production, production-level drills, surprise game days | "production drills are the only way to verify that your plan meets RTO and RPO targets in real conditions" |
| [AWS Well-Architected Framework, REL13-BP03, Test disaster recovery implementation](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/framework/rel13-bp03.html) | Isolated drills, failover in production on a schedule | "Recovery paths that are tested often are the only ones that work in practice" |
| [Google Cloud Well-Architected Framework, Perform testing for recovery from failures, last updated 27 October 2026](https://cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/reliability/perform-testing-for-recovery-from-failures) | Regional failovers, release rollbacks, restores from backup | Tests validate the recovery time objective (RTO) and the recovery point objective (RPO), and the ability to drain a region |

Microsoft adds the caution that belongs beside every production drill: "Performing DR drills directly in production can introduce unexpected, potentially severe failures. Test recovery procedures in non-production environments first to validate safety and uncover issues before running drills in production", and asks that the disaster recovery (DR) plan be reviewed with all teams "ideally every six months" (same page). AWS lists the anti-patterns its readers will recognize: "Teams never exercise failover in production and rely on untested assumptions", "Recovery paths multiply until none of them is tested frequently enough to be trusted", and "A secondary data store is assumed to handle full production load but is never tested under it" ([AWS, REL13-BP03](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/framework/rel13-bp03.html)). Google's framework gives the criteria for a restore test, "data integrity, recovery time objective (RTO), and recovery point objective (RPO)", and asks that a restore test cover "the entire application stack and all the critical infrastructure services with the restored data" ([Google Cloud Well-Architected Framework, Perform testing for recovery from data loss, last updated 27 October 2026](https://cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/reliability/perform-testing-for-recovery-from-data-loss)).

### The ladder, assembled

| Rung | What happens | What it proves | What it cannot prove |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tabletop | People talk through a scenario | Roles, decisions, contacts, documents | That anything technical works |
| Component test | One supply, link, member or process is removed, in a window | That the local mechanism fails over within its timers | Anything about load, dependencies or people |
| Functional exercise or non-production drill | A recovery is run in a copy of production | Procedures, restore steps, tooling, timing in the copy | Scale, real traffic and real dependencies |
| Production failover drill or game day | A zone, cluster or site is failed over for real, announced | RTO and RPO under real conditions; hidden dependencies | Unannounced conditions; combinations not scripted |
| Continuous fault injection | Automated failures run on a schedule in production | That new services keep surviving known failures | Rare, large or novel failures |
| Full facility or region removal | A whole site is taken out | The thing the redundancy was bought for | Nothing above it; it is the top rung |

The table is a synthesis of the documents above; each rung's limits are why the next one exists. The reports at the end of this article show where the top rung was skipped.

## Chaos engineering, from the people who named it

### The definition and the four steps

Netflix's Traffic and Chaos Team defined the term in 2016: "Chaos Engineering is the discipline of experimenting on a distributed system in order to build confidence in its capability to withstand turbulent conditions in production" ([Basiri and others, Chaos Engineering, IEEE Software, May-June 2016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1702.05843)). The Principles of Chaos Engineering, last updated in March 2019, set the experiment out in four steps: define "steady state" as "some measurable output of a system that indicates normal behavior"; hypothesize that it will continue in both a control group and an experimental group; "Introduce variables that reflect real world events like servers that crash, hard drives that malfunction, network connections that are severed, etc."; and "Try to disprove the hypothesis by looking for a difference in steady state between the control group and the experimental group" ([Principles of Chaos Engineering, last update March 2019](https://principlesofchaos.org/)). Five advanced principles follow: build the hypothesis around steady state, vary real-world events, run experiments in production, automate them to run continuously, and "Minimize Blast Radius", because "it is the responsibility and obligation of the Chaos Engineer to ensure the fallout from experiments are minimized and contained" (same page).

The steady state is an output, not an internal metric. Netflix's paper uses stream starts per second (SPS): "We use SPS as our primary indicator of the overall health of the system", and it designs hypotheses around such metrics because finer ones like processor load "are not a direct measure of system availability", while still watching them to stop an experiment early (Basiri and others, as above). For a network, the equivalent is a measure the users feel: completed transactions, successful logins, page loads, not interface counters.

### What Netflix broke, and when

The paper lists the inputs Netflix used: terminating virtual machine instances, injecting latency into requests between services, failing requests between services, failing an internal service, and making "an entire Amazon region unavailable" (same paper). For the last it is precise about the limit: "at Netflix we do not actually take an entire Amazon region offline, since we don't have the capability to do so. Instead, we take actions under the assumption that a region has gone offline: redirecting client requests to other Amazon regions, and we observe the effects" (same paper). The cadence is part of the design: "while we have Chaos Monkey running continuously during weekdays, we run Chaos Kong exercises at a cadence of once a month", Chaos Kong being the exercise that simulates the failure of a whole region, and "Chaos Monkey is only active during normal working hours so that engineers can respond quickly if a service fails due to an instance termination" (same paper). The paper also explains why it runs in production at all: "it is never possible to fully reproduce all aspects of the system within a test context" (same paper).

Chaos Monkey's own documentation turns the schedule into a probability. "Every weekday, for each instance group, Chaos Monkey flips a weighted coin to decide whether to terminate an instance from that group. If the coin comes up heads, Chaos Monkey schedules a termination at a random time between 9AM and 3PM that day"; the number of work days between terminations follows a geometric distribution, P(X=n) = (1−p)^(n−1) × p, with mean 1/p, so an application that asks for a mean of μ work days gets p = 1/μ ([Netflix, Chaos Monkey documentation, Termination behavior](https://netflix.github.io/chaosmonkey/Termination-behavior/)). The configuration file carries the window, `start_hour = 9` and `end_hour = 15` in the `America/Los_Angeles` time zone by default ([Chaos Monkey documentation, Configuration file format](https://netflix.github.io/chaosmonkey/Configuration-file-format/)), and an outage checker can disable terminations "during ongoing outages" ([Chaos Monkey documentation, Outage checker](https://netflix.github.io/chaosmonkey/Outage-checker/)). Worked for the documentation's own example, μ = 5:

| Work days | Chance the group has had no termination yet | Chance it has had at least one |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 80.0% | 20.0% |
| 3 | 51.2% | 48.8% |
| 4 | 41.0% | 59.0% |
| 10 | 10.7% | 89.3% |
| 20 | 1.2% | 98.8% |

Half the groups have lost an instance within four work days, and after four weeks almost all of them have; the point of the design is that no team can plan around it. The same arithmetic runs in the [Python playground](/tools/python-playground):

```python
# Chaos Monkey's schedule: each weekday a weighted coin with p = 1/mu decides a termination (geometric distribution).
mu = 5
p = 1 / mu
for days in (1, 3, 4, 10, 20):
    none_yet = (1 - p) ** days
    print(days, f"no termination yet {none_yet:.1%}", f"at least one {1 - none_yet:.1%}")
```

### What the providers say about doing it safely

AWS defines chaos engineering in its framework as injecting "controlled faults into a system to verify that it withstands and recovers from real-world disruptions", lists among the anti-patterns "Teams design for resilience but never verify how the whole workload behaves when faults occur" and "Teams ignore past post-incident analyses when choosing which faults to inject", and asks for guardrails: "Define stop conditions on the metrics that represent steady state" ([AWS Well-Architected Framework, REL12-BP04, Test resilience using chaos engineering](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/framework/rel12-bp04.html)). Microsoft ties the budget for experiments to reliability targets: "Your error budget represents the investment you can make in fault injection. Limit chaos testing to stay within your SLOs", its service level objectives, and advises starting outside production and moving there "only after you've exhausted insights from non-production environments and have safeguards in place to limit blast radius and roll back quickly" ([Microsoft Learn, Architecture strategies for designing a reliability testing strategy, updated 29 May 2026](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/well-architected/reliability/testing-strategy)). The same page warns that "Fault-injection testing in production can be disruptive and can potentially cause downtime" and asks for transparency with stakeholders about it (same page). Both providers offer a managed fault injection service, AWS Fault Injection Service and Azure Chaos Studio, the latter described as "a managed service for chaos engineering and Azure resilience testing" ([Microsoft Learn, What is Azure Chaos Studio?](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/chaos-studio/chaos-studio-overview)); the practice does not depend on either.

## Game days, DiRT and storm drills

### Game days

AWS defines a game day as "a scheduled exercise where your team responds to a simulated failure in a production-like environment, using the same people and procedures you would use for a real event" ([AWS Well-Architected Framework, REL12-BP05, Conduct game days regularly](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/framework/rel12-bp05.html)). Its anti-patterns read like a summary of the reports later in this article: "Teams document recovery procedures but never exercise them", "Exercises exclude business decision-makers and involve only technical staff", "A game day runs without informing all relevant stakeholders in advance", and "Failures during the exercise lead to blaming teams rather than improving the system" (same page). It argues for including decision-makers because a game day with engineers only "tests the technology but misses the coordination, decision-making, and communication that are required in real incidents", and it names the most valuable output: game days "can discover previously-unknown dependencies between systems, services, and teams" (same page).

### Google's DiRT

Google calls its version Disaster Recovery Testing. The SRE book describes "Our annual Disaster and Recovery Testing (DiRT) drills", in which "SREs push production systems to the limit and inflict actual outages" to check that systems react as expected, to find unexpected weaknesses and to find ways of making the systems more robust ([Google SRE book, chapter 33, Lessons Learned from Other Industries](https://sre.google/sre-book/lessons-learned/)). The same chapter compares the practice with other industries: aviation, which cannot test live in production without putting equipment and passengers at risk and uses simulators instead; telecoms, which drill for hurricanes; and the United States nuclear Navy, whose live drills involve "actually breaking real stuff but with control parameters" and are "carried out religiously, every week, two to three days per week" (same chapter). Kripa Krishnan's account of the programme in the Association for Computing Machinery's ACM Queue (2012) describes it as an "annual, company-wide, multi-day Disaster Recovery Testing event" ([Krishnan, Weathering the Unexpected, ACM Queue 10, 9, 2012](https://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=2371516)).

### Meta's storm drills, and the one it had not run

Meta's own account of its 4 October 2021 outage credits drills for the recovery and names the drill it had not run. "Helpfully, this is an event we’re well prepared for thanks to the “storm” drills we’ve been running for a long time now. In a storm exercise, we simulate a major system failure by taking a service, data center, or entire region offline, stress testing all the infrastructure and software involved" ([Meta, Santosh Janardhan, More details about the October 4 outage, 5 October 2021](https://engineering.fb.com/2021/10/05/networking-traffic/outage-details/)). The drills helped bring load back without further failures, but "while we’ve never previously run a storm that simulated our global backbone being taken offline, we’ll certainly be looking for ways to simulate events like this moving forward" (same report). The hub's [outage table](/learn/redundant-architectures-how-networks-survive-failure) summarizes the outage itself.

### When the test is the incident

Breaking production on purpose can break more than intended, and Google's SRE book publishes an example. To flush out hidden dependencies on a test database, SREs planned "to block all access to just one database out of a hundred"; within minutes, "numerous dependent services reported that both external and internal users were unable to access key systems" ([Google SRE book, chapter 14, Emergency Response](https://sre.google/sre-book/emergency-response/)). The team aborted the test, failed to roll back the permissions change, and restored access with "an already tested approach" within an hour (same chapter). Its findings name the gap: "Because we hadn’t tested our rollback procedures in a test environment, these procedures were flawed, which lengthened the outage. We now require thorough testing of rollback procedures before such large-scale tests" (same chapter). A test plan without a tested way back is a change without a back-out, which is the subject of the next section.

## Change windows, and when to break things

Two logics of timing pull in opposite directions. Changes go into quiet hours to limit who is affected: Microsoft's worked example of a reliability target schedules maintenance in the roughly four hours a day when the example's interface "is mostly unused" ([Microsoft Learn, Architecture strategies for defining reliability targets, updated 20 August 2024](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/well-architected/reliability/metrics)). Chaos experiments go into working hours so that people are awake to respond, which is why Chaos Monkey's default window runs from 9:00 to 15:00 on weekdays (above). A drill of a redundancy mechanism sits between the two, and the documents above give it four rules:

- **Announce it.** AWS treats an unannounced game day as an anti-pattern and asks to "inform everyone of the date, time, and scenarios in advance" ([AWS, REL12-BP05](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/framework/rel12-bp05.html)); Microsoft adds surprise game days only once scheduled drills are routine ([Microsoft Learn, disaster recovery](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/well-architected/reliability/disaster-recovery)).
- **Use the windows that exist.** NIST's footnote counts planned and unplanned maintenance as opportunities for a functional exercise ([NIST SP 800-34 Rev. 1, footnote 28](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-34r1.pdf)); a firmware upgrade of one member of a pair is a failover drill whether or not it is called one.
- **Stop when something real is happening.** Chaos Monkey's outage checker exists for that ([Chaos Monkey documentation](https://netflix.github.io/chaosmonkey/Outage-checker/)), and Microsoft asks testers to "stay flexible about when to run tests or avoid them during sensitive periods" ([Microsoft Learn, reliability testing](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/well-architected/reliability/testing-strategy)).
- **Test the way back first.** The SRE example above is the reason.

The way back has a budget. A drill that fails over a pair, watches it, and fails back must fit the window with time to spare. In an illustrative 60-minute window, a 10-minute failover and 20 minutes of observation leave 30 minutes; a 15-minute failback and 10 minutes of verification leave a margin of 5. The [change window runbook builder](/tools/change-window-runbook-builder) does this arithmetic and assembles the runbook around it, the [health snapshot comparator](/tools/health-snapshot-comparator) structures the before-and-after comparison that decides whether the drill passed, and [Change windows that do not become incidents](/learn/change-windows-that-do-not-become-incidents) covers the rest of the window's discipline. GitHub drew the same rule from its outage of 22 December 2012, told in [part 4](/learn/layer-2-redundancy-lag-mlag-and-spanning-tree): "From now on, we will place our fileservers high availability software into maintenance mode before we perform any network changes, no matter how minor, at the switching level. This allows the servers to continue functioning but will not take any automated failover actions"; it also postponed upgrades to its aggregation network "until we have a functional duplicate of our production environment in staging to test against" ([GitHub, Downtime last Saturday, 26 December 2012](https://github.blog/2012-12-26-downtime-last-saturday)).

## Monitoring that proves the standby works

### The problem: redundancy hides failures

A redundant system that loses one member keeps working, which is the point, and which also means nobody notices. Google's SRE book draws the line between two kinds of monitoring: black-box monitoring is "symptom-oriented" and reports what is failing now, "The system isn’t working correctly, right now", while white-box monitoring inspects the system's internals and "therefore allows detection of imminent problems, failures masked by retries, and so forth" ([Google SRE book, chapter 6, Monitoring Distributed Systems](https://sre.google/sre-book/monitoring-distributed-systems/)). A dead standby is a failure masked by redundancy; only white-box signals show it before the day it is needed.

| Links and paths | Bidirectional Forwarding Detection (BFD) sessions up on the backup path; link aggregation members and partners in sync | [BFD: when a link is up and dead](/learn/bfd-when-a-link-is-up-and-dead); [part 4](/learn/layer-2-redundancy-lag-mlag-and-spanning-tree); [part 6](/learn/layer-3-redundancy-ecmp-bfd-and-fast-reroute) |

| Database replication | `write_lag`, `flush_lag` and `replay_lag` within the RPO | [PostgreSQL 18, pg_stat_replication](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/monitoring-stats.html) |
| Replicated logs | Under-replicated and under-minimum-ISR partitions at zero (ISR: in-sync replicas) | [Apache Kafka 4.2, Monitoring](https://kafka.apache.org/42/operations/monitoring/) |
| Consensus | Health endpoint answering; no leader heartbeat warnings; quorum health per region | [etcd v3.5, Monitoring](https://etcd.io/docs/v3.5/op-guide/monitoring/); [Spanner, dual-region quorum health timeline](https://cloud.google.com/spanner/docs/instance-configurations) |
| Backups | Last successful backup newer than the RPO; last timed restore within its target | [Google Cloud Well-Architected Framework, data loss testing](https://cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/reliability/perform-testing-for-recovery-from-data-loss) |
| Recovery site | No configuration drift; capacity and quotas sufficient for full load | [AWS, REL13-BP04](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/framework/rel13-bp04.html) |

Two of these come with explicit advice from the providers. Google's framework asks to "Set a clear RPO based on your business needs, and monitor adherence to the RPO. If backup intervals exceed the defined RPO, use Cloud Monitoring to set up alerts" ([Google Cloud, data loss testing](https://cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/reliability/perform-testing-for-recovery-from-data-loss)). Spanner's documentation says: "For a faster recovery time objective (RTO), monitor or set up an alert on the Dual-region quorum health timeline metric" ([Google Cloud, Spanner instance configurations, last updated 12 November 2026](https://cloud.google.com/spanner/docs/instance-configurations)). AWS asks that drift detection run continuously, listing as an anti-pattern "No drift detection runs, so the recovery site appears ready when it is not" ([AWS, REL13-BP04](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/framework/rel13-bp04.html)).

### Monitoring that shares the failure

The operators' reports add a second rule: the monitoring must not depend on what it monitors. Roblox found during its 73-hour outage of 2021 that "Critical monitoring systems that would have provided better visibility into the cause of the outage relied on affected systems, such as Consul", and afterwards wrote: "Our telemetry systems no longer depend on the systems that they are configured to monitor" ([Roblox, Roblox Return to Service 10/28-10/31 2021, 20 January 2022](https://blog.roblox.com/2022/01/roblox-return-to-service-10-28-10-31-2021/)). GitLab's backups had failed for an unknown time before its 2017 data loss because the failure notices travelled by email that the receiving server rejected: "This means we were never aware of the backups failing, until it was too late" ([GitLab, Postmortem of database outage of January 31, 10 February 2017](https://about.gitlab.com/blog/postmortem-of-database-outage-of-january-31/)). Atlassian's site deletions of 2022 went unseen by its own systems: "Our internal monitoring did not detect an issue because the sites were deleted via a standard workflow" ([Atlassian, Post-Incident Review on the Atlassian April 2022 outage, 29 April 2022](https://www.atlassian.com/engineering/post-incident-review-april-2022-outage)). Cloudflare learned its data centre had switched to generators only afterwards: "None of our observability tools were able to detect that the source of power had changed" ([Cloudflare, Matthew Prince, Post mortem on the Cloudflare Control Plane and Analytics Outage, 4 November 2023](https://blog.cloudflare.com/post-mortem-on-cloudflare-control-plane-and-analytics-outage/)). [Part 3](/learn/physical-and-power-redundancy-feeds-paths-and-carriers) tells the older version of the same story, a monitoring link whose battery lasted thirty minutes. [Part 9](/learn/data-and-service-redundancy-replication-quorum-rpo-and-rto) has the full accounts of the GitLab, Roblox and Atlassian incidents.

## Post-mortems where the redundancy itself failed

Each case below rests on the operator's own report; the lesson given is the one the report draws or the remedy it commits to.

### Cloudflare, 2 to 4 November 2023: the facility never removed whole

Cloudflare's control plane and analytics ran in three data centres around Hillsboro, Oregon, "independent of one another", close enough to "run active-active redundant data clusters", so that "if any of the facilities goes offline then the remaining ones are able to continue to operate" ([Cloudflare, Post mortem on the Cloudflare Control Plane and Analytics Outage, 4 November 2023](https://blog.cloudflare.com/post-mortem-on-cloudflare-control-plane-and-analytics-outage/)). Two exceptions were known: some newer products had not been moved onto the high availability cluster, and logging had been left off it on purpose (same report). On 2 November the largest of the three, which Cloudflare calls PDX-04, lost its utility feeds and its generators together; in Cloudflare's account of the facility's side, part of which it labels "informed speculation", the batteries "started to fail after only 4 minutes", and Cloudflare's routers there went offline at 11:44 UTC (same report). [Part 3](/learn/physical-and-power-redundancy-feeds-paths-and-carriers) covers why power chains fail at the handover; the redundancy question here is what happened to the cluster.

Most of it worked. What did not was the part nobody had tried: "a subset of services that were supposed to be on the high availability cluster had dependencies on services exclusively running in PDX-04", among them the Kafka and ClickHouse systems behind logs and analytics (same report). The report says why it was not found earlier: "We had performed testing of our high availability cluster by taking each (and both) of the other two data center facilities entirely offline. And we had also tested taking the high availability portion of PDX-04 offline. However, we had never tested fully taking the entire PDX-04 facility offline" (same report). Cloudflare decided at 13:40 to fail over to its disaster recovery sites in Europe, brought the first services up at 13:43, hit a thundering herd of retried requests, and had them stable by 17:57; some newer products "where we had not fully implemented and tested a disaster recovery procedure" did not come up there at all, and full restoration came at 04:25 UTC on 4 November (same report). Computed from the report's times, the failover decision came 1 hour 56 minutes after power was lost, the recovery site was stable 6 hours 13 minutes after it, and the incident lasted 40 hours 41 minutes.

Among the remedies: "Require all products and features that are designated Generally Available have a reliable disaster recovery plan that is tested", "Test the blast radius of system failures and minimize the number of services that are impacted by a failure", and "Implement more rigorous chaos testing of all data center functions including the full removal of each of our core data center facilities" (same report). The facilities had been removed from service one or two at a time, and the third only in part; the failure came as the one combination not tried.

### Meta, 4 October 2021: the drill catalogue with one gap

Meta had run storm drills for years, taking "a service, data center, or entire region offline", and the outage that came was the one shape missing from the catalogue, the whole backbone at once (above). The hub's table and [BGP and the routing chokepoint](/learn/bgp-and-the-routing-chokepoint) cover the mechanism; the report's own last line is the testing lesson: "From here on out, our job is to strengthen our testing, drills, and overall resilience to make sure events like this happen as rarely as possible" ([Meta, 5 October 2021](https://engineering.fb.com/2021/10/05/networking-traffic/outage-details/)).

### Google Cloud, europe-west9, April 2023: placement nobody verified

Three buildings with independent power, cooling and networking were defeated by two of three database replicas sitting in one of them ([part 9](/learn/data-and-service-redundancy-replication-quorum-rpo-and-rto) works the quorum arithmetic). The testing lesson is in Google's remediation: "Enhanced Zonal & Regional Failure Testing", including "validating that critical services consistently meet our expectations for how they allocate resources within our physical failure domains, that automated verification tests are in place to ensure no regressions, and ensure recovery times are efficient", and an improved "testing process of removing and restoring a GCE control plane for a zonal and regional issue", GCE being Google Compute Engine ([Google Cloud Service Health, europe-west9 incident report, 2023](https://status.cloud.google.com/incidents/dS9ps52MUnxQfyDGPfkY)). A placement rule that is not checked continuously drifts like any configuration.

### GitHub, October 2018: a restore tested daily, never at full size

GitHub's backups were restored every day as a test, "so the recovery time frame was well understood, however until this incident we have never needed to fully rebuild an entire cluster from backup" ([GitHub, October 21 post-incident analysis, 30 October 2018](https://github.blog/news-insights/company-news/oct21-post-incident-analysis/)). The catch-up after restore then followed "a power decay function instead of a linear trajectory", so every published estimate was too short (same report). Its organizational conclusion was explicit: "We will take a more proactive stance in testing our assumptions", and "we will also begin a systemic practice of validating failure scenarios before they have a chance to affect you. This work will involve future investment in fault injection and chaos engineering tooling at GitHub" (same report).

### Atlassian, April 2022: restores practised for one site

Atlassian's restores had been prepared and tested for "infrastructure, data corruption, single service events, or single-site deletions", and "The site-level deletion did not have runbooks that could be quickly automated for the scale of this event" ([Atlassian, Post-Incident Review, 29 April 2022](https://www.atlassian.com/engineering/post-incident-review-april-2022-outage)). Its commitment is a test at the scale of the failure: "We will regularly run DR exercises that involve restoring all products for a large set of sites", tests that "will verify that runbooks are up to date as our architecture evolves" (same report).

### GitLab, January 2017: nobody owned the test

GitLab's five-whys ends with a question that every backup programme should ask of itself: "Why was the backup procedure not tested on a regular basis? - Because there was no ownership, as a result nobody was responsible for testing this procedure" ([GitLab, 10 February 2017](https://about.gitlab.com/blog/postmortem-of-database-outage-of-january-31/)). One of the remedies it listed was "Automated testing of recovering PostgreSQL database backups" (same report).

### Amazon S3, February 2017: a restart not done for years

Amazon's summary of the Simple Storage Service (S3) outage, also a row of the hub's table, says that "we have not completely restarted the index subsystem or the placement subsystem in our larger regions for many years", and that the restart and its safety checks "took longer than expected"; AWS changed its capacity tool and committed to "improve the recovery time of key S3 subsystems" ([AWS, Summary of the Amazon S3 Service Disruption in the Northern Virginia (US-EAST-1) Region, 2017](https://aws.amazon.com/message/41926/)). A cold start is a recovery path like any other.

### The seven reports at a glance

| Operator | What had been tested | What had not | What the operator committed to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cloudflare, 2023 | Removing each of the other two facilities; the HA portion of the third | Removing the third facility whole; DR for newer products | Tested DR plans for every product; chaos testing including full facility removal |
| Meta, 2021 | Storm drills: a service, a data centre, a region offline | The global backbone offline | Ways to simulate such events; stronger testing and drills |
| Google Cloud, 2023 | Zonal behaviour of services | Continuous verification of replica placement across buildings | Automated placement verification; zonal and regional failure testing |
| GitHub, 2018 | Daily restore of backups | A full cluster rebuild; cross-region promotion against the application | Fault injection and chaos engineering; testing assumptions |
| Atlassian, 2022 | Restores of infrastructure, single services, single sites | Restoring hundreds of sites at once | Regular DR exercises restoring a large set of sites |
| GitLab, 2017 | Not stated; no owner for backup tests | Restoring from the backups | Automated testing of backup restores |
| AWS S3, 2017 | Not stated in the summary | A full restart of large-region subsystems in years | Faster recovery of the subsystems; capacity safeguards |

Read down the third column: in most of the reports the missing test was the large one, the one that is expensive, risky and disruptive to run, and it is the test that the redundancy was bought to pass.

## A test programme, step by step

The documents above agree on the steps; this list puts them in order, with the source of each.

1. **List the recovery paths, and keep the list short.** Each path is something that must be tested; AWS asks to "keep the number of distinct recovery paths small so each one is tested often" ([AWS, REL13-BP03](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/framework/rel13-bp03.html)).
2. **Give each path an owner.** GitLab's report shows what happens without one ([GitLab, 2017](https://about.gitlab.com/blog/postmortem-of-database-outage-of-january-31/)).
3. **Write the steady state and the hypothesis.** A user-facing output, measured in both groups, as the Principles of Chaos Engineering define it ([principlesofchaos.org](https://principlesofchaos.org/)); for a recovery test, the RTO, RPO and data integrity as Google's framework names them.
4. **Set the blast radius, the stop conditions and the rollback, and test the rollback.** AWS's stop conditions, Microsoft's safeguards and the SRE book's tested rollback ([AWS, REL12-BP04](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/framework/rel12-bp04.html); [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/well-architected/reliability/testing-strategy); [SRE book, chapter 14](https://sre.google/sre-book/emergency-response/)).
5. **Climb the ladder.** Tabletop, component, non-production, production, whole site; NIST's grading by impact sets a floor ([NIST SP 800-34 Rev. 1, section 3.5.4](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/SP/nistspecialpublication800-34r1.pdf)).
6. **Announce, schedule and staff it.** Business decision-makers included, stakeholders informed ([AWS, REL12-BP05](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/framework/rel12-bp05.html)).
7. **Measure what was achieved, not what was planned.** Time each step; the [incident timeline builder](/tools/incident-timeline-rca-builder) structures the timeline as for a real incident.
8. **Write it up blamelessly and change something.** NIST's after-action report and AWS's blameless retrospective; [What belongs in a post-incident technical review](/learn/what-belongs-in-a-post-incident-technical-review) applies to drills as much as to outages.
9. **Repeat on a cadence and after substantive changes.** DORA's "at least yearly" and "in the event of any substantive changes" are a legal floor for the financial sector ([Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, article 11(6)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2554)); Netflix's weekday Chaos Monkey and monthly Chaos Kong show what continuous looks like ([Basiri and others, 2016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1702.05843)).
10. **Automate the experiments that pass.** AWS: "Successful experiments become automated regression tests that guard resilience over time" ([AWS, REL12-BP04](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/framework/rel12-bp04.html)).

The [fault hypothesis builder](/tools/fault-hypothesis-builder) helps phrase what a drill is meant to disprove, the [change blast-radius mapper](/tools/change-blast-radius-mapper) maps what it can reach, and the [FortiGate HA failover simulator](/tools/fortigate-ha-failover-simulator) lets a team rehearse a cluster election before it pulls a cable.

## What to remember

- A failover path changes slowly and runs only on failure, so it drifts out of step with everything around it; the only recovery that works is the one tested often, and the shorter the list of recovery paths, the more often each is tested.
- The ladder runs from tabletop to component test, non-production drill, production failover, continuous fault injection and whole-site removal; each rung proves what the one below cannot, and NIST, ISO, DORA and the Banco Central's resolutions all require some of it.
- Chaos engineering is an experiment: a user-facing steady state, a hypothesis, real-world faults, a control group, a minimized blast radius; Netflix ran Chaos Monkey on weekdays in working hours and Chaos Kong monthly.
- A test needs stop conditions and a rollback that has itself been tested; Google's own test of blocking one database in a hundred became an hour-long outage because the rollback had not been.
- Redundancy masks failures, so the standby needs white-box monitoring of its own readiness, and the monitoring must not depend on what it monitors.
- In seven operators' reports, the test that was missing was always the large one: a whole facility, a whole backbone, a full restore, hundreds of sites.

<ReaderQuestions>

- Which of your recovery paths has never been exercised as a whole, and what would it take, in people, hours and risk, to exercise it once?
- Netflix runs its experiments in working hours so that people can respond; most network changes run at night so that fewer users are affected. Where should a failover drill of your core sit, and who decides?
- Google's test of one database in a hundred caused a wider outage than the failure it simulated. How do you decide that a drill is safe enough to run in production, and who has the authority to stop it?
- If your monitoring system depends on the network, the identity provider or the cluster it watches, what will tell you that the standby has failed on the day the primary does?
- DORA requires yearly tests including switchovers to redundant capacity, and the Banco Central requires continuity tests at suitable intervals. Would a test frequency written in regulation improve your redundancy, or only your reporting about it?
